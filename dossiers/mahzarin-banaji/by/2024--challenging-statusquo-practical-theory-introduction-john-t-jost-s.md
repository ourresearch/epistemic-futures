The young student I encountered 30 years ago is today among psychology's most prolific contributors and among the most influential and admired voices of his generation.

The author or co-editor of six books and more than 250 papers with a citation count of nearly 74,000, his five papers that spell out both theories alone have been cited over 21,000 times.

My focus here is on John's contributions to theory because the occasion of the Lewin Award directs my attention to John's fulfilment of the famous Lewinian adage about praxis, the practical element of theory.

In the term paper John submitted in the early 1990s, I was struck by the central thesis that he had isolated and analyzed under the modest title "Stereotypes and Social Justification".

It was nothing short of a challenge to one of psychology's sacred cows -ingroup favoritism -a concept so well-established that it would be wise even for a gray-haired expert to stay away from challenging it, let alone a first-year graduate student.

But clarity and boldness of thought have marked John's work from the day he committed himself to becoming a scholar-scientist.

Evidence of ingroup favoritism, displayed in thoughts, feelings, and morality had emerged early in social psychology's history.

How could it not, given the prominent rainclouds of two world wars centered in Europe, under which the scientific field had been born.

How could intergroup conflict not have loomed large in the mind of any social scientist contemplating Europe, arguably the most violent continent on the planet (outside the past 75 years), and America, the country to which millions of Europeans came to escape repression and even genocide, only to encounter new knowledge of European genocide right here in the new country.

Favoring one's group as an extension of favoring oneself was an intuitive idea that became, for good reason, a fundamental assumption supported by evolutionary and sociocultural accounts of individual-group relationships.

The concept of ingroup preference and ingroup favoring actions produced mountains of evidence, making ingroup favoritism a ubiquitous feature of human social psychology.

It achieved textbook status, and every college student learned about the ways in which humans are selfand group-protective, even when group lines are minimal and even when group protection is detrimental to the self.

The evidence all pointed in that one direction.

John did not dispute these accounts.

But at the center of his term paper was the possibility that the opposite of this great truth may also be true: that under particular conditions, humans may not show this seemingly ubiquitous, in-group favoring pattern; that given the power of hierarchy and status, low status group members will themselves favor outgroups that sit on the top in society.

I hope that the course on social cognition that John took powered his thinking to be able to assert that the mechanisms by which this bizarre, self-defeating outcome was achieved were ordinary cognitive-affective processes of attentional and perceptual bias, memory distortions, and lapses in judgment that emerge from a lack of awareness and that these combine to allow rationalization of existing arrangements -social, economic, and political -even at the expense of individual and collective self-interest.

But as I recall, John went even further in that term paper.

Stating it more clearly than I had previously encountered, John implied that members of groups that sit at the bottom of society want to believe that the existing social order is legitimate and therefore that it should remain as is, even if it means also believing that their lower status is deserved.

If this account is true, then the honest appraisal would be that the probability of changing the status quo was even more diminished than previously assumed.

The core ideas John wrote about in that term paper were serious and important, the argument was lucid and forthright, and I found most admirable the manner in which he engaged with evidence that was opposed to the one he was advocating.

In reading the paper he has now written as his offering in response to the Lewin Award, I see the same seriousness and conceptual deftness, the same forthrightness, and the same desire to understand and not merely to score points.

INTRODUCTION

To appropriately encourage a vigorous young scholar who had placed in my mailbox an unsettling idea, I suggested that John reach beyond existing evidence from social psychology (an easy task for him given his already decent grasp over theories of mind and society) and to spell out the experiments that would be needed to generate compelling evidence (a much harder task).

The latter piece was crucial, and in my own mind, there was little chance that the theory would hold up to even the first experimental tests.

But as we talked about the ideas in the weeks that followed, John continued to generate good arguments for why a system-justifying tendency may not only exist, but that under certain conditions it might beat out the ubiquitous tendencies of self-and group-enhancement.

Direct evidence for this idea was largely lacking, but as John explained it, that was because the concept of system-justification had simply not been subjected to empirical scrutiny.

It was itself a sacred cow.

Our discussions had brought us to a point where, at John's request, I collaborated with him on that first paper on a theory of system justification.

A special issue of a journal was being mounted; we decided it would be a good idea to try to make that deadline.

Yet, to be honest, even after the paper was accepted, I assumed that the idea would largely remain an interesting argument to be ping-ponged in graduate seminars.

I had no sense of what was to come; that John would unleash an experimental juggernaut designed to test the theory from every empirical angle with full opportunities to prove it wrong.

He would expand his expertise and commitment to new areas and new methods to seek ways to keep on testing it over the next three decades and produce overwhelming evidence that would come to define a generation of social psychological thinking about a puzzling and paradoxical tendency in humans.

Soon, the scope of system-justification theory was blown open to incorporate related concepts of justice and fairness, the role of ideology, legitimacy and deservingness, and even strengthen a core aspect of work on implicit social cognition.

Enshrined in the theory is the idea that was present in that original term paper that humans are motivated to protect and defend even those aspects of existing social, economic, and political systems that disadvantage them and their groups.

So was the idea that John emphasized from the start, that social systems can serve the seductive and sedative function of keeping an internal peace by avoiding threats to beliefs, relationships, and existence itself.

So, you can imagine my pleasure at being able to write this introduction upon John Jost receiving the Kurt Lewin Award from the Society for the Psychological Study of Social Issues.

As I mentioned, a single theoretical contribution on the scale of system justification theory is sufficient to guarantee its progenitor a place in the history books.

But my engagement with John's early contribution should not preclude mention of a second theoretical contribution that he has offered, and in the process ruffled many a feather of many a son of the status-quo.

This contribution required John to intellectually engage with political ideology, and he did so again, quite early in his career, when he arrived at the lab of Arie Kruglanski as a postdoc.

Kruglanski posed a simple question: is psychological conservatism (i.e., a comfort with information that is familiar and accessible, a need for cognitive closure) associated with political conservatism (i.e., the tendency to maintain the social status quo)?

With other collaborators joining, they published a theoretical account derived from a solid meta-analysis of 88 studies from 12 countries showing that political conservatism is indeed associated with cognitive conservatism, specifically, with a need for order and closure, and a lower tolerance of ambiguity.

Additionally, greater perceptions of danger and threat were associated with political conservatism.

The article ignited intellectual debates that continue to this day.

But to my knowledge nobody has shown that John is wrong in his fundamental conclusion based on historical and experimental analysis that resisting social change and hierarchy attenuation are obvious correlates of conservatism.

Although one can say that John is merely summarizing a version of a dictionary definition of what it means to be conservative (to favor tradition and the past and associated status quo over progress and change), the reactions he has received would lead one to think that he is proposing a new argument for baby-killing.

As is often the case when reactionary responses to new ideas emerge, the disparity between the evidence John marshals and the opinions of his detractors is itself evidence for the importance of his work and reminds us yet again that John isn't playing with small ideas -he is, as he has always been, in the business of challenging sacred cows.

As with system justification theory, dozens of labs around the world have engaged with the ideas on ideology so well laid out by John, that hypothesis testing is crystal clear.

The combined work shows that the two core ideas of resistance to change and equality have a genetic basis; that childhood temperament predicts adult political orientation, that social attachment styles predict ideology, that threat of a variety of sorts, measured behaviorally and neuronally, is linked to ideology.

Additionally, the theory has been applied to core topics, such as domestic and foreign policy decision-making on the part of elites and voting behavior on the part of ordinary citizens.

All in all, John has breathed new life into the scientific study of political ideology.

A small contribution that I can offer to bolster John's core idea in the paper you will read in this issue is research led by Tessa Charlesworth on change in implicit attitudes and stereotypes
(Charlesworth &amp; Banaji, 2021)
 and the research led by Kirsten Morehouse on implicit dehumanization
(Morehouse et al., 2023)
.

Although I was certain that implicit attitudes would remain stable over time and change only in response to major social upheavals or over long periods of time, Charlesworth showed that counter to my expectation, anti-gay bias has decreased by 64% and anti-Black and anti-dark skin bias has decreased by 25% over a relatively short 14-year period.

Explicit bias has also dropped off and on both measures, implicit and explicit, and we see that change has taken place in nearly all demographic groups.

But two groups consistently show faster change towards neutrality than others -younger individuals and left-liberal leaning individuals.

One can ignore these data if one wishes to cling to the idea that the right and left are equally prejudiced (in opposition to what the self-reported data show) and equally biased (in opposition to the data from a measure of implicit bias), but that would be a delusion.

In another line of research, we see confirmation for John's view again.

Morehouse et al. (2023)
 showed that the stronger association of "human" to one's own group is a feature of dominant groups (White Americans) but not non-dominant groups (Asian Americans, Latinx Americans, Black Americans), a result that is consistent with system justification theory.

But the data also showed, replicating the Charlesworth result and speaking to the question of ideology, that this automatic association is more prevalent among the elderly as well as among those who selfidentify as conservative relative to liberal.

In understanding both results, remember, we the experimenters are not ascribing the label of liberal or conservative to participants through shrouded or sneaky tests of political ideology.

We are simply posing the most direct question that is possible to pose about where a person stands on the ideology continuum that participants can answer in any way they wish.

Then, using their own self-identification we see -across millions of participants in the first study and in thousands of participants in the second -that to be conservative is to be more explicitly prejudiced and more implicitly biased.

These data are, of course, behavioral data obtained on a task that has little ecological validity.

To go to the other side of fullest ecological validity, we can look at the recently published INTRODUCTION Anti-defamation League's (ADL) Center on Extremism 2023 report on individuals affiliated with "extreme causes and movements" who have killed people in the service of their ideology.

The report finds that "All the extremist-related murders in 2023 were committed by right-wing extremists of various kinds . . .

This is the second year in a row that right-wing extremists have been connected to all identified extremist-related killings."

John's critics need to answer how we should explain these results while holding on to both-sideology.

And the rest of us need to be curious about the motivation for both-sideology in the face of evidence that the most heinous of ideology-based crimes seem to be 100% the actions of one side of the spectrum.

The paper by John that follows is a tribute to Kurt Lewin and it poses an important challenge to a sacred cow that the two sides of the ideological continuum are fundamentally "just the same"
(Jost, 2024)
.

Liberals and conservatives are, of course, as humans with commitments to particular views and values, similar in several ways; but there are vast differences as well, and to ignore them or deny them is to put off an understanding of how and why we find ourselves in the shock of the present political moment, a moment in which we are asking ourselves how it is even possible to have gotten to this place.

To quote John himself, "I aim to show that both-sideology-however innocent, well-meaning, or broad-minded it might seem-is scientifically untenable.

As Lewin understood well, we cannot build a lasting democracy or a credible social science on the basis of convenient falsehoods."

It is aesthetically pleasing in science when one sees certain recursive effects -John's theories about resistance to change away from the status quo is itself pushing against the status quo.

John Jost is in a league by himself in the influence he has had on our field.

He has achieved this status by being an integrator of ideas that include wide swaths of thinking that range from philosophy to biology but are rooted in the best and most historically accurate social science; he has done this work by engaging with scholars who hold different world views as long as there is a commitment to intellectual and experimental honesty; he once led a group of his peers in writing a treatise to explain what every leader should know about bias and this paper
(Jost et al., 2009b)
 has shaped the conversations people still have about bias detected in the laboratory and its implications for the workplace, be it the courtroom or the classroom.

John is a public intellectual.

He speaks with authority and deep knowledge about the conditions in our world that not only should be improved but that can be improved.

He is a brilliant speaker, a devoted teacher, and somebody who builds esteem in others and drives them to greater heights.

To conclude my celebration of the recipient of the 2023 SPSSI Kurt Lewin Award, let me simply acknowledge that it would be difficult to find a more worthy recipient of this award or a better scientist to help commemorate the legacy of Kurt Lewin.

Like Lewin, John forces us to contend with difficult aspects of ourselves, and he moves us by the power of his ideas and his evidence to set higher standards by which to judge ourselves as we wrestle to find scientific solutions to the problems we confront as a species, as a planet, and as we engage in the creation of a more praiseworthy world.
