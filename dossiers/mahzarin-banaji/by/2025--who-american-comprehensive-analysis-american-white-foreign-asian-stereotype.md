# Who is American? A comprehensive analysis of the American = White/Foreign = Asian stereotype (2007–2023)

                OPEN            Who is American? A comprehensive
                                analysis of the American = White/
                                Foreign = Asian stereotype (2007–
                                2023)
                                Kirsten N. Morehouse          1, Tessa E. S. Charlesworth          2, Thierry Devos       3&

                                Mahzarin R. Banaji 1
                                Against the backdrop of increasing ethnic diversity in the U.S., we replicate, extend, and challenge
                                previous examinations of the American = White/Foreign = Asian stereotype in the largest sample
                                to date (N = 666,623 respondents) over 17 years (2007–2023). Six key findings emerged. First, a
                                robust American = White association emerged on implicit (Cohen’s d = 0.50) and explicit (Cohen’s
                                d = 0.51) measures. Second, the strength of this effect varied by respondents’ race/ethnicity with
                                implicit stereotypes strongest among White respondents (Cohen’s d = 0.86) and absent among East
                                Asian respondents (Cohen’s d = 0.02). Third, the strength of implicit stereotypes was modulated
                                by age, religion, and ideology—older, Christian, and conservative respondents displayed stronger
                                implicit American = White associations—but not gender or education. Fourth, respondents living
                                in U.S. metropolitan areas with greater Asian representation or a history of voting for Democratic
                                candidates exhibited weaker implicit American = White associations. Fifth, over the past 17 years,
                                implicit and explicit American = White associations decreased by 41% and 47%, respectively, and
                                14/14 demographic subgroups changed towards neutrality. Finally, we observed suggestive evidence
                                that implicit stereotype trends towards neutrality were temporarily disrupted during the COVID-19
                                pandemic for White Americans but not Asian Americans.

                                Keywords Ethnic-national stereotypes, Implicit/explicit stereotype change, Implicit Association Test,
                                American = White association

                                In 1999, Wen Ho Lee, an American nuclear scientist working at the Los Alamos National Laboratory in New
                                Mexico, was accused of transferring American nuclear secrets to the Chinese government. Although indicted on
                                59 charges by a federal grand jury, a separate federal investigation failed to find support for the espionage charges.
                                Ultimately, Lee received $1.6 million in damages from the government and media outlets, and an apology from
                                the judge who had denied him bail.
                                    The incident led Devos and Banaji1 to ask whether this case reflected a broader tendency to associate
                                ‘American’ with some ethnic-national groups (e.g., European American) more than others (e.g., Asian American).
                                Indeed, in convenience samples of college students, they discovered that European Americans were more
                                strongly associated with the concept ‘American’ than Asian Americans on the Implicit Association Test (IAT;2).
                                The existence of this American = European American/Foreign = Asian American stereotype (hereafter referred
                                to as the American = White effect) was striking, given that previous research primarily documented implicit
                                associations that were inherently subjective, such as implicit attitudes or preferences. Unlike a preference with
                                no truth value (e.g., a preference for mountains over beaches), American–Foreign stereotypes can be evaluated
                                against a factual basis: European Americans and Asian Americans are both members of the superordinate
                                category “American.” Thus, while social and cultural features can intervene to shape which American groups
                                are perceived as more American3,4, such biases contradict the fundamental principle that all citizens are equal
                                citizens.
                                    In a series of follow-up studies, Devos and colleagues used the IAT to investigate the nature of the
                                American = White effect (for explorations using other measures, see4–7). This work led to three main findings.

                                First, they showed that the American = White effect was not restricted to White Americans respondents; in
                                small community samples, Asian Americans also associated European Americans with ‘American’ and Asian
                                Americans more with ‘Foreign’1. Second, the American = White effect persisted even when its factual inaccuracy
                                was made conspicuous: White European celebrities of the time (e.g., Kate Winslet, Hugh Grant) were more
                                strongly associated with “American” than were Asian American celebrities (e.g., Lucy Liu, Michael Chang;1,8).
                                Third, this American = White effect emerged when White Americans were compared to other ethnic groups
                                (e.g., Black Americans, Latinx Americans), pointing to its generality1,9–12. In fact, European Americans were
                                more strongly associated with “American” than Native Americans, who are indigenous to North America11,
                                challenging the idea that the American = White effect simply reflects the timeline of immigration to the United
                                States.

                                Overview of the present research
                                The present work provides a comprehensive investigation of the American = White association over the past
                                17 years (2007–2023). In doing so, it replicates, extends, and challenges previously reported results. This
                                investigation is particularly timely, given the changing demographic landscape in the United States. In 1980,
                                Asians constituted 1.5% of the U.S. population13; today, they represent 7%14. Even in the last two decades alone,
                                the population of Asian Americans has nearly doubled, from 11.9 million in 2000 to 22.4 million in 202015.
                                Moreover, the representation of Asians in media16 and politics17 has increased dramatically, further challenging
                                the view that ‘American’ is synonymous with White. At the same time, events like the COVID-19 pandemic have
                                highlighted the persistence and strength of the longstanding stereotype that Asian Americans are foreigners18–23.
                                    Motivated by these societal changes and events, the present analysis leveraged data from over 660,000 tests
                                across 17 years to provide the most comprehensive investigation of this stereotype to date. This investigation
                                centered on three questions. First, what is the strength of the American = White effect? Specifically, we investigate
                                (a) the overall strength of implicit and explicit American–Foreign stereotypes, with a focus on implicit stereotypes;
                                and (b) the variability of stereotypes across respondent race/ethnicity. Second, what predicts variation in the
                                American = White effect? Here, we probed variation both across respondent-level variables (e.g., race/ethnicity,
                                political ideology) and across region-level features (e.g., ethnic diversity of a metropolitan area). Third, has the
                                American = White effect changed over the past 17 years (2007–2023)? And if so, is change observed across all
                                demographic groups (pointing to the role of macro-level variables like media) or only among certain groups
                                (pointing to the role of demographic-specific ideologies)? Finally, we explored whether long-run stereotype
                                trends were disrupted during or after the COVID-19 pandemic, supporting insights into how and for whom
                                societal events can influence stereotypes.
                                    Together, this work (a) replicates, extends, or challenges previous results; (b) resolves questions about whether
                                and how the American = White effect has changed; and (c) provides empirical discoveries that can support the
                                development of new hypotheses about the nature of ethnic-national stereotypes.

                                Results and discussion
                                Strength of the implicit American = White effect among American respondents
                                Overall implicit stereotype magnitude
                                In the largest available sample to date, spanning a 17-year period, evidence of an implicit American = White
                                effect was observed, IAT D = 0.24, t(509,910) = 358.94, p < 0.0001, Cohen’s d = 0.50. That is, Asian Americans were
                                more strongly associated with the concept of “Foreign,” and European Americans were more strongly associated
                                with the concept of “American.”
                                    Notably, this American = White effect emerged even though “Foreign” was represented entirely by non-
                                Asian landmarks (e.g., Eiffel tower, Pyramid of Giza) in the IAT procedure. In fact, the inclusion of European
                                landmarks such as the Eiffel tower and London Bridge, but not East Asian landmarks (e.g., Great Wall of China),
                                provided a particularly conservative test of the American = White effect.

                                Implicit stereotype strength by respondent race/ethnicity
                                Despite the overall strength of the effect, the degree of association varied substantially by respondents’
                                racial/ethnic membership. White American respondents displayed the strongest implicit American = White
                                associations, with a large effect size: IAT D = 0.38, t(231,530) = 413.00, p < 0.0001, Cohen’s d = 0.86. Third-party
                                respondents (e.g., Black or Latinx respondents) similarly displayed an implicit American = White association,
                                IAT D = 0.21, t(137,531) = 164.00, p < 0.0001, Cohen’s d = 0.44, but to a lesser degree than White Americans
                                (b = − 0.17, SE = 0.002, t(509,907) = − 109.63, p < 0.001). By contrast, East Asian American respondents displayed
                                no American = White effect, IAT D = 0.01, t(98,543) = 7.23, p < 0.0001, Cohen’s d = 0.02. Instead, they equally
                                associated Asian and European Americans with the concepts of “American” and “Foreign.”
                                    Finally, South Asians represent a unique group of interest. On the one hand, they are part of the category
                                “Asian American,” and therefore may exhibit associations that align with East Asian respondents. On the other
                                hand, the IAT included line drawings of Caucasian and East Asian faces. As such, South Asian respondents may
                                exhibit associations that align with third-party respondents. Providing support for the latter hypothesis, South
                                Asian Americans displayed a small American = White association, IAT D = 0.13, t(42,307) = 54.50, p < 0.0001,
                                Cohen’s d = 0.27, but to a lesser extent than White respondents (b = − 0.26, SE = 0.002, t(509,907) = − 105.53,
                                p < 0.001) or third-party respondents (b = − 0.09, SE = 0.003, t(509,907) = − 33.23, p < 0.001).
                                    Taken together, three findings are worth emphasizing. First, the magnitude of this overall American = White
                                effect is striking; it was almost twice as strong as the effect of another non-factual stereotype, Human = White/
                                Animal = Black24. Second, unlike the samples of college students collected in the early 2000s, the East Asian
                                respondents (n = 98,543) in the present data displayed no implicit American = White association. Third and

                                Strength of the explicit American = White effect among American respondents
                                Strikingly, this American = White effect was similarly observed on a measure of explicit cognition. When
                                respondents were asked to report their explicitly held beliefs, a moderate American = White association emerged,
                                M = 0.48, t(509,910) = 360.71, p < 0.0001, Cohen’s d = 0.51. However, unlike data from the IAT, East Asian and
                                South Asian respondents reported the strongest explicit American = White association and White and third-party
                                respondents displayed the weakest explicit association (see Table 1; see SI Table 2.2 for post-hoc comparisons).
                                That is, both East Asian (M = 0.74, t(98,543) = 209.00, p < 0.0001, Cohen’s d = 0.67) and South Asian (M = 0.65,
                                t(42,307) = 115.00, p < 0.0001, Cohen’s d = 0.56) respondents were most likely to report that Americans of
                                European descent are “more American” than Americans of Asian descent. In the General Discussion, we explore
                                the possibility that this result reflects Asian respondents’ awareness of societal stereotypes, rather than their
                                own endorsement of this belief. As noted above, White respondents (M = 0.38, t(231,530) = 228.00, p < 0.0001,
                                Cohen’s d = 0.47) and third-party respondents (M = 0.41, t(137,531) = 162.00, p < 0.0001, Cohen’s d = 0.44)
                                similarly endorsed this American = White association, but to a lesser extent than Asian respondents.
                                    To summarize, although the overall effect size for implicit (Cohen’s d = 0.50) and explicit (Cohen’s d = 0.51)
                                American = White effects was nearly identical, the degree of bias across racial/ethnic groups was reversed.
                                Implicit stereotypes were strongest amongst White respondents whereas explicit stereotypes were strongest
                                among Asian respondents.

                                The American = White effect is not reducible to pro-White attitudes
                                Relative to “Foreign”, the term “American” is evaluated much more positively28. In fact, labeling groups
                                as “Foreign” can engender negativity towards them28. This raises an important theoretical question: Are
                                American = White associations merely an effect of attitude (liking) rather than a stereotype about national
                                identity? In SI Appendix 5 we tested and eliminated the possibility that American = White associations are
                                reducible to pro-White attitudes. Specifically, we conducted additional analyses and an experiment (N = 1500) to
                                demonstrate that American = White associations are not driven by a valence matching between American and
                                White (more positive), and Foreign and Asian (more negative).

                                from main analyses (see SI Tables 1.1–1.2). The size and scope of the dataset also allowed for comparisons

                                Stereotype magnitude by respondent gender, religion, education, and political ideology
                                Four of the six demographic variables emerged as significant predictors of overall implicit stereotype
                                magnitude (2007–2023; see SI Appendix 3 for full regression output). Specifically, a significantly stronger
                                implicit American = White association was observed among respondents who were older (vs. younger), more
                                ideologically conservative (vs. ideologically liberal) and identified as Christian (vs. non-religious and vs.
                                identifying with another religion). By contrast, respondents’ gender (men vs. women) and level of education
                                (college-educated and non-college-educated) had little effect on IAT D scores (see Table 1). These patterns
                                persisted when regression models controlled for covariation between demographic variables and when age,
                                education, and ideology were represented with numeric, rather than categorical, values (see SI Appendix 3).
                                    A slightly different pattern emerged for explicit stereotypes, with three demographic features emerging
                                as significant predictors: race (as reported above), age, and political ideology. Mirroring implicit stereotypes,
                                gender and education had little effect on stereotype magnitude and ideologically conservative (vs. ideologically
                                liberal) respondents displayed stronger explicit American = White associations. However, diverging from
                                implicit stereotypes, explicit stereotypes were consistent across religious subgroups and younger (vs. older)
                                respondents displayed stronger explicit American = White associations (see Table 1). This latter pattern has been
                                observed elsewhere11, but future work should explore whether it is driven by increased endorsement of the
                                American = White association among young respondents, or a desire to signal stereotype awareness.

                                Stereotype magnitude by country of citizenship
                                Given the content of this stereotype, the main analyses focus on data from American respondents (see SI Table
                                1.4). However, the present analyses harness data from over 70 countries to explore how nationality impacts the
                                American = White effect (see also11,29).
                                    Nationality did emerge as a significant predictor of implicit and explicit stereotypes (see Table 1; implicit:
                                b = 0.10, SE = 0.0015, t(637,002) = 68.24, p < 0.0001; explicit: b = − 0.19, SE = 0.0064, t(637,002) = − 29.98,
                                p < 0.0001). In particular, and consistent with the ingroup prototypicality effect29, a significant interaction
                                between nationality and respondent race/ethnicity emerged. White respondents who were U.S. citizens
                                displayed an implicit stereotype that was almost 70% higher than White respondents who were not U.S. citizens,
                                b = 0.17, SE = 0.003, t(636,996) = 63.57, p < 0.0001. By contrast, East Asian respondents who were U.S. citizens
                                displayed slightly weaker implicit American = White associations than East Asian respondents who were not U.S.
                                citizens, b = − 0.04, SE = 0.003, t(636,996) = − 16.34, p < 0.0001. Similar results were obtained when using country
                                of residence (rather than citizenship) to demarcate groups (see SI Appendix 4). Moreover, given that individual
                                countries within East and South Asia vary in their culture and attitudes towards social groups30, country-level
                                means are also reported in SI Appendix 4.

                                Predictors of stereotype variability: region-level features
                                Even within the United States, significant variation was observed across geographic location. For example,
                                average implicit stereotypes (across all time) ranged from 0.57 (Johnstown, PA) to 0.09 (San Francisco-Oakland-
                                Fremont, CA; see SI Appendix 4 for MSA-level and state-level averages). Descriptively, this variation suggests
                                that the strength of respondents’ American–Foreign stereotype may be tied to characteristics of their local
                                environment. However, places vary along many interwoven features, such as demography (e.g., racial/ethnic
                                composition) and structural features (e.g., local politics). In this section, we investigate the relationship between
                                such regional features and stereotype strength. Specifically, focus on how the minority representation and the
                                political ideology of a region may help explain variation in the implicit American = White effect (see SI Appendix
                                6 for explicit analyses).

                                Relationship between minority representation and implicit stereotype strength
                                As observed in previous studies31, MSAs with larger percentages of East Asian residents (“minority representation”)
                                displayed weaker implicit American = White associations, r = − 0.68 [− 0.744, − 0.600], t(220) = − 13.70, p < 0.001.
                                Put differently, respondents living in areas with larger proportions of Asian residents had weaker implicit
                                associations. To probe the robustness of this relationship, we additionally fit multilevel models that controlled
                                for individual-level (i.e., respondent demographics) and region-level (i.e., median age, political leaning, total
                                population) covariates. These models also included a random intercept for MSA to account for the nested
                                structure of the data. Consistent with the correlation results, minority representation had a significant effect on
                                implicit stereotypes, b = − 5.72, p < 0.001. Specifically, a 1 percentage point increase in minority representation
                                predicted a 0.057 decrease in IAT D scores (see SI Appendix 4 for initial evidence that this relationship was
                                modulated by respondents’ race/ethnicity).
                                    In SI Appendices 4–5, we also report output from models exploring the relationship between implicit and
                                explicit stereotypes and other regional features. For example, these models indicate that implicit stereotypes
                                similarly decreased as a function of an MSA’s variety (the presence of multiple ethnic groups in an area31) and
                                non-White population size but was far less responsive to the percentage of Black residents (see SI Table 4.13).

                                Relationship between regional ideology and stereotype strength
                                Mirroring the finding that conservatives display stronger American = White associations than liberals,
                                Republican and swing states have been shown to have stronger implicit American = White associations32. We
                                replicated this pattern using a larger timespan (2007–2023), a more granular unit of analysis (MSAs rather
                                than states), and a continuous measure of local ideology (percentage voting for a Republican presidential
                                candidate). Specifically, increases in the percentage of the MSA voting for a Republican presidential or House
                                of Representatives candidate corresponded to increases in the strength of the American = White association
                                (|r > 0.35|; see SI Table 4.13). These patterns were replicated in multilevel models (b ≥ |2.3|; see SI Table 4.15).

                                Patterns of stereotype change
                                Thus far, we have shown that while the magnitude of the American = White association varies by respondent-
                                level and region-level predictors, the stereotype is robust and prevalent across most demographic subgroups and
                                geographic areas. Such bias prompted the following question: Can this strong and pervasive stereotype change or
                                is it too deeply rooted? Previous research has documented that some implicit biases have shifted toward neutrality
                                (e.g., sexuality and race bias) over the past decade while others have remained stable (e.g., disability and weight
                                bias)33,34.
                                     This question of change or stability in American–Foreign stereotypes was explored against the backdrop of a
                                rapidly changing U.S. demographic landscape, with the population of Asian Americans growing faster than any
                                other racial/ethnic group15. On the one hand, changes in demographics may translate into changing stereotypes,
                                given that respondents living in areas with more minority representation displayed weaker American = White
                                associations (see also31,35). On the other hand, the COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the persistence of the
                                perpetual foreigner stereotype, and the possibility that this stereotype has remained stable over the past 17 years.
                                We examined these competing possibilities by exploring change overall and across demographic subgroups (see
                                Analytic Strategy for details about time series analyses and SI Tables 4.1–2 for full ARIMA output).

                                Overall patterns of change (2007–2023)
                                Over the past 17 years (2007–2023), both implicit and explicit stereotypes have moved towards neutrality.
                                Crucially, implicit American = White associations decreased by 41% over the past 17 years, from a mean IAT D
                                score of 0.35 in 2007 to 0.20 in 2023. ARIMA forecasts—which have been shown to reliably predict future change
                                in implicit associations34—predict that implicit stereotypes could reach neutrality as early as 2035, should past
                                trends continue. Even if implicit stereotypes take longer to reach neutrality than forecasted, the speed of change
                                observed over the past 17 years is notable; a decrease of 41% makes American–Foreign stereotypes the second
                                fastest changing implicit bias, measured over similar timeframes34. The only implicit bias that has recorded faster
                                change is implicit sexuality bias (65% drop from 2007 to 2020).
                                    Explicit American = Foreign associations changed toward neutrality at the same rate, decreasing by 47% from
                                0.67 in 2007 to 0.35 in 2023. Moreover, ARIMA forecasts project that explicit stereotypes could reach neutrality
                                in just seven years, as early as 2030, should past trends continue.

                                Demographic differences in stereotype change
                                Given this substantial change toward neutrality, we examined whether change was widespread across
                                demographic subgroups or limited to particular demographic subgroups. Remarkably, for implicit stereotypes,
                                14/14 demographic subgroups recorded change toward neutrality over the past 17 years (see Fig. 1 for a visual
                                depiction of subgroup trajectories and SI Table 4.1 for corresponding values). That is, despite change in overall
                                stereotype magnitude, change was widespread. Indeed, respondents across self-reported gender, education, and
                                religion not only showed shifts towards neutrality but changed in parallel (i.e., at the same rate). Even White
                                and East Asian respondents, who displayed the largest gaps in stereotype strength, recorded similar levels of raw
                                change (see Table 2).
                                    Although every demographic group changed toward neutrality, two groups changed faster than others: self-
                                identified liberals and younger respondents showed a steeper reduction in implicit stereotypes than conservatives
                                and older respondents, respectively. In fact, older respondents recorded the lowest rates of change of any
                                demographic group (see Table 2). Supplemental analyses indicate that this pattern was driven by the Boomer
                                generation, suggesting that early cultural experiences (e.g., small proportion of Asian Americans, proximity to
                                wars fought in East Asia;36) may durably shape implicit stereotypes (see SI Appendix 6).
                                    These widespread trends toward neutrality are promising, but it is worth emphasizing that only one
                                demographic group has reached neutrality, i.e., to display no implicit American = White association. In 2016,
                                East Asian Americans became the first and only group to reach neutrality. South Asian respondents are also
                                projected to reach neutrality within the next year, should past trends continue. Nevertheless, all other groups
                                displayed an American = White association, even at the most recent time point (see Table 2).
                                    Change in explicit stereotypes was also observed in every demographic subgroup (see Fig. 1 and SI Table 4.2
                                for full ARIMA output with model parameters and forecasts). However, unlike implicit stereotypes, no group
                                reached neutrality. In fact, ARIMA forecasts predict that it will take at least 12.4 years for East Asian respondents’
                                explicit stereotypes to reach neutrality if past trends continue.
                                    Nevertheless, mirroring the results from implicit stereotypes, respondents’ explicit stereotypes changed
                                towards neutrality in parallel, regardless of gender, education, or religion, and younger respondents changed
                                more, in terms of raw change, than older respondents. Finally, diverging from implicit patterns, explicit stereotype
                                trends converged for liberals and conservatives, with conservatives changing slightly more than liberals in terms
                                of raw change (see Table 2).

                                Exploratory analyses of change during COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2023)
                                We additionally sought to explore whether a major societal event—the COVID-19 pandemic—influenced long-
                                term stereotype trends. Darling-Hammond and colleagues23 reported that implicit stereotype trends reversed
                                course and began increasing in the three weeks following the proliferation of the term “Chinese virus” on
                                Twitter (3/9/20–4/1/20). Other work similarly documented the consequences of such xenophobic language and
                                actions18–22,37. With the benefit of more data covering the pandemic years, we explored whether the change
                                observed in March 2020 was a reliable inflection point or only a temporary disruption (see34 for examples of
                                temporary disruptions in attitude trends).
                                    Given both the importance and difficulty of understanding the relationships between events and associations,

                                General discussion
                                Overall, the present paper provides the first comprehensive investigation of the American = White association.
                                Although several of these findings have been reported in piecemeal fashion elsewhere, the current research offers
                                three key contributions. First, it provides important benchmarking for the current strength of both implicit and
                                explicit American–Foreign stereotypes in the largest sample to date (N = 666,623). Second, it examines individual,
                                regional, and temporal features of this stereotype in a single location. Third, it charts patterns of change across
                                the past 17 years (2007–2023), a period marked by substantial change for Asian Americans. Together, these
                                findings elucidate new insights into the strength, patterns, and correlates of the cultural stereotypes of Asian
                                Americans, thereby encouraging new empirically-derived hypotheses.

                                    First, pointing to the magnitude of this stereotype, robust implicit and explicit American = European
                                American/Foreign = Asian American (“American = White”) associations emerged in a massive sample of
                                American respondents. In fact, the magnitude of the implicit stereotype reported here is likely a conservative
                                estimate, given that “Foreign” was represented partially by European landmarks and the overrepresentation
                                of East Asian and non-White respondents, who had markedly lower levels of stereotyping. Indeed, stereotype
                                trends weighted to the US population indicate higher levels of bias (see SI Appendix 2). Thus, regardless of how
                                the stereotype is assessed (on implicit or explicit measures) or analyzed (with or without weighting), we see clear
                                evidence for a strong, but non-factual stereotype. Both groups have an equal probability of being American
                                (p = 1), given that they are labeled as “Asian American” or “European American.” Yet, European Americans are
                                consistently more associated with American (vs. Foreign) than Asian Americans.
                                    Second, implicit and explicit American = White associations were observed in 13/14 and 14/14 demographic
                                subgroups, respectively. However, although this stereotype was widely held, meaningful variations were observed
                                in the degree of bias across respondent-level and region-level features. Most notably, East Asian and White
                                respondents displayed opposite patterns of results. Specifically, East Asian respondents displayed no implicit
                                American = White association, but the strongest levels of explicit American = White association. By contrast,
                                White respondents displayed the strongest levels of implicit stereotyping, but significantly weaker explicit
                                stereotypes than East and South Asian respondents. This dissociation amongst Asian American respondents
                                may emerge because they routinely have their American identity questioned39, and therefore may be especially
                                attuned to this perpetual foreigner stereotype. As such, Asian American respondents may (a) genuinely possess
                                a feeling of being less “American” than their European counterparts; and/or (b) report that Europeans are more
                                American because they are aware of this societal association, not because they endorse this stereotype.
                                    In addition to variation across race/ethnicity, stronger implicit stereotypes were observed among Americans
                                (vs. non-Americans), and American respondents who were older (vs. younger), more ideologically conservative
                                (vs. ideologically liberal) and identified as Christian (vs. non-religious and vs. identifying with another religion).
                                For explicit stereotypes, stronger stereotypes were observed among East Asian, younger, and conservative
                                respondents. Ultimately, documenting these patterns across demographic groups is important as they can be used
                                to create hypotheses about why American = White associations exist and potentially the types of interventions
                                that can loosen their hold.
                                    Interestingly, when aggregated to the region-level, indicators of both racial/ethnic composition and regional
                                ideology continued to explain significant variation in stereotype strength. Specifically, building on previous work
                                (e.g.,31), metropolitan areas with larger percentages of East Asian residents displayed weaker American = White
                                associations. Similarly, MSAs with larger shares of residents voting for Republican candidates displayed stronger
                                American = White associations. Future work should examine the mechanisms underlying this effect (e.g.,
                                contact driving the reduction of bias among MSAs with high minority representation) and the direction of this
                                effect. That is, whether (a) local ideology impacts stereotype strength; or (b) the American = White effect is more
                                consistent with conservative ideology, and therefore covaries with local ideology.
                                    Third and finally, an analysis of change across 17 years (2007–2023) showed that the American = White
                                effect is rapidly changing toward neutrality. Implicit stereotypes decreased by 41%, making American–Foreign
                                stereotypes the second-fastest changing implicit bias. Explicit stereotypes similarly showed substantial decreases,
                                with bias decreasing by 47%. Remarkably, implicit and explicit stereotypes moved towards neutrality in every
                                demographic subgroup, pointing to the potential role of more societal-level factors in shaping long-term change.
                                However, despite this widespread change, meaningful differences in the speed of change were observed. In
                                particular, self-identified liberals and younger respondents showed a steeper reduction in implicit stereotypes than
                                conservatives and older respondents, respectively. Indeed, further emphasizing the role of cultural experiences
                                in both spurring or hindering change, respondents born between 1946 to 1964 (“Boomer” generation) displayed
                                the lowest rates of change. These respondents were not only raised against the backdrop of WWII and the Korean

www.nature.com/scientificreports/


                                War, but also belong to the generation that fought in the Vietnam War. Additionally, during much of their
                                early life, Asians accounted for < 1% of the population. All these factors may have contributed to the stronger
                                stereotypes observed among Boomers.
                                    In addition to change overall and across demographic subgroups, we explored the potential role of COVID-19
                                in disrupting stereotype trajectories. We found that implicit stereotype trends were disrupted for White but not
                                East Asian respondents, while explicit stereotypes were disrupted for both White and East Asian respondents,
                                and at similar times. However, given the conservative pandemic range (2020–2023), future work should more
                                clearly disentangle any effect of COVID-19 from other concurrent events (e.g., changes in media representation).
                                In fact, with the current data in hand, future work is poised to explore questions including the lag (i.e., the delay
                                between the event and stereotype change) or duration of the stereotype change following an event. Additionally,
                                future work can consider differences in the lag and duration when the event is singular (e.g., a statement from a
                                prominent individual) versus more diffuse (e.g., media information received during a long pandemic)34.
                                    Overall, the present work provided a comprehensive investigation into the magnitude, predictors, and change
                                of the American = White association in the largest sample to-date. At the highest level, results suggest cautious
                                optimism. Implicit and explicit stereotypes reduced by 41% and 47%, respectively, and trends moved towards
                                neutrality in every demographic subgroup. Moreover, demographic subgroup and region-level analyses point to
                                predictors of where even more rapid change is observed and may help inform future interventions that decrease
                                the American = White association more durably.

                                Materials and methods
                                All original data collection reported in this manuscript was approved by Harvard University’s Institutional Review
                                Board (identification code: IRB20-1024; date of approval: 6/8/2020) and conducted with informed consent
                                and in accordance with relevant guidelines. All measures and materials are provided in the Supplementary
                                Information (SI) Appendix 2, and reproducible cleaning and analysis scripts are publicly available from OSF:
                                https://osf.io/7zg62/.

                                Respondents
                                Volunteer respondents completed the Asian-European American IAT (also called the “Asian American IAT” or
                                an “ethnic-national IAT”) on the Project Implicit demonstration website (https://implicit.harvard.edu) between
                                January 1, 2007 and December 31, 2023. Project Implicit is a non-profit organization and international research
                                collaborative that hosts internet-based IATs and other implicit measures. Respondents on the demonstration
                                website can choose to complete one of several IATs and volunteer their time in exchange for learning more about
                                their implicit associations. The database was retrieved from open data archived at the Open Science Framework
                                (https://osf.io/cpmfk/).
                                    Following previous work with this data33,34,40,41, respondents who did not complete the measures of implicit
                                and explicit stereotypes, or failed to meet the standards of the IAT D score algorithm42, were excluded from
                                analyses. Additionally, because of the need to re-weight the data for demographic representativeness (see below)
                                respondents without complete demographic information on age, gender, race, political ideology, education, and
                                religion were excluded. Finally, given the content of this association, the primary analyses were restricted to
                                respondents who resided in the United States and were US citizens (n = 509,991). That is, the findings reported
                                in the main text rely on data from US respondents unless otherwise specified. Data from non-US citizens
                                (n = 149,250) were retained for nationality contrasts (see “Stereotype Magnitude by Country of Citizenship”)
                                and supplemental analyses (see SI Appendix 4). For detailed information about the data used in each section,
                                see SI Table 1.4.
                                    This final sample of respondents was female (63%) and liberal (54%), with a mean age of 28.4 (SDage = 12.67).
                                Unlike many other tests on the demonstration site (e.g.,40), the present sample was majority non-White;
                                specifically, it consisted of 45% White (n = 231,530), 19% East Asian (n = 98,543), 8% South Asian (n = 42,307),
                                8% Hispanic (n = 43,069), and 4% Black or African American (n = 20,975) respondents. Additional details about
                                demographics are available in the SI Tables 1.1–1.3.

                                Materials
                                Implicit stereotypes
                                To measure the strength of implicit ethnic-national associations, respondents completed a standard 7-block
                                Implicit Association Test (IAT;2). The target categories (European American, Asian American) were represented
                                by line drawings of European and East Asian faces, and the attributes (American, Foreign) were represented by
                                famous American landmarks (e.g., The White House, Hollywood sign) and foreign landmarks (e.g., Eiffel tower,
                                Pyramid of Giza). None of the foreign landmarks were from Asia, creating a conservative test of the association
                                between Asian with ‘Foreign.’ Images of stimuli are included in SI Appendix 2.
                                    In this ethnic-national IAT, positive IAT D scores indicate a stronger association of European American
                                (relative to Asian American) with the attribute American (over Foreign). Negative IAT D scores indicate a
                                stronger association of Asian American (relative to European American) with the attribute American (over
                                Foreign). For ease of reporting, we commonly referred to the categories European American and Asian American
                                as “White” and “Asian,” respectively. Furthermore, in the context of this paper, “Asian” refers to individuals of
                                East Asian descent. These broad labels are adopted because of the narrow context of the present study, but have
                                inherent limitations25.

                                Explicit stereotypes
                                To measure explicit stereotypes, respondents were asked to report their beliefs about the degree to which
                                Americans of Asian or European descent are “more American” on a scale from − 1 (“I consider Americans of

www.nature.com/scientificreports/


                                Asian descent to be much more American than Americans of European descent”) to 7 (“I consider Americans
                                of European descent to be much more American than Americans of Asian descent.”). For ease of interpretation,
                                this scale has been recoded to make 0 the midpoint. As with the IAT D scores, positive scores indicated that the
                                attribute “American” was more strongly associated with European Americans, whereas negative scores indicated
                                that attribute “American” was more strongly associated with Asian Americans.

                                Explicit attitudes
                                To measure explicit attitudes, respondents reported their preference for Asian Americans or European
                                Americans on a scale from 1 (“I strongly prefer Asian Americans to European Americans.”) to 7 (“I strongly
                                prefer European Americans to Asian Americans.”), with the midpoint (4) representing equal liking for both
                                groups (“I like European Americans and Asian Americans equally.”). Like the explicit stereotype measure, this
                                scale has been recoded to make 0 the midpoint; positive values indicate a preference for European Americans
                                and negative values indicate a preference for European Americans.

                                Demographic variables
                                Data about respondents’ age, gender, race/ethnicity, level of education, political ideology, religion, college major,
                                occupation and geographic information (including their country or region of primary citizenship, their country
                                or region of primary residence, their current zip code, and the zip code corresponding to their longest residence)
                                were requested. To protect respondents’ privacy43, zip code data was aggregated into metropolitan statistical
                                areas (MSAs).

                                Analytic strategy to measure differences across demographic groups
                                Descriptive Statistics. For every demographic subgroup, mean levels of implicit and explicit stereotypes,
                                one-sample t-tests, and corresponding Cohen’s d effect sizes were provided (see, for example, Table 1). These
                                descriptive statistics were provided as an estimate of stereotype strength within each subgroup. For comparisons
                                of stereotype strength across subgroups, see “Inferential Statistics.”
                                    Inferential Statistics. A series of regression models were fitted to estimate demographic differences in
                                stereotype strength. Specifically, linear regression models with IAT D score as the outcome variable and the
                                demographic variable of interest (e.g., respondents’ race/ethnicity) were used to assess the main effect of the
                                demographic variables on implicit stereotypes. Pairwise comparisons were used to assess whether demographic
                                subgroups were significantly different from each other. Moreover, to control for covariation between
                                demographic variables, we fitted additional models with all demographic predictors entered into the same
                                model. We employed a similar analytic approach to estimate main effects of demographic variables on explicit
                                stereotypes, conduct pairwise comparisons, and control for covariates. The only difference was that we fitted a
                                series of robust regressions, rather than linear regressions, given that explicit stereotypes were measured on a
                                7-point Likert scale.

                                Analytic strategy to measure stereotype change
                                Following Charlesworth and Banaji33, we use Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) timeseries
                                models44 to assess stability or change of American–Foreign stereotypes. Briefly, these models (a) account for
                                temporal autocorrelations; (b) capture non-linearity and seasonality in trends; and (c) offer intuitive forecasts
                                about likely patterns of future change. Indeed, recently, Charlesworth and Banaji34 demonstrated that ARIMA
                                forecasts for implicit and explicit attitude trends (based on data from 2007 to 2016) provided generally accurate
                                estimates of more recent trends (from 2016 to 2020), thus placing further confidence in the validity of the
                                ARIMA method.
                                       Additionally, raking and weighting was used to ensure that any observed long-term changes were not
                                attributable to changes in sample characteristics (e.g., sample becoming more liberal over time). These
                                approaches adjusted the final monthly mean estimates to maintain similar demographic compositions over time
                                (e.g., to ensure that the relative weight given to liberals vs. conservatives was similar over all time; see1,2 for
                                details). Moreover, additional robustness analyses were conducted to ensure that the observed patterns of change
                                were not a consequence of the weighting approach, repeat test-takers, or sample source (see SI Appendix for full
                                details).
