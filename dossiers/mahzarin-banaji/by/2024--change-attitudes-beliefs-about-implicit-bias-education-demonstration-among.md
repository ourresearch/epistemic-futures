reach the desired goal of effective policing. Recently, many of these efforts
have focused on increased education about historically ignored aspects of
social relations between police and communities and, specifically, on the
psychology of bias (CBS News, 2019, Justice Policy Center, 2019).

about implicit bias education among law enforcement. Utilizing a 2-wave,
pre-/post-education panel design (N = 263), we provide a demonstration of
significant and even dramatic opinion change among members of a police
department concerning the value and importance of implicit bias education.

enforcement law (Kella, Morehouse, & Banaji, 2022).


Implicit bias and the law
A large body of evidence in psychology has established the existence of
automatic or unconscious bias (Devine, 1989, Dovidio & Gaertner, 2000).
The term implicit stereotype was introduced by Banaji, Hardin, and
Rothman (1993), and the term implicit bias was introduced into scientific
psychology by Greenwald & Banaji (1995) in their paper titled “Implicit


                                                  PHILOSOPHICAL PSYCHOLOGY      3


Social Cognition: Attitudes, Stereotypes, and Self-Esteem”. Today, the term
implicit bias is widely used in academic scholarship and public discourse as
an umbrella term for thoughts, feelings, and behaviors of which we may be
unaware or not able to control. When disparities emerge in the data between
the values of fairness and equity, on the one hand, and disparities in
treatment and outcome by social group (e.g., age, class, gender, sexuality,
race/ethnicity, religion, nationality), on the other hand, implicit bias is
commonly evoked as a possible explanation. A person or institution did
not intend to discriminate based on social group membership but the data
reveal disparity in treatment or outcomes. This tension is the signature of
implicit bias, and such dissociation (i.e., a cognitive-affective separation
between an individual’s implicit cognition and that same individual’s own
conscious values and intentions) is repeatedly observed (Devine, 1989,
Dovidio & Gaertner, 2000, Banaji & Greenwald, 2013).
   Thousands of published papers document the presence of implicit bias in
laboratory and online settings, as well as demonstrations of implicit bias in
real-world settings such as jobs, housing, finance, healthcare, education, and
law and law enforcement (Charlesworth & Banaji, 2021, Greenwald et al.,
2022). Implicit bias has been observed on a wide range of specific tests,
including age, gender, race, ethnicity, sexuality, disability, body weight, and
many more social categories, among people who believe that they, unlike
others, do not harbor bias or that they have less bias than their own behavior
might imply (Pronin, Lin, & Ross, 2002). Although research on implicit bias
has its share of deniers, as do other scientific findings (e.g., climate change),
it is broadly understood in the social and behavioral sciences to be an
ordinary feature of human minds, with clear effects on determining life
opportunities and outcomes (Jost et al., 2009).
   At the individual level, implicit bias has been linked to discriminatory
policing behavior (for a review, see Spencer, Charbonneau, & Glaser, 2016
or Fridell, 2017) and disparate outcomes in criminal justice (e.g., Vitriol,
Appleby, & Borgida, 2019) and civil law (Kurdi, Carroll, & Banaji, 2021).
Payne, Vuletich, and Lundberg (2017) have demonstrated that such biases,
when viewed at the aggregate level, can also predict consequential disparities
and inequities in many important life domains. For example, regional levels
of anti-Black implicit bias (usually at the county or state level) predict racial
disparities in outcomes like lethal use of force by police, the extent of
militarization of police departments, traffic stops, school disciplining (see
Charlesworth & Banaji, 2022 for a summary; Hehman, Flake, & Calanchini,
2018 (Jimenez, Helm, & Arndt, 2022).;
   The concept of implicit bias was quickly regarded by legal scholars and
scientists to be relevant to the fundamental question of what it means to be
fair and how to determine whether justice has been done (see papers on the
topic from an early special issue at a law review, Greenwald & Krieger, 2006,

symposium on the topic at the National Academy of Sciences and a special
issue in the journal of the American Academy of Arts and Sciences,
Dadealus is underway. In general, such efforts have challenged traditional
notions of harm as mainly intentional and have brought recognition to the
possibility of harm in the absence of discriminatory intent (Bartlett, 2009).
The existence of implicit bias has also been cited in a wide range of judicial

Andujar, 2021).


Implicit bias education in law enforcement contexts
The law enforcement community values education to improve professional
conduct (e.g., Justice Policy Center, 2019; Kahn & Martin, 2016; Miles-
Johnson & Pickering, 2018; Paoline, Terrill, & Rossler, 2015). Police depart­
ments have commonly implemented educational programs to improve the
standards of professionalism for police interaction with and service to
multicultural communities (Cornett DeVito and McGlone, 2000, Israel et
al., 2014, Israel et al., 2017, Lyons-Padilla et al., 2018, Miles-Johnson,
Mazerolle, Pickering, & Smith, 2018). Over the past two decades, a new
angle to such teaching has evolved out of the science of implicit bias. The
focus of such teaching is to educate about the nature of the human mind via
demonstrations of the ways in which error can creep into decisions without
intent. The focus of this component of education often relies on errors in the
ways in which all humans are prone to in sensing and perceiving the world,
in attention mechanisms, in how learning and memory unfold, and in how
these processes feed into higher level cognitive operations we refer to as
inferences, evaluations, and judgments. Such educational material is often
provided in the context of theories of evolution, the role of the broader
culture and specific environments in shaping attitudes, beliefs, and beha­
vior, as well as theories of moral cognition that highlight disparities between
values and actions. This approach is itself a departure from other strategies


                                                 PHILOSOPHICAL PSYCHOLOGY      5


for discussing bias that focus on “a few bad apples”. The sheer weight of
evidence of bias in human psychology and its linkages to disparate treat­
ment and outcomes in the worlds of business, education, healthcare, law and
law enforcement is presented with the question posed: If explicit attitudes
and beliefs reveal strong and genuine endorsements of the values of fairness
and equity, how are we to understand the evidence of continued disparate
outcomes in treatment as a function of social group membership? The
answer that appears to be feasible is the presence of implicit bias. In
response to a demand for education about this concept broadly, Mahzarin
Banaji founded a free and open education program that is available at
https://www.OutsmartingImplicitBias.org.
   Many researchers, legislators, and law enforcement officials have called
for increased development, implementation, and assessment of these kind of
education programs that target implicit bias in law enforcement contexts (e.
g., Justice Policy Center, 2019, Kahn & Martin, 2016, Lyons-Padilla,
Hamedani, Markus, & Eberhardt, 2018, Miles-Johnson & Pickering, 2018,
President’s Task Force, 2015, Spencer, Charbonneau, & Glaser, 2016). For
example, a recent survey by CBS News (2019) of 155 police departments
located in major municipalities throughout the U.S. indicate that 69% are
implementing some form of implicit bias education – of these, 75%
described reforming their education programs following public controver­
sies involving police use-of-force. The increased focus on this issue since
July 2020 prompts an even more urgent set of questions concerning the role
of implicit bias education today.


Why teaching about implicit bias may matter
Human beings are able to adopt a unique pedagogical stance. Although
many species teach and learn in the “here and now,” humans appear to be
unique in the ability to “use communication to pass on generalizable knowl­
edge to others” – a feature referred to as “natural pedagogy” (Csibra &
Gergely, 2009). This of course is a major path by which humans have
changed their attitudes and beliefs so dramatically, as recorded history
bears witness. In fact, many attitudes and beliefs that were once completely
ordinary, acceptable, and put into practice no longer exist and even seem
shocking by today’s standards (e.g., trial by ordeal, the ritual of sati).
However slow progress may seem to be from the point of view of those
who are the harbingers of change, humans as a species clearly undergo
massive and, at times, rapid change, even on implicit, seemingly rigid
attitudes and stereotypes (see Charlesworth & Banaji, 2019; Charlesworth
& Banaji, 2020). People give up false beliefs, even compelling ones, such as
that the earth is flat or that it is the sun that rises and sets. Humans replace
these false beliefs with truer beliefs even though the false beliefs seem more

intuitive or easy to accept. We have come to believe, that the earth is not flat.
We have come to believe, at least consciously, that different racial/ethnic
groups of humans are equally human.
    In the demonstration provided by the current study, we applied the
simple idea that “teaching matters” to examine whether we could change
negative attitudes and beliefs toward the value of implicit bias education on
equity and accountability in members of a police department. What the
actual nature of the teaching should be and how or whether it achieves its
desired goals are empirical questions. Can bias education increase knowl­
edge of bias and its consequences, promote favorable attitudes toward the
value and perceived importance of such education, and can it empower
those who participate with the skills and confidence needed to improve their
interactions and relationship with diverse others? These outcomes are gen­
erally understood to promote reductions in bias and to minimize its con­
sequences for behavior (Carter, Onyeador, Lewis, 2020). The current
research is a modest effort to test the success and limits of implicit bias
education in a context where it has proven to be challenging.
    Any form of teaching has its challenges, and teaching about implicit bias
encounters unique challenges (Moskowitz & Vitriol, 2021). As with any
information that can pose a threat to one’s core beliefs, values, or self-
concept, some resistance to the idea of implicit bias is to be expected
(Howell, Gaither, & Ratliff, 2014). Attitude change, even on topics that are
shocking or frightening, have nonetheless occurred throughout human
history. Although a single session of teaching and learning may alone be
insufficient to bring about change in behavior – for which committed
practice and implementation of debiasing strategies at the individual, insti­
tutional and societal levels are needed – change in attitudes and beliefs often
precede or accompany change in behavior. As such, societies around the
globe value the teaching of generalizable knowledge. The entire endeavor of
learning and education, formal and informal, is understood to be a driver of
change in the contents of what we feel and believe. For this reason, we
undertook this examination as a test case of whether implicit bias education,
consisting of a few core principles, could produce a change in attitudes and
beliefs in a population of professionals known to be unfriendly, even
antagonistic, to the concept of implicit bias (i.e., law enforcement
community).
    Imposing new ways of thinking from the top (i.e., “management says”)
onto professionals who have spent their lives in the trenches of their vocation
is a mistake (Dobbin & Kalev, 2016). Such tactics are not only likely to fail, but
they can even backfire (Moskowitz & Vitriol, 2021). Institutionally driven
change, which must come with the backing and support of the leadership, is
more likely to succeed if the rank and file in that organization are tuned in
their own values and commitments to be in the same direction. Often when


                                                    PHILOSOPHICAL PSYCHOLOGY       7


institutional mandates fail, it is because they run counter to a basic human
desire for autonomy, with self-directed control over the nature of the work
one does, serving as a key example (e.g., Legault, Gutsell, & Inzlicht, 2011). In
fact, reactance to the forced implementation of rules and regulations that
constrain or threaten individuality and perceived self-interest is commonly
documented (Steindl et al., 2015). Social psychologists have also shown that
intrinsic (rather than extrinsic) motivation is more likely to lead to sustained
change (Deci & Ryan, 2013). Thus, institutions large and small, for profit and
not for profit, seeking to implement change in thinking and behavior that
responds to institutional procedures and policies (e.g., such as wearing a mask
or social distancing during a pandemic) often require “buy in” from all
members. Programs geared toward change may be more likely to succeed if
they focus on changing individuals, in addition to change that must also be
sought at the institutional level. We put to the test a specific question: can
teaching about implicit bias, a topic that is often met with suspicion among
members of police departments, be constructed in such a way as to produce
positive attitude change about the idea that implicit bias exists and must be
addressed by members of law enforcement departments?


A seminar on implicit bias
The content of implicit bias education that was devised for this experiment
was taken from the material available at https://www.
OutsmartingImplicitBias.org, and specifically from Units 1–6 provided in
the section on Guided Learning https://outsmartingimplicitbias.org/
#guided-learning. The Seminar on Implicit Bias (hereafter, SIB) was con­
structed for the current study based on two assumptions: (a) decisions may
be biased, even though the decision maker and the target of the decision
may be unaware of it and may not desire it, and (b) revealing the existence of
bias may not be welcome news. Challenging though it may be, the concept
of implicit bias has made it possible to provide education about ordinary
mental processes that produce bias. In doing so, education guided by these
assumptions are able to demonstrate that bias is a universal human phe­
nomenon rather than the problem of “a few bad apples,” which, even today,
after decades of research from social psychology, remains a dominant view.
    Although not targeted at law enforcement, education on diversity-related
issues in organizational settings has been shown to be effective at increasing
awareness of factors that contribute to bias (Bezrukova et al., 2016), with clear
benefits for reducing its impact. For example, a meta-analysis by Bezrukova et
al. (2016) concludes that diversity training is particularly effective at increasing
knowledge of cultural diversity issues (Hedges g = .57), the acquisition of
behavioral skills that facilitate positive interactions with diverse others
(Hedges g = .48), and the belief that one is able to perform competently in


8      J. A. VITRIOL ET AL.


multicultural settings and interactions (Hedges g = .30). Carlana and colleagues
observe similar effects, and further demonstrated that teachers whose bias was
made known to them are more likely to change behavior but only if they
accepted that they were biased (Alesina et al., 2018; Carlana, 2019). Similar
investigations have demonstrated that awareness is necessary for bias reduction
in real-world contexts (e.g., Carnes et al., 2015, Devine et al., 2012, Parker et al.,
2018, Regner et al., 2019, Stone et al., 2020). Clearly, efforts at teaching and
learning about bias is one path to creating a readiness to change behavior that
may not otherwise be perceived as appropriate or self-interested. Law enforce­
ment institutions and its leaders must also be ready to do their part to imple­
ment the very mechanisms that will be necessary to undermine bias, but this is
only likely to succeed if their personnel has been persuaded that such change is
indeed both right and good (for them). In this paper, we address the question of
whether education about implicit bias can change attitudes and beliefs about
such education, with specific interest in perceptions of its value for improving
police-public relations.
   Despite the call for and widespread implementation of implicit bias
education, little empirical research has evaluated its effects among law
enforcement (CBS News, 2019, Swencionis & Goff, 2017). The kind of
implicit bias education being adopted by police departments throughout
the United States has implemented evidence-based principles (e.g., Fair &
Impartial Policing, 2020, Fridell, 2017, Justice Policy Center, 2019).
However, no peer-reviewed publication is available at present. As such, we
currently lack published data on the prevalence, content, and effects of
implicit bias education for law enforcement. An important exception to
this is reported by Lyons-Padilla et al. (2018) and Janetta et al. (2019), two
separate investigations that examined the effects of procedural justice edu­
cation on police officers in law enforcement agencies throughout the United
States. This procedural justice education also included a brief module on
implicit bias. Results indicated that police officers acquired increased
knowledge of how procedural justice, implicit bias, and diversity-related
issues can impact policing behaviors. Further, both Lyons-Padilla et al.
(2018) and Janetta et al. (2019) observe gains in positive beliefs about
diversity education following participations in this intervention.
   While the results of these two investigations are promising, the unique
effects of implicit bias in law enforcement communities remains, to our
knowledge, unavailable at present in the literature. We are only able to
identify one empirical investigation of the unique effects of implicit bias
education on law enforcement that exists as a white paper, but that has not
undergone peer-review. Worden et al. (2020) examined the impact of
implicit bias training on 36,000 personnel of the New York City Police
Department (NYPD) and observed pre- to post-education increases in
knowledge about and awareness of implicit bias, expressed concern about


                                                 PHILOSOPHICAL PSYCHOLOGY     9


discrimination, motivation to act without prejudice, and knowledge of a
range of strategies they can implement to minimize the impact of bias on
their professional duties. Perhaps because of these important gains in
knowledge about and skills to control bias, NYPD officers clearly benefited
from this program, with many reporting significant increases in the use of
bias management strategies 2 to 13 months post-education. Importantly, the
present study differs from this report in the nature of the education and in
the how the program was evaluated, with our focus not on knowledge about
and skills to control bias, but on the perceived value of such education in
general.
   What is needed at this time, given the regular debates about imposing or
banning bias education (e.g., al-Gharbi, 2020; Malnick, 2021; Williamson &
Foley, 2018), is evidence of what such education can achieve by way of
increasing (or not) (a) an accurate understanding of implicit bias and its
consequences for behavior, (b) a non-defensive posture toward the infor­
mation, (c) positive change in attitudes and beliefs about the value of such
education, and (d) the belief that bias education is useful and that, with such
education, one is able to improve their interactions and relationship with
diverse others. At present, implicit bias education is often implemented
without sufficient detail for meaningful evaluation and there is scant pub­
lished research on whether it can or cannot change attitudes and beliefs.


Navigating and overcoming defensive reactions to bias education
Law enforcement personnel who participated in the program implemented
by Lyons-Padilla et al. (2018) and Janetta et al. (2019) reported negative pre-
education beliefs and expectations. Such a defensive attitude is commonly
observed among police and others when education programs address biased
treatment of members of disadvantaged or marginalized groups (Carter,
Onyeador, & Lewis, 2020, Dobbin & Kalev, 2016, Howell et al., 2013, Israel
et al., 2017, Rowe, 2007, Moskowitz & Vitriol, 2021, Thomas & Plaut, 2008,
Vitriol & Moskowitz, 2021). Unfortunately, bias education programs in law
enforcement contexts and other organizational settings are often provided
by those with only a superficial understanding of the scientific evidence;
anti-bias educators may employ pseudoscientific frameworks and jargon or
may otherwise be delivered in a way that is not grounded in the principles of
effective science communication and persuasion (Dobbin & Kalev, 2022;
Moskowitz & Vitriol, 2021). As a result, bias education programs can
backfire (Dobbin and Kalev, 2016). For example, one police officer
described the experience with bias education as “reinforcing that the police
are racist, but specifically the white male police are racist” (Pegues & Mintz,
2019, para 9). These negative expectations and experiences can undermine
the effectiveness of anti-bias programs (Dobbin & Kalev, 2016), and,

consequently, may reduce the support among law enforcement for contin­
ued investment and education on diversity issues (CBS News, 2019,
Coderoni, 2002, Miles-Johnson, 2016).
   How to educate and train law enforcement on the science of implicit bias
has long been a pressing issue. But, since the summer of 2020, in the wake of
widespread social protest and civic unrest following the death of George
Floyd (Chenoweth and Pressman 2020), it has transformed into a critical
one, if not a crisis. If police departments are interested in improving the
quality of policing they provide, and of gaining the trust of the public,
education about the science of implicit bias is a natural first step. We wish
to be clear that we are not claiming that implicit bias education is a sufficient
condition to be met to reduce police bias and improve police-citizen rela­
tionships. However, we believe that this research and especially future
research should begin to test the idea that such education may be a necessary
condition for effective policing, especially in multicultural and diverse
communities. Our reasoning is that if performed with competence and by
invoking the trust of the attendees, and when administered by qualified
scientifically grounded education about implicit bias can serve to open
minds to the evidence. We regard this education, however challenging, to
be similar to the education that police already undertake when seeking to
implement new practices that are in their best interest to adopt, such as
wearing a bullet-proof vest, or following legal procedures for conducting a
stop and search. In all professional occupations, including policing, knowl­
edge about the workings of the human mind, its capacities and limitations,
its malleability or adaptability to change, are core components of practical
and ethical conduct for modern management and leadership (Beier, 2021,
Carter, Onyeador, & Lewis, 2020, Holroyd, 2012, Jost et al., 2009).
   Throughout history, as knowledge advances societies change what they
believe and value. We wish to offer a modest empirical demonstration, using
a natural starting point for evaluating the potential of implicit bias educa­
tion in a police department whose leadership came forward to seek it. We
asked one primary question: What impact, if any, does actual participation
in scientifically grounded implicit bias education have on the attitudes and
beliefs of law enforcement officers and staff toward the education itself and
their belief that the educational experience may be valuable in improving
relations between police and citizens.
   Negative expectations about “diversity training” can lead to avoidance of
critical information or feedback (Howell et al., 2013, Dovidio & Gaertner,
2000), thereby limiting or undermining opportunities for learning about the
psychology of bias and strategies for control. Negative experiences with
diversity and bias education risk noncompliance, not in the sense that
attendance is lacking but that attendance meant little if the participant
was disengaged or explicitly counterarguing. Both negative expectations

and experiences may lead to the backfiring that undermines the effective­
ness of any education, including that about implicit bias, and instead
increases entrenchment or resentment among members of higher-status
and/or high-powered social groups, including police. Understanding atti­
tudes and beliefs toward scientifically grounded implicit bias education, and
if or how these opinions change over the course of such education, is
essential to understanding if it is a viable way forward.


The current study
Prior research indicates that awareness of bias is necessary (although alone
insufficient) to mitigate bias and increase prejudice-regulation (e.g., Alesina
et al., 2018; Bezrukova et al., 2016; Carlana, 2019; Carnes et al., 2015; Devine
et al., 2012; Perry et al., 2015). However, attempts to enhance this awareness
can be problematic, often leading to unintended negative effects, such as
diminishing commitment toward egalitarian objectives (Dobbin & Kalev,
2016, Moskowitz & Vitriol, 2021). Despite these challenges, we propose that
acceptance of potential bias is more likely to occur after education that
accurately represents scientific understandings of bias, especially when the
educational approach minimizes the triggering of defensive reactions that
can hinder learning (Rothman, Vitriol, & Moskowitz, 2022; Vitriol, O’Shea,
& Calanchini, 2023); Vitriol & Banaji, forthcoming). In the current study,
we test this proposition in a context in which defensiveness toward bias
education may be particularly high by evaluating attitudes and beliefs prior
to and after an in-person educational Seminar on Implicit Bias (SIB) among
members of the Cambridge Municipal Police Department. The major goal
of the current study was to assess change in attitudes and beliefs about the
value and importance of bias education, following participation in the SIB.
   The content of the SIB was first created by Mahzarin Banaji in 1998 and
presented regularly since 2004 to organizations including for-profits, gov­
ernmental agencies, and nonprofits. Today, a great deal of this material is
available at a public website for any individual or organization free of charge
(see https://www.OutsmartingImplicitBias.org). This material served as the
critical educational event, the SIB, and was administered between the first
and second taking of the surveys. By presenting a survey measuring attitudes
and beliefs toward such an educational experience both before and after the
SIB, we tracked group-level and where possible, intraindividual change in
relevant attitudes and beliefs as a function of participation in the SIB.
   We should note that the SIB did not include any material on police-public
facing issues such as stops, searches, use of force, etc. Rather, the content of
the SIB focused on understanding how human minds work, the errors that
are inherent in human thinking and feeling processes, and the costs that we
bear in personnel decisions (closely mirroring the units 1–6 at https://www.


12     J. A. VITRIOL ET AL.


OutsmartingImplicitBias.org). The focus of the SIB was on internal person­
nel matters that concern all organizations, such as recruitment, hiring, and
promotion. Yet, many of the questions we posed in the nearly identical pre
and post surveys concerned police-public relations. We regarded this to be a
particular important feature of the study: to measure whether a general
seminar on implicit bias as it affects personnel decisions would translate
into more general optimism regarding the public’s perception of policing. If
the study is able to detect effects of such education on police expectations for
creating better relations with the public, it will be especially noteworthy,
since no direct content on policing per se was covered.
   The Commissioner of the Cambridge Police Department as well as the
then Deputy Superintendent Robert Lowe1 attended an educational pro­
gram led by Mahzarin Banaji for the City of Cambridge, MA. They
approached her with the possibility of conducting the same educational
seminar for the city’s police department. After discussion, the idea of this
study was conceived, by using a pre-post survey that would be administered
on either side of an educational seminar on implicit bias, with the purpose of
testing the effects of the seminar on attitudes and beliefs toward such
education. An understanding that whatever the data revealed would be
submitted for publication was also reached. Of course, based on what we
knew and newly learned, we expected law enforcement personnel to report
negative prior experience with and expectations about the specific SIB as it
would be seen as “diversity education” generally. We also expected law
enforcement to be skeptical of the potential for such education to improve
police-public relations. We entered this unusual collaborative study, one
that emerged from an invitation by the police department, with no strong
hypotheses about what we would find about the role of the SIB in changing
attitudes and beliefs among law enforcement personnel toward the value
and importance of implicit bias education. No prior research utilizing the
approach undertaken here and with a law enforcement unit had been
published at the time of our study. In other words, given what was
known, there was no basis to hypothesize which of three possibilities
would emerge: SIB would improve, diminish, or leave unchanged existing
attitudes and beliefs about implicit bias education. This research is a full
report of what we found.


Method

The general method employed in this study consisted of three phases:
administering a survey of attitudes and beliefs toward education about
topics surrounding diversity and equity, followed by delivering the semi­
nar (SIB), and then readministering a similar survey after SIB. An exact


                                                PHILOSOPHICAL PSYCHOLOGY     13


copy of both surveys, as administered, is available in the Supplemental
Materials.


Design

We henceforth refer to the survey administered prior to the SIB as Time 1
(or T1) and after the SIB as Time 2 (or T2). The same set of questions were
administered for both surveys. However, T1 survey evaluated attitudes and
beliefs about diversity education in general, whereas T2 survey posed the
same questions but about the specific SIB they had experienced. Thus, by
comparing T1 to T2 change or differences in responses to the survey, we are
able to estimate the difference between general views about such education
relative to the specific SIB that was delivered. Not all participants completed
both pre and post surveys. As such we report data in two forms. First, we
examine within-subject change in attitudes and beliefs among participants
who complete both the pre and post surveys (i.e., matched panel respon­
dents). Second, we examine between-subject differences between people who
did or did not participate in the SIB. Both analyses allow us to estimate the
impact of the SIB on attitude and beliefs toward topics both directly covered
in or indirectly implicated by the SIB. The SIB itself which lasted approxi­
mately four hours, contained one intermission. T1 surveys were made
available to participants via an online link approximately 2–3 weeks prior
to the SIB. T2 surveys were administered immediately following the SIB
upon its completion.


Participants
Data were collected from a sample of personnel from the same municipal
police department in Cambridge, Massachusetts, which employed a total of
276 law enforcement officials at the time of data collection. To allow full
participation, two separate SIBs were conducted on two different dates.
However, they consisted of the same material presented in the same order
of topic by the same presenter.

information to generate a unique identifier to match responses across
surveys) and a total of 193 participants completed the T2 survey (145
provided enough information to generate a unique identifier that was

excluded from analysis because they reported having responded to the same
survey earlier at the time of the first SIB session. To expedite the adminis­

Procedure
Members of the Cambridge Police Department, whose leadership organized
voluntary completion of the survey and attendance at the SIB, were notified
by internal e-mail communications from supervising officers of an oppor­
tunity to attend a lecture from a professor of psychology at a local university
on the science of implicit cognition and bias. The commissioner of the
Cambridge municipal police department expressed support for “police
education about blind spots that affect workplace decisions” and enthusiasm
for “opening the entire Cambridge Police Department to a scientific under­
standing about our minds and behavior.” A link to the online survey for T1
and information about the date, time, and location of the SIB was embedded
in these communications. Members of the Cambridge Police Department
this communication when the online survey was first made available
(approximately 2–3 weeks before the SIB) and were reminded again of the
survey a few days prior to the SIB.
   In prior discussion with leadership, the decision was made to offer the
session on a volunteer basis. This decision was based on previous research
that has shown the lack of effect, and even negative effects, of mandatory
bias education (CBS News, 2019, Dobbin & Kalev, 2016). Initially the
leadership was skeptical that voluntary participation would produce atten­
dance, given knowledge of negative attitudes and beliefs about such educa­
tion, but became persuaded that if the SIB was described accurately and was
of high quality it would produce high levels of participation. Indeed, our
sample includes approximately 62% of total law enforcement personnel
from the Cambridge Police Department. Thus, participation in the online
survey at T1, SIB, and in-person survey at T2 was completely voluntary, and
at no point in time was participation described as required or mandatory by
supervising officers or the researchers. At the start of the online survey, for
example, participants were provided a standard consent form that described
the purpose of the survey (i.e., “to understand what people think about
diversity training”) and reminded them that their involvement was volun­
tary. Participants were also informed that they could withdraw from the
survey or SIB at any point in time without “consequences or any loss of
benefits that you are otherwise entitled to receive”.
   After providing consent, participants at the start of T1 were given the
following instruction prompt:

Discussion
Effective and legitimate law enforcement can be enhanced by fair and
impartial policing (Justice Policy Center, 2019, Kahn & Martin, 2016).
Thus, it is no surprise that many leaders in law enforcement around the
country continue to invest in education in order to improve the effec­
tiveness and professionalism with which police serve multicultural com­
munities (e.g., Jackson & Gau, 2016; Justice Policy Center, 2019; Kahn &
Martin, 2016; Morin et al., 2017; Lyons-Padilla et al., 2018; Miles-
Johnson & Pickering, 2018; Paoline, Terrill, & Rossler, 2015).


24     J. A. VITRIOL ET AL.


Educational programs focused on procedural justice, for example, have
been found to improve the professional conduct of law enforcement and
increase the trust of the general public (Boulware-Brown, 2004; Cornett
DeVito and McGlone, 2000; Israel et al., 2014; Israel et al., 2017; Lyons-
Padilla et al., 2018; Miles-Johnson et al., 2018). Yet very little is known
about the impact of such initiatives when focused specifically on redu­
cing implicit bias among law enforcement (Swencionis & Goff, 2017).
This oversight is significant.
   Can members of the law enforcement community accept and learn from
the science of implicit bias? Given widespread implementation of educa­
tional programs that purportedly target the psychological processes that give
rise to implicit bias and its effects (e.g., CBS News, 2019; Fair & Impartial
Policing, 2015; President’s Task Force, 2015; Spencer, Charbonneau, &
Glaser, 2016), answers to this question are pressing. We provide one of
the first demonstrations of significant and even dramatic opinion change
among members of a police department concerning the value of under­
standing implicit bias as a path to increased equity and accountability.
   We evaluated reactions to and experiences with an educational seminar
on the science of implicit bias among members of the Cambridge Municipal
Police Department (N = 263). Results clearly indicate that participants held
negative preexisting beliefs or expectations about the benefits and effects of
diversity training in general, and relatively pessimistic expectations that
police-public relations could be improved. However, following participation
in our SIB, these perceptions changed dramatically; participants not only
liked the seminar but also viewed it as important and valuable to law
enforcement and the communities they serve. These findings are consistent
with that reported by Lyons-Padilla et al. (2018). Furthermore, our results
were consistent for both between-subjects who did or did not participate in
the SIB (unmatched participants) and within-subjects, who reported pre- to
post-SIB intra-individual increases in positive perceptions, experiences, and
expectations. These increases were often larger for White (vs. non-White)
participants and Patrol (vs. Supervising) Officers, indicating that the type of
individual who might be resistant was open to change in this study. No
gender differences in attitudes, beliefs, or perceptions were observed,
demonstrating the broad impact of the education. Together, this pattern
of results suggests that the negative prior experiences and expectations with
diversity training, in general, did not carry over among those who partici­
pated in the SIB – instead, many came to perceive bias education as in their
own best interest and able to improve their relationship with the general
public. These results primarily show a positive reaction to our specific bias
training program, compared to officers’ past experiences or expectations of
diversity training, in general, which may or may not signal a widespread
change in attitudes toward all bias education and diversity trainings. Future


                                                 PHILOSOPHICAL PSYCHOLOGY      25


studies should investigate if positive experiences with specific programs like
ours can influence broader attitudes toward diversity training.
   Our results are particularly compelling given that defensiveness is com­
monly observed among law enforcement for other kinds of diversity train­
ing (Carter, Onyeador, & Lewis, 2020, CBS News, 2019, Howell et al., 2013,
Israel et al., 2017, Miles-Johnson, 2016, Rowe, 2007, Moskowitz & Vitriol,
2021, Thomas & Plaut, 2008, Vitriol & Moskowitz, 2021). Unfortunately,
diversity training is not always implemented by those with adequate quali­
fications or expertise, nor is it always guided by cutting-edge research in the
social and behavior sciences (Dobbin, 2009; Ehrke, Berthold, & Steffens,
2014; Paluck, 2006). Consequently, even well-intended implicit bias educa­
tion often is misguided and ineffective or, worse, backfires (Moskowitz &
Vitriol, 2021). For example, anti-bias education that is perceived as threa­
tening the integrity of its participants engenders avoidance, resentment, and
noncompliance (CBS News, 2019; Dobbin & Kalev, 2016; Dover, Major, &
Kaiser, 2016). Despite these obstacles, our results demonstrate that law
enforcement came to regard implicit bias education, when grounded in
credible evidence in the social and behavioral sciences, as consistent with
their duty and responsibilities to their communities.
   It is noteworthy that we observed significant increases in the belief that
police-public relations can be improved, as no material directly relevant to
the police-public relationship was mentioned in the seminar. We take this as
evidence that law enforcement is interested in a rational, science-based
approach to understanding their own minds as a guide to improving
behavior or policy. Even a general seminar about the concept and meaning
of implicit bias can change the existing attitudes and beliefs of law enforce­
ment about the value of such knowledge. Positive attitudes toward the topic
of implicit bias is likely to reduce resistance to efforts directed at individual
and institutional change. Our results, obtained from no less than members
of a police department, suggest that implicit bias education can play a
necessary and sufficient role in changing minds about the presence of bias
in individuals and institutions. Yet, while receptivity to the science of
implicit bias can indeed facilitate learning and growth, it is merely a first
step on a path toward improvement. Changing behavior over the long-term
will require additional work, the foundation of which, we argue theoreti­
cally, will still require persuasion of individuals in any system seeking to
change.
   It is our considered opinion that the educational program that was
conducted is unlikely to have direct impact on critical behaviors, such as
enhanced fairness in personnel decisions or actual change in police-citizen
interactions. The reason is clear: the intervention was designed to test
whether change in attitudes and beliefs in regard to implicit bias education
can occur. We believe that this educational program is likely to increase an


26     J. A. VITRIOL ET AL.


openness, among members of this police department, to consider changes in
their own behavior to ensure greater fairness and equity in their interactions
with the public. It may also help precipitate changes in their institution’s
practices, procedures, and policies that are typically resisted (e.g., wearing of
bodycams).
   We wish to bring this question into relief and assert with clarity that
interventions must be measured in relation to what they are meant to do,
using the same standards applied to any educational program: in this case,
did it inform and edify the target audience and leave them with a sense that
they had acquired useful and valuable knowledge? We answer this question,
for the first time, affirmatively in this paper. One can also ask, did the
audience accurately learn the material? Lyons-Padilla et al. (2018), Janetta et
al. (2019), and Worden et al. (2020) demonstrate that implicit bias educa­
tion can equip participants with the means to answer, with accuracy, ques­
tions of facts. As to the extent to which such knowledge led to meaningful
change in behavior and policies, however, while critically important, remain
a task for future research to address (see Carter, Onyeador, Lewis, 2020).
   Of course, the quality of the particular education program on any of these
topics is a critical factor to consider when evaluating its impact on change in
attitudes and beliefs. Bias education programs sometimes do such a poor job
of achieving its goals that audience members disengage or even leave in
protest. As such, all programs that claim to provide education on a host of
challenges our planet confronts, be it on climate change, disinformation, or
implicit bias, requires evaluation and the results publicly reported, prefer­
ably via peer review. In this paper, we provided a single example of the
impact of a particular educational experience on change in attitude and
beliefs in a population known to resist and even resent such education. In
fact, the T1 survey we administered verified that negative attitudes and
beliefs toward diversity training programs were clearly present. We also
showed that these opinions can be sharply changed by education that is
scientifically grounded. We encourage other similar reports based on any
method of education that is considered worthy of test.
   Our investigation of this first step in the unfolding process of change
anticipates that increases in the perceived value of learning about bias will,
over time, lead to increased understanding and therefore change in behavior
and policies. Nonetheless, our analysis is limited by its focus on perceptions
of and experiences with the specific SIB that we implemented, among this
group of law enforcement professionals from this particular police depart­
ment. A strength of this study is that we were able to obtain almost all
members of the police department in our study. However, by focusing on a
single department we were also limited in the size of the sample we could
obtain. Specifically, the study is underpowered for analysis of matched
participants, i.e., participants who had completed both baseline and post-


                                                  PHILOSOPHICAL PSYCHOLOGY      27


education session. Although the between-subjects analysis and the within-
subjects analyses tell the same story, future research will be needed replicate
the matched participants analysis with a larger sample. Additional investi­
gations should therefore seek to replicate our observations on a larger
sample, and more directly examine the underlying mechanisms that are
theorized to reduce defensive responding, increase bias awareness, and
motivate prejudice-regulation and bias mitigation over time (Devine et al.,
2012). Indeed, the path to behavioral change is complicated and fraught (see
Forscher et al., 2019; Moskowitz & Vitriol, 2021). It remains a scientific
imperative to develop theoretical frameworks to understand the link
between (a) the content and administration of bias education programs,
(b) targeted psychological processes (e.g., bias awareness, egalitarian moti­
vations, skills for prejudice-regulations), and (c) behavior change, both in
the short and long-term. This goal can be advanced by assessing, in both
laboratory and applied contexts, the isolated effects of distinct aspects or
features of bias education programs, underlying psychological mechanisms,
and change in motivations, attitudes, and behaviors over time. We antici­
pate that providing access to the content of the material we used in a free
and publicly accessible format (https://www.OutsmartingImplicitBias.org)
will allow others to conduct tests on the variations of the material we used
here.
   Future research is also needed to evaluate whether or not the positive
perceptions, experiences, and expectations reported by police officers in
attendance at our SIB generalize to other contexts, education programs, and
police departments. We sought to examine the effects of participating in
evidenced-based education by a qualified expert, but there can be variability
in the individual who administers such a program, and it is not always
possible to recruit a scientist who is an expert on research on implicit bias be
the presenter. Yet another limitation of our experiment is that a professor at
Harvard University conducted the seminar for a police department in the
city in which the university is located. It remains to be tested whether these
results generalize to situations where institutional credibility is not similarly
working to the advantage of the educational program.
   Similarly, future investigations are needed to evaluate the indirect effects
of implementing these programs in law enforcement communities on long-
term behavioral or policy change (Kahn & Martin, 2016). Interventions that
fail to induce meaningful or sustainable change in policies and behavior
reinforce “illusions of fairness” (Kaiser et al., 2013, p.504). Failed or counter­
productive interventions can create the perception that bias is not an issue
of concern (e.g., Coderoni, 2002) and that claims to the contrary are
erroneous, misguided, and undeserving of remediation. Law enforcement
may gain transient increases in public trust by implementing ineffective or
superficial programs that at least appear sincerely committed to addressing


28        J. A. VITRIOL ET AL.


the problem of bias in policing. But, when such programs fail to actually
change policies or behavior, they are unlikely to produce sustainable
increases in perceptions of legitimacy that come from actual improvement
in fair and impartial practices. Examinations of the effects of these programs
on the behavior of law enforcement remains critical. Doing so will help aid
police departments around the country in their effort to identify ways to
improve the objectivity, accuracy, and perceived legitimacy of modern
policing in diverse and multicultural communities.
