# Mahzarin Banaji on bias and AI: How will they co-exist?

## Full text

Editor's Note: This exclusive interview with Mahzarin Banaji marks the third in our six-part series exploring the crucial intersection of technology and inequality, based on Harvard Business School's invitation-only summit "Inequality in the Digital Age." On March 8, 2024, HBS Associate Professor Michael Luca and the Race, Gender, Equity Initiative convened economists, psychologists, and other scholars to explore how the latest technology boom is rapidly reshaping today's world. As part of our ongoing commitment to sharing world-class, evidence-based insights with you, BiGS Head of Communications Barbara DeLollis engaged in candid conversations with six distinguished scholars on the sidelines. We've edited this transcript for length, clarity, and style. Stay tuned for more and be sure to join our growing LinkedIn community ! 
 Q. Thanks for sitting down with us to discuss your latest research. You've been doing groundbreaking work for nearly 40 years on bias that humans are not consciously aware of ("implicit bias"). Before we dig into its relevance in the age of artificial intelligence (AI) , tell us about this bias.
 A. I think what I study in some ways is the new American dilemma. That is to say, we've changed so much since then that at some conscious level, we are truly unbiased. When we ask Americans how they feel about people from two different groups receiving the same opportunities, you know, access to the same resources … the majority of the country believes that you should be judged by the content of your character, by your talents, by the work you've done, not by the groups that you belong to, not based on whether you were born into a rich family or a poor family, for example. So we genuinely, truly believe that.
 And yet when we look at the data in important domains … what you find over and over again are huge disparities. … whether they're gender-based or race-based or whatever language, nationality, religion, etc. … for example, if I'm asked, do you have any bias, would you treat two people equally? I would say, ‘Of course I would. Why are you bothering me with this silly question?’ But it turns out if you watch my behavior as a doctor or as a teacher or as a judge or a lawyer or any person doing any ordinary job, you will see that those very people who profess to be true egalitarians aren't behaving that way.
 So, for example, two people, one black and one white, can say, ‘I'm in pain and I'm a six on a seven-point scale. That's how bad the pain is right now.’ The data show in every region of the country where these tests have been done that black Americans get prescribed lower amounts of painkillers for reporting the same level of pain. Or, two kids in a school will behave in the same somewhat aggressive way, shoving another kid. How those kids get treated depend[s] on gender, depend[s] on race, depend[s] on social class. 
 
 This is the new American dilemma. … And, when we talk to … amazing doctors who do everything to try to create equality in the way in which people will get treatment in their medical system, they're just shocked when they see the data. So that's what I'm interested in. How is that happening?

 Q. Let's transition to the theme of the day - AI .
 A. We are in a moment now when AI is available to us, but also has been introduced into just about every aspect of our lives …
 
 We know that the introduction of algorithms in certain decision-making can be very good. So for people, places that do health research, you know, they will show us that an algorithm is able to detect a tumor or something X percentage times better than the average radiologist.
 
 If an algorithm is catching more real tumors that a human was missing, that's what we want AI for. And it will do that. It is already doing that. But there are many other instances in which AI has been introduced without testing and where we should have reason to worry and worry quite deeply. Some algorithms look at facial tics and movements during a job interview, and then those data are fed to the higher-ups] and somehow it's been interpreted to say that this kind of twitching is not as good as that kind of facial twitching.

 Q. Many are proclaiming AI to be a magic bullet, but you don’t think it’s that easy. What are some problems that you see?
 A. We have no evidence about the validity of what is being measured. Then, of course, there is the problem that algorithms are not transparent. Even the people who wrote those algorithms, there is no way I think [they] would be able to tell you what is actually under the hood.
 
 For example, a judge has to decide if [a] person likely to repeat this crime. And so people want to have a model, and the model gets fed information about where they live, where they grew up, what they've done in their past, etc., then the algorithm spits out as an assistance to the judge on [for example] what the bail should be set to. 
 
 These are places where we should worry a great deal. We should worry because it takes away from the judge the responsibility that judges have traditionally had, and it's replacing that with evidence that's coming from an algorithm we may not fully understand.

 Q. Speaking of worries, there are fears that AI-driven deepfakes can influence elections. What gives you hope?
 A. This next election will certainly see more deepfake advertisements, but I'm encouraged by the state of New Mexico, which just passed a bill a couple of weeks ago. Several of my colleagues from New Mexico were involved in writing, testifying, and so on. So we're all sort of collectively proud that New Mexico now at least has a bill.
 
 [It] says that if you use deepfakes in a political ad, you need to say you did. That to me is probably the way we should be going. Though not everybody will know that's a deepfake, at least you had to print right under it … I'm not one for imposing lots of regulation and legislation, but I do believe that at the most basic levels, we ought to be able to know how accurate the system is and what it's giving us in terms of data.

 Q. You are here to discuss how you are collaborating across disciplines to study AI. What are you probing?
 A. How fair is it, how transparent is it, and how accountable is it? We put these together in four letters called FEAT. We could call it Fairness, Equity, Accountability, and Transparency. So what I talked about in this symposium is work that I've been doing with a computer scientist and a psychologist. We now have access, really for the first time, to very large corpora of language. I mean, we now have access to all of Google Books that have been scanned in, books from the 1800s to today, billions of books that [form the] base of language, which can be a wonderful source for us to go into and say, can we treat it as if it were a mind? And now look inside that mind and see in 1800, what did people think about the Irish, and what do we think about them today?
 
 The result that I talked about is that, of course, what we believe about groups has been changing over the 200 or so years. But every word has not only a meaning, it also has an emotion attached to it. So a word like love is not just about what love means, but it is a positive thing … And similarly, there are negative words with very different meanings, but all negative. One of the things that we show is that over time, even though our beliefs about who different groups are has changed in Google Books, something that underlies these words, their effect or their emotion, has remained pretty much stable over that 200-year period.
 
 Our language is drenched with attitudes of a sort. … we're going to see not only that we dump what our minds contain into the Internet. Then, of course, that material comes back to us through podcasts and whatever else enters our mind and reinforces certain things.
 
 This is a big part of the problem with AI, that its computations can only be as good as its underlying data are.

 Q. Tying together the two themes of this conversation, what are the implicit biases of AI?
 A. So I asked GPT, I said, ‘What are your implicit biases?’ … It said something very different before that I won't go into, which was funny. But today it has been taught to say bias is a bad thing. ‘I try not to have any biases. I'm not going to tell you what I think about this or that group, but I'm based on the data of humans, so I may show some biases that reflect the biases of the data.” It has been trained to sound so politically correct that you know, that's a facade that was put in to not offend.

 Q. You're joining a conference with people from across disciplines. How do you see the future of doing research on AI in this world of inequality?
 
 A. I think that we are already on a very good path. Sometimes, fields produce something that could be dangerous. Just take physicists. They made something called an atom bomb. It was necessary given the time period we lived in. But as everybody who's watched Oppenheimer knows, the building of the bomb led to so many questions about who are we to play God, how did we do this?
 
 What are we going to do to protect ourselves? I think that in this case, while everybody is going to use it, there are people who actually build it and they come from a few different disciplines. Largely, these are people who were trained in computer science and in work on AI. What's encouraging to me is that the community is incredibly mindful about the problems of AI.
 
 So there are large numbers of people studying bias in AI. There are institutes that are cropping up whose main job is to focus on this. I mentioned to you the algorithmic justice group at the Santa Fe Institute, many of whose members were involved in working with the New Mexico State Government in order to pass this legislation. So I think we will be okay, but only if we don't let corporate interests drive how this work unfolds.
 
 If it's being unfolded with one interest, money, we're in deep trouble now.
