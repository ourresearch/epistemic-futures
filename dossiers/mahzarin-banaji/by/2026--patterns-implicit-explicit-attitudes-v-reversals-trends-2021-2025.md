CHANGE FROM 2021-2025
                                             Abstract

Between 2007-2020, implicit and explicit intergroup attitudes declined in bias steadily and were

forecasted to continue toward attitude neutrality. But, since 2020, five years of new data (2021-

2025) from 2.8 million U.S. respondents reveal that past trends have stalled or, in some cases,

even reversed. The largest reversals in bias emerged for sexuality, transgender, race and skin-

tone bias which increased by 12%-22% on implicit measures and by 24%-300% on explicit

measures. Age, disability, and body weight bias, also increased, but at slower rates. Breakpoint

analyses showed that implicit attitudes were the earlyindicators of change, reversing trend ~1

year earlier than explicit reports. For most topics, reversals were widespread across demographic

groups and even extended to an international sample of 274,884 non-U.S. respondents. However,

younger respondents who had previously shown the largest decreases in bias now showed

increases in bias, and interactions with politics and gender (e.g., young conservative men were

most likely to increase). Exploratory analyses ruled out various single-cause explanations.

Instead, the best explaination is that 2020-2021 marked a turning point in the sociopolitical

climate, whereby co-occurring existential threats from the covid-19 pandemic, amplified

economic insecurity, political sectarianism, and online toxicity likely shaped the observed

increases in intergroup bias. Together the data show that large populations of individual-level

attitudes are continually shaped by more macro, societal-level events, with the full-spectrum data

from 2007-2025 showing both reductions and increases in bias over nearly two decades.

       Keywords: Implicit attitude change, explicit attitude change, Implicit Association Test

(IAT), long-term change, timeseries analysis (ARIMA)

CHANGE FROM 2021-2025
                                      Statement of Relevance

Recently, social commentators have warned that sociopolitical conditions are re-emboldening

prejudice. Consistent with these concerns, analyses of five years (2021-2025) of implicit and

explicit measures of bias from 2.8 million U.S. respondents revealed that previous evidence of

long-standing declines in bias (2007-2020) have stalled or reversed. All seven attitudes (on

sexuality, transgender, race, skin-tone, age, disability, and body weight) showed increasing bias,

with the largest relative increases in race, skin-tone, sexuality, and transgender attitudes.

Surprisingly, younger respondents—who previously showed the largest bias reductions—now

showed the largest bias increases. After ruling out common single-cause explanations, we

suggest that this unpredicted backsliding may be explained by widespread and co-occurring

societal threats (covid, economics, democratic challenges, online toxicity) that activated and

normalized intergroup bias.

       For more than a decade, shifts in U.S. intergroup attitudes told an optimistic story. From

2007 to 2020, explicit, consciously endorsed attitudes on six topics (age, disability, body weight,

race, skin-tone, and sexuality) decreased sharply towards reduced bias (1–3). Indeed, for the first

time since data collection began, explicit race attitudes had even reached a point of neutrality in

2020, with U.S. respondents no longer reporting a preference for White over Black people.

Perhaps even more remarkable, implicit attitudes—more automatic and less controlled attitudes

that were long thought to be resistant to change (4, 5)—were also heading towards neutrality.

Data showed that implicit anti-gay bias had dropped by as much as 64% and and anti-Black bias

by 25% (1). Such decreases were notably widespread across most demographic groups, with

largely parallel trends for respondents of different gender, education, religion, and race, although

bias reduction was consistently faster among self-reported liberals and younger respondents.

Across this time period, decreasing explicit biases were also seen in representative surveys (2, 6)

underscoring that U.S. society appeared to be on a durable path towards greater egalitarianism.

       However, those same data also provided the first hint that change need not

unidirectionally head towards neutrality (1). Around 2016, implicit attitude trends for race, skin-

tone, disability, and weight bias reversed direction and increased for approximately one year

before returning again to their decreasing trends. Such temporary disruptions also provided

external validation because the specific increase for each attitude were temporally tied to a

targeted sociopolitical event (e.g., a spike in anti-disability bias temporally coincided with

Trump publicly shaming a disabled journalist) and also appeared stronger in those respondents

most likely to have tracked that event (e.g., conservative respondents in conservative states).

Nevertheless, these specific perturbations were only temporary, and statistical forecasting models

The backdrop of 2021-2025: A fraying social fabric

        Despite such predictions of continued bias reductions in bias, it is also clear that, starting

around 2020, a combination of sociopolitical events in the United States were solidifying a new

zeitgeist of intergroup bias. For instance, national polling and surveys documented that, over this

period, the U.S. experienced: peak levels of sectarianism and ideological polarization (7, 8);

health threats from the global Covid-19 pandemic (9); economic insecurity and inequality (10);

and high social media toxicity (11). Empirical evidence suggests that each of these four classes

of events, in turn, shape intergroup attitudes and behaviors. For instance, political polarization

leads political elites (from both sides) to use identity politics to reinforce party lines (12), thus

entrenching intergroup hostility. Perceived health threats from pandemics are shown to increase

outgroup avoidance (13). Existential threats of economic scarcity increase outgroup scapegoating

(14, 15). Social media algorithms normalize, even incentivize, toxic outgroup language (16, 17).

        It is therefore against this backdrop of co-occurring sociopolitical events that we re-

examine trends in implicit and explicit group attitudes between 2021-2025. Past empirical and

theoretical perspectives suggest two possible patterns. First, in line with model predictions from

past data (2007-2020), all explicit attitudes as well as implicit sexuality, race, and skin-tone

attitudes should continue to show reduction in bias (while implicit age, disability, and weight

bias should continue to remain stable). This aligns with multiple investigations showing that past

perturbations of decreasing bias are short-lived (1, 18) and even that some events (e.g., Obama’s

election) do not affect attitudes (19). After all, attitudes, and especially implicit attitudes, are

argued to reflect enduring social systems that are extremely hard and slow to change (20).

       An alternative perspective is suggested by the Bias of Crowds model (21), which

emphasizes how attitudes are deeply tuned to environmental cues and norms. In the rare cases

that the perceived norms of an environment can be wholly and rapidly changed (such as through

new legislation, elections, or elite rhetoric) attitudes should also update. Indeed, evidence shows

that strong signals of new norms, such as state and federal legalization of same-sex marriage,

preceded inflection points in attitude trends (22, 23). Importantly, this new norm must be

sufficiently strong and repeated in society to switch an attitude trajectory. For instance, same-sex

marriage legislation also coincided with grassroots movements, lesbian and gay media

representation, and changing demographics (24). While any single event might get washed away

(1), the combination of multiple co-occurring events may signal a new ecosystem that alters

attitudes in more enduring ways.

Continuous collection of implicit and explicit attitudes across time.

       To test these possibilities we bring five years of continuously-collected data (2021-2025)

from 2.8 million U.S. respondents towards seven social group topics: sexuality, race, skin-tone,

age, disability, body weight, and, newly beyond past work, transgender identities. We also

compare these recent trends to previously-published trends (2007-2020), resulting in a total

sample of 9.9 million U.S. respondents (across 2007-2025). Data were obtained from the Project

Implicit (PI) demonstration website—a platform for volunteer participants to learn about their

implicit and explicit cognition by taking demonstration Implicit Association Tests (IATs) and

explicit measures of attitudes. Although self-selection is a common concern, in supplemental

analyses (see SI Appendix) we show that all observed trends are consistent across respondents

regardless of whether they come to the website as volunteers or for work/school assignments.

       Sample demographics are reported in detail in the SI Appendix (Tables S3). On average,

across all tests and all 19 years, the modal sample was female-identifying (67% of sample),

White (71%), college-educated (80%), liberal (40%), and Christian (54%), consistent with

previous years of this data. Although sample demographics changed slightly over the 19-years

from 2007-2025 (e.g., the sample generally became older and less White; see Table S6 in the SI)

we controlled for any such sample changes through raking and weighting approaches described

in the SI. This ensures a comparable timeseries because each monthly attitude estimate comes

from the same (weighted) demographic composition.

       Implicit attitudes were measured with the Implicit Association Test (IAT; (25), the most

widely-used measure of implicit cognition, with extensive psychometric validation (26). Briefly,

the IAT compares participants’ speed of categorizing image and word stimuli together in

“congruent” blocks (e.g., Thin=good/Fat=bad) against the speed of categorizing those stimuli in

“incongruent” blocks (e.g., Fat=good/Thin=bad). The psychological assumption behind the test

is that faster sorting in congruent blocks indicates that congruent pairs (e.g., Thin=good/Fat=bad)

are more strongly and commonly associated in a participants’ mind and culture, thus manifesting

as stronger implicit biases. Although debates continue about the psychometric properties of using

the IAT as an individual difference measure, there is clear evidence that, at a population level (as

we use it here), the IAT is correlated moderately-to-strongly with meaningful outcomes and

consistently passes benchmarks for construct validity (27–30).

       Explicit attitudes were measured with a 7-point scale ranging from -3 reflecting strong

counter-cultural preferences, such as “I strongly prefer Fat people to Thin people” to +3,

reflecting strong normative-cultural preferences, such as “I strongly prefer Thin people to Fat

                                               Results

Implicit and Explicit Attitudes Have Stalled or Reversed Since 2021

       Since January 2021 every attitude trajectory, on both implicit and explicit measures, has

stalled (changed by <10%) or, in 9 out of 14 cases, descriptively increased in bias away from

neutrality. Among those increasing trends, the increases ranged from +12% to more than tripling

in the magnitude of bias since 2021 (Table 1; Figure 1, data after the dashed lines). These trends

challenge forecasts of what should have happened from 2021-2025 if past attitude trends (from

2007-2020) had continued uninterrupted. For instance, based on 14 years of previous data,

sexuality attitudes were forecasted to continue decreasing by 63% on implicit measures and by

64% on explicit measures. However, the true observed data from 2021-2025 show that sexuality

bias increased by 22% on implicit measures and more than doubled on explicit measures over the

five years of new data. Similarly, implicit race attitudes were forecasted to drop in bias by 16%

and but have, in fact, since 2021 have increased by 15%, with even larger relative increases of

53% on explicit attitudes. Even those attitudes that were relatively stable have shown descriptive

increases in bias. The only exception is explicit age attitudes which have continued to decrease

slightly towards neutrality (as forecasted).

       These results are echoed in findings from Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average

(ARIMA) timeseries models (see Methods and SI) fit to the 60 months (5 years) of new data

from 2021-2025. Model predictions show that the predicted time for future trends to double in

bias is closer than the time for those future trends to reach neutral. As one example, if the trends

Long-run Trends Still Reveal Overall Decreases in Bias

         The trends reported so-far—of stalled or increasing bias from 2021-2025—may be

concerning, especially for the U.S. culture where most of the population still explicitly values

equality and diversity (31). But the current data also suggest some optimism. Combining recent

years with previous data, long-run trends still reveal meaningful change was achieved since 2007

(Table 2). Over the 19-year timespan, explicit attitudes decreased in bias by 85% (sexuality),

106% (race), and 69% (skin-tone). Body-related topics of age, disability, and weight attitudes

changed too, but at about half the rate, dropping in bias by 37%, 47%, and 30%, respectively.

         Implicit attitudes also showed the potential for long-term change, decreasing in bias since

2007 by 68% (sexuality), 17% (race), and 14% (skin-tone). Still, implicit age and disability

       Critically, 2020/2021 reflected a true structural breakpoint in attitude trends. Structural

change analyses (see Methods, SI) showed that five implicit attitudes (out of six trends,

excluding transgender attitudes due to their shorter timeseries) consistently revealed a final

breakpoint starting around April-August 2020 (SI). Only implicit age attitudes showed an earlier

breakpoint in December 2019. Interestingly, results also showed that breakpoints for implicit

attitudes preceded those for explicit attitudes: explicit race, skin-tone, disability, and body-

weight attitudes reversed about a year after their implicit attitudes, generally starting to reverse in

April-December 2021 (age attitudes again showed no breakpoint discovered in the last 4 years;

Table S25). This ordering—where implicit precedes explicit attitude reversals—was also robust

across demographics (e.g., among liberals and conservatives; Table S26). Such results align with

the emerging perspective that implicit attitudes may be more attuned to cultural phenomena (21)

and therefore may be more sensitive, early detectors of social change. The only attitude that did

not fit the pattern of implicit attitude change leading explicit attitude change were anti-gay/pro-

straight attitudes. Instead, these attitudes showed nearly simultaneous change on both implicit

and explicit attitudes, reversing course within one month of each other.

Recent Trends Have Increased Consistently Across (Most) Demographics

       Examining differences or similarities across demographic groups sheds light on possible

sources of change. If systematic group differences emerge it suggests change likely comes from

events and rhetoric targeting specific groups/places. If groups move in consistent ways, however,

and voting patterns of young conservative men, in particular, are polarizing away from other age-

increased in bias more than older respondents is true of only some specific intersectional

Exploring Correlates of Attitude Reversals

       Prejudice spillover. In our last set of analyses, we explore possible correlates of attitude

reversals. We focus on a case study of sexuality attitudes, which revealed the largest deviations

from past forecasted trends. First, some have argued that anti-gay attitudes may have resurged as

a consequence of being associated with transgender people, who have been particular targets of

legislation, political campaigning, and hate crimes, especially among the political right.

Empirically, if there is such a “spillover effect” we would expect a temporally-lagged

relationship, such that anti-gay attitudes spike only after anti-transgender prejudice spikes.

       Across some (but not all) model specifications of Granger causality (see SI), we found

initial evidence that changes in conservatives’ explicit transgender attitudes preceded changes in

explicit sexuality attitudes at lags of 2-6 months, Fs > 2.42, ps < .02, but never in the reverse

directions, all ps > .38 (consistent with a Granger-causal explanation). Additionally, conservative

states that increased more in explicit anti-transgender attitudes also increased more in explicit

anti-gay attitudes across model choices, rs > .36, ps < .03, implying that change in these two

attitudes is robustly linked for this demographic. However, such lagged relationships did not

emerge for liberals, nor consistently for the overall sample, nor for implicit attitudes (see SI; e.g.,

correlations of percent changes were rs < .23, ps >.11). Thus, if there is any evidence of a

spillover effect it is only for explicit, conscious bias and uniquely for the group that supports

most of the anti-transgender legislation.

       Targeted Moral Rhetoric. Since rises in sexuality bias is not coming (entirely) from

spillover of another attitude topic, we next explored more sexuality-bias specific explanations.

Recently, the rhetoric and moral panic of “sexual grooming” has sought to legitimize anti-gay

bias by claiming that older gay people are “grooming” young people to become gay themselves

(34). Indeed, we found that increases in Google Searches for “sexual grooming” icoincided with

the breakpoint in sexuality attitudes (see SI) but we found no consistent evidence of any

temporally-lagged relationships nor any spatio-temporal relationships between this targeted

rhetoric and rising anti-gay bias. Thus, specific rhetoric is also unlikely to be a single cause.

       Generalized Twitter Negativity. Perhaps targeted rhetoric is too specific and recent

increases instead reflect a broader change in social media representations of gay people. Using

an existing sample of 9.7 million Twitter/X posts collected across 2013-2023 from ~12,000

politically-active Twitter users (35) we fit monthly word2vec word embeddings models (which

have been previously validated to show correspondence with human attitude-like representations

(36)). Then, we examined the relative positivity/negativity of the top traits associated with gay

versus straight over the span from 2016-2022. This Twitter data should be interpreted with

caution since the actual frequency of group references was low (which could harm the specificity

of results; see SI). Nevertheless, suggestive results appeared in line with expectations that

negative online rhetoric could precede attitude spikes: for explicit sexuality attitudes a rise in

anti-gay/pro-straight sentiment on Twitter preceded a subsequent rise in explicit anti-gay

attitudes, significantly at lags of 1 month, F(1, 79) = 5.28, p = .025, and 2 months, F(2, 78) =

3.25, p = .044, while the reverse lagged direction was never significant, p > .13. Implicit attitudes

also showed suggestive evidence (but only at marginal significance) whereby a spike in negative

Twitter sentiment preceded negative attitudes; with no evidence for the reverse direction (see SI).

       Taking all results together we have (1) usually null effects for specific targeted rhetoric or

targeted spillover, but (2) suggestive evidence of more general online sentiment preceding

attitudes. Moreover, we have (3) widespread reversals across all tests, and (4) similar trends

across most demographics and places. Thus, single-cause, targeted explanations appear unlikely.

                                        General Discussion


       For nearly a decade, surveys of long-term attitude change showed hopeful evidence of

steadily declining biases in the U.S. and internationally, especially for some highly-discussed

topics like race and sexuality (1, 37, 38). The current data of attitudes measured 2021-2025

complicate that picture. Instead of expected declines in bias, attitudes measured since 2021 have

stalled or reversed in their movement towards neutrality for all topics on both implicit and

explicit attitudes. For example, forecasts from past data predicted that implicit sexuality attitudes

should have decreased by as much 63% over this period but, in fact, they increased by 22%.

       Without such data, many people could have assumed that “all is well,” that attitudes

remained on track toward equality. When people assume continued decreases in prejudice, they

stop being concerned about maintaining or furthering those changes (39). Thus, by showing that

change halted and, in some cases, even reversed, these data help motivate researchers and the

public to act towards renewed efforts for attitude change—efforts that align with widely-shared

values of the importance of diversity and equality (31).

Proposing (and ruling out) sources of widespread increasing bias.

       Reversals in attitude trends are not without precedent. In previous analyses (1), we have

reported reversals in attitudes around 2016 tied to specific events targeting a specific group (e.g.,


                                                                                                    23
CHANGE FROM 2021-2025
the shaming of a disabled journalist affected disability attitudes). But, within a year, these

reversals had halted and attitude trends were returning to previous trends of decreasing bias.

       The reversals observed in the current data starting in 2021 are not of this type. For one,

the increases are not isolated to one topic but are widespread across every topic, across both

implicit and explicit measures, and across nearly every demographic group we examined. For

another, the current reversals are longer lasting, with breakpoints detected in mid-2020 and mid-

2021 suggesting enduring new slopes that do not appear to be turning around even after five

years of data. Such endurance and spread underscore that no one targeted event (e.g., one piece

of legislation, one election) is likely the source of recent change. Direct tests of common

hypotheses about rising sexuality biases (i.e., prejudice spillover, moral panic rhetoric) showed

no strong consistent evidence of causal, lagged relationships for the overall sample. Although

more general online sentiment (from a sample of political Twitter users) showed suggestive

evidence that wider online sentiment could precede subsequent spikes in attitudes.

       We propose that observed increases in bias reflect a complex process whereby a

combination of destabilizing events activated realistic and symbolic threats that were translated

into intergroup hostility. As noted above, four classes of threatening events coincided around

2020-2021 (the timing of our identified breakpoints), each widely established to be particularly

tied to intergroup bias: health threats from the COVID pandemic; economic insecurity (including

that spurred by the high job loss during the pandemic); online toxicity (especially likely to be

encountered when most jobs and education were forced online); and unprecedented political

sectarianism including challenges to electoral processes (40). This last point also helps explain

why reversals may have happened even after the Biden election in 2020. Although Biden, as a

Democrat, might have been expected to symbolize and normalize liberal values that improved


                                                                                                  24
CHANGE FROM 2021-2025
intergroup relations, the 2020 election was vocally challenged by elite political leaders,

fracturing civil discourse and public trust, perhaps especially in liberal systems.

Strongest rises in bias among younger people.

       Finally, this speculative perspective—that recent change reflect responses to realistic and

symbolic threats manifesting as intergroup bias—not only helps us explain the widespread

reversals across topics and most demographics but also the unique finding that younger

respondents consistently increased faster than older respondents. This result is initially surprising

because younger respondents are typically at the vanguard of egalitarian progress (1). However,

young people in the U.S. today express the strongest existential concerns about their future

economic, environmental, and political situation (41). These existential concerns are funneled

towards intergroup hostility especially in young-dominated (and male, conservative dominated)

“manosphere” and “red pill” online communities that weaponize anti-LGBTQ, anti-Black, and

general toxic discourse (42, 43). The current data suggest such ideologies, even if started in

online sub-populations, could spread to entire populations of young people.

       Of course, young people may also be changing faster towards greater bias because they

are particularly attuned to social norms and peer influence (44). Related work identifies young

people as early adopters of new trends (45), but we show this may also be the case for intergroup

attitudes. Young people may both be the fastest to change towards neutrality (when egalitarian

norms dominate) but also the fastest to reverse towards hostility (when anti-egalitarian norms

emerge).

Limitations and future directions.

   Causal inference is notoriously difficult in observational data. While we speculate that the

observed reversals stem from co-occurring destabilizing events, future work should attempt to


                                                                                                    25
CHANGE FROM 2021-2025
test this explanation by isolating causal variables of events (e.g., COVID, election challenges),

and existential concerns (e.g., about health, economics). Difference-in-difference approaches, for

instance, could examine how local county-level variation in the timing and co-occurrence of

covid-19 threats and online rhetoric amplification could explain variation in the timing and

degree of attitude reversals. Given the scale of analyses already presented, we leave these

important directions to future research.

   Our reliance on Project Implicit data, which is large but non-representative, also limits claims

about representative population attitudes. We note, however, that current findings align with

representative surveys (2), and supplemental analyses ruled out deflationary concerns of sample

change, repeat test-takers, or cohort replacement. Nevertheless, Project Implicit data and

representative polls under-represent extremist groups, despite these groups disproportionately

shaping perceived norms (46). Future work identifying attitudes of more extremist groups using

naturalistic text from alt-right or extremist sources (e.g., Gab, 4chan) will help uncover whether

these groups may be early signals of attitude reversals.

Conclusion.

       Even after more than a decade of declining bias, the current data shows that intergroup

attitudes can still stall or, in some cases, even reverse course and increase in bias. In the U.S.—a

culture that still explicitly and widely endorses values of “diversity” and “inclusion” (47)—these

trends are concerning. We speculate that reversals likely arose from threats, beginning around

2020, that eroded the societal fabric of the U.S. and became translated into intergroup hostility.

While this bias spread across all attitude topics and nearly every demographic group, the effects

were amplified among young people, conservatives, and men—groups most affected by

existential concerns and most exposed to toxic rhetoric. Ultimately, as anti-democratic and anti-

egalitarian threats continue to unfold in U.S. society, it remains critical to track implicit and

explicit attitudes so that we can better quantify, understand, and intervene on the progress or

devolution of intergroup harmony.


Materials and methods.

       Data Sources. Our primary data were obtained from the open data from the Project

Implicit (PI) demonstration website at https://osf.io/px8h3/. Data was excluded from participants

if: (1) their IAT D scores fell outside of the conditions in the revised scoring algorithm (48); (2)

they did not have complete explicit measures or demographic information on age, gender, race,

political ideology, and education; or, for our primary sample, (3) they were not residents of the

United States. An additional sample of respondents on the sexuality and race tasks who had

available IAT D scores and demographic information but reported non-U.S. residency were also

included to examine international trends.

       For exploratory supplemental analyses, covariate data was obtained on (1) voting patterns

(votes for Trump vs. Biden in the 2020 federal election); (2) Google searches for the moral panic

related to “sexual grooming” (34), downloaded from Google Trends, using “sexual grooming” as

a topic search and limiting to searches from January 2007 – December 2025; and (3) Twitter data

from Puryear and colleagues (35) with details outlined in the SI.


       Primary timeseries modeling: Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA)

models. We follow past work (1, 37) in using Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average

(ARIMA) models (49, 50) to estimate past and future trends that we can compare against

continued data collection. All ARIMA models were estimated using an automated approach

through the auto.arima() function implemented in the forecast package (version 8.24.0) in the R


                                                                                                    27
CHANGE FROM 2021-2025
computing environment (version 4.4.1). Briefly, ARIMA models work by separating the

underlying timeseries process into seasonal (i.e., repeated yearly patterns) and non-seasonal

components, as well as a possible slope (or “drift”) capturing any constant linear trend in the

data. For each seasonal/non-seasonal component, ARIMA models first ensure that the timeseries

process is stationary (i.e., the process itself is not changing or drifting over time) by differencing

the series. Next, the ARIMA model tests whether the (differenced) process is best captured by

moving average processes (i.e., experiencing “shocks” in the system) and/or by autoregressive

processes (i.e., reliable lagged relationships). This results in an ARIMA model with up to seven

parameters (p,d,q)(P,D,Q)+drift, capturing the autoregressive (p), differencing (d) and moving

average (q) parameters first for the non-seasonal, then the seasonal process, and finally any

slope.

         The number of ARIMA parameters indicate how many of these autoregressive,

differencing or moving average parameters are needed to fully capture the observed timeseries

process. However, the exact order and values of these parameters are typically not the main

interpretation. Instead, ARIMA models are more commonly interpreted by focusing on: (1) the

forecasts of future trends, based on the ARIMA models description of past trends; and (2)

summaries of both the raw and percent change from decomposed trends after removing the

seasonal and noise components of the timeseries process. In the text, we focus on these more

descriptive outcomes.

         Secondary analyses: Breakpoint detection. In addition to describing overall trends using

ARIMA models, we also explored when a structural change occurred in the most recent data. We

performed exploratory breakpoint detection to estimate possible inflection points in otherwise

continuous trends. We perform breakpoint detection using an automated approach implemented


                                                                                                     28
CHANGE FROM 2021-2025
in the breakpoints() function in the strucchange package (version 1.5.4 in R). Briefly, this

automated approach successively estimates various segmented regressions and determines

whether (and when) a new segment is needed because it has a significantly different slope from a

prior segment. We identified breakpoints for the overall series of implicit and explicit attitudes

(from 2007-2025), as well as for subsets of liberal and conservatives’ trajectories.

       Secondary analyses: Demographic differences. For demographic differences, we

compared trajectories across all groups with sufficient data across tasks and years. Specifically,

we compared six demographic categories: (1) politics (liberal, conservative); (2) age (< 20-year-

olds, >40-year-olds); (3) gender (men, women); (4) race (White, Black, Asian); (5) education (no

college, college); and (6) religion (Christian, Jewish, Non-religious, Other religion). We

compared the trends and ARIMA models/forecasts across all pairs of subgroups to identify

whether groups were moving in parallel, converging, or diverging in their trends.

       For pre-processing demographic data, we also performed a revised weighting approach to

control for the possibility that two demographic groups (e.g., women, men) could change in

different ways over time (e.g., women could become more liberal, men could become more

conservative). We therefore first subset the data along each demographic difference (e.g.,

women, men) and then reweighted each of these subsets to match the same target weights from

the overall population. This approach also ensures that the two demographic samples (e.g., men

and women) are otherwise matched on other demographics (e.g., race, age), ensuring that we are

focusing only on the demographic difference of interest.

       Secondary analyses: Geographic differences and Granger causality. We first subset the

data according to U.S. state and then computed the weighted mean (using the target weights of

the full sample population) for each state in each year. We used cut-offs of at least 50


                                                                                                      29
CHANGE FROM 2021-2025
observations per year (i.e., any state had to have at least 50 observations in a year to be included

as a mean in the final data). Additionally, any trajectory that was missing more than 25% of its

data (due to not passing the cut-offs) was excluded from analysis. We also recomputed

trajectories for liberals and conservatives in each state separately to investigate the interaction

between participant demographics and place.

        After computing U.S. state-level variation, we explored correlational analyses with

various single-cause explanations: (1) the correlation between transgender and sexuality attitudes

(reflecting “prejudice spillover”); (2) correlations with moral panic rhetoric (about “sexual

grooming”); and (3) correlations with the representation and negativity of gay on Twitter. First,

we examined Pearson’s correlations between attitudes and the correlate (e.g., rates of “sexual

grooming searches”) within the same state/time to test if these variables were, as expected,

correlated with the attitude.

        Second, and more critical, we tested lagged relationships using Granger causality with the

grangertest() function in the lmtest package (version 0.9.40) in R. Granger models test whether

the monthly rises/falls in a given timeseries (e.g., sexuality attitudes) are better explained when

we include a lagged second timeseries (e.g., monthly rises/falls in, for example, Google searches

for the previous month). We compare effect sizes and significance of Granger causality in a

forward direction (e.g., Google searches preceded sexuality attitudes) versus a backward

direction (e.g., sexuality attitudes preceded Google searches) at lags of 1-6 months. If only one

direction (e.g., forward) is significant, then this was interpreted as evidence for Granger

causality. If both directions are significant, then a third variable causes rises/falls in both series,

if neither direction is significant then the two variables are not likely to be temporally related to

each other. All results are reported in detail in the SI.

       Robustness checks and ruling out alternatives. As elaborated in the SI, we also ruled out

four common critiques. First, sample change, which we addressed through re-weighting the

sample over time. Second, regression to the mean and practice effects, which we addressed by

inspecting trends from only novice responders who have not taken an IAT before. Third, self-

selection, which we addressed by computing trends only for those who came to the website for

that increases in bias also occurred within birth cohorts, underscoring that change is not entirely
