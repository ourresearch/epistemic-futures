# Andrew Pettegree and Arthur der Weduwen. The Bookshop of the World: Making and Trading Books in the Dutch Golden Age.

## Full text

If one wanted to see the culture of print in early modern Europe at its apogee, everyone agreed, he or she should go to the Netherlands. Dutch booksellers and printers were the most successful of the age. They prospered as few of their counterparts in other countries did, dominating the continental trade, while the citizenry they courted in their own nation spent their lives immersed in a welter of printed paper to an extent that their peers in France or Italy could rarely match. English-language historical scholarship has long acknowledged all this in principle. But it has not grappled with the phenomenon seriously, tending to rest content with generalizations about Dutch enterprise. Andrew Pettegree’s and Arthur der Weduwen’s The Bookshop of the World not only remedies this embarrassing situation but does so with a brilliantly original analysis that sets new standards of quality and ambition for the field in general.

In part, Pettegree and der Weduwen succeed by taking the apparently simple step of treating the printing and bookselling trades as emerging simultaneously with Dutch society itself. The Bookshop of the World begins not with the invention of printing or the shakeout of the European book trade in 1500 but with the sieges of Leiden and Antwerp (1572–74). These events made possible the subsequent ascendancy of the Dutch trade by securing the existence of a composite political nation and driving into its towns an enterprising population alienated from the Catholic region to the south. The result was a small, embattled, and diverse nation of proudly autonomous towns linked by canals. It had both the best internal connections and the most variegated culture of any early modern polity. In this setting, what sometimes seemed to contemporaries to be weaknesses—intractable religious diversity, civic pride hindering press regulation, the lack of a strong monarchy—were sources of strength, at least to the printers. The history of print, on this account, is that of the interconnected diversity of the Netherlands.

As this implies, Pettegree and der Weduwen largely ignore the kinds of questions that preoccupied earlier scholars. Rather than worrying about whether the press was responsible for modernization, they focus their attention on what the press did produce. Of paramount concern to printers was the tricky matter of keeping their enterprises going at all. Printing houses were economically fragile endeavors, and this was true of those in the Netherlands too. Although history has lauded publishers like Joan Blaeu for their grand contributions to scholarship, works like his Atlas Maior were anything but representative. Dutch houses prospered through work that was small scale and risk averse. University dissertations were a mainstay, for example, valuable to the trade because graduating students had to pay up front for them. “While the Elzeviers made their reputation printing Galileo,” the authors say, “they made their profits printing theses” (192). Contrary to received opinion, the trade was not particularly devoted to innovative books. It largely left cutting-edge science, medicine, and law to houses in Basel and elsewhere. Those fields were simply too risky.

In effect, our archives lie to us. The trade in fact comprised a range of products that have vanished from view almost completely. Small-format works of devotion, for example, sold perennially. Even better were municipal documents, such as ordinances, because these were paid for in advance: over half the output of the Dutch press, we find, was not commercially published at all. We can know that these objects existed and that they were produced by the million, as they left traces in ledgers and stock catalogues. And we can occasionally catch a glimpse of one that by some chance has survived. But they were meant to be ephemeral, and ephemeral they were. These were the dark matter of the print universe, as it were: undetectable now but vital to that universe’s very existence.

In consequence, Dutch printers and booksellers were notably inactive in several areas that we might associate with modernizing processes, including capitalism itself. Business information, like experimental scientific results, continued to be conveyed by correspondence and word of mouth. When things were really serious, it often remained better to trust a known correspondent than a dodgy newsletter printed by some unscrupulous artisan. And even though the trade did produce a flourishing news market, Dutch newspapers generally remained anodyne and devoted to foreign matters. Most “news” that might actually have been useful to their readers was printed in the form of posters tacked up at key locations by official “public affixers.” Why, then, did people buy newspapers at all? For social reasons, probably. They gave citizens the ability to participate in a lively social ecology as well-informed conversationalists.

Caution was also the watchword when it came to the trade’s international role. Despite their reputation, it seems that the Dutch did not publish many books for a European market; the Elzeviers’ pocket editions of the classics were the major exceptions, and even these were originally intended for domestic students. Instead, the Dutch invented a reexport trade. Secure in their local markets, they had the wherewithal to go to the Frankfurt Book Fair and drive hard bargains. They returned with cartloads of books printed in the great European centers, bought cheaply enough that they could be resold for profit. Printed stock catalogues advertised these wares to dealers in Paris, London, Copenhagen, and elsewhere. “The engine of the Dutch miracle,” Pettegree and der Weduwen argue, “was the stealthy appropriation of the market in everyone else’s books.”

These insights—and many more could be cited—make The Bookshop of the World a landmark work in the history of the book. It transforms our understanding of the Dutch trade, thereby implicitly challenging much that we routinely assert about print’s historical importance. And its integration of politics, religion, social life, and even environment makes it a model for scholars to emulate. They will have a hard job matching it.
