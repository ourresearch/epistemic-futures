# We’re officially launching our database today. Here’s what you need to know.

## Full text

<span style="font-weight: 400;">[](https://retractionwatch.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/01/rw-twitter-1.jpg)Readers, this is a big day for us.</span>

<span style="font-weight: 400;">We’re officially launching the </span>[<span style="font-weight: 400;">Retraction Watch Database</span>](http://retractiondatabase.org/)<span style="font-weight: 400;"> of more than 18,000 retractions, along with a [six-page package of stories and infographics](https://www.sciencemag.org/news/2018/10/what-massive-database-retracted-papers-reveals-about-science-publishing-s-death-penalty) based on it that we developed with our partners at Science Magazine. In that package, you’ll learn about trends — some surprising, some perhaps not — and other tidbits such as which countries have the highest retraction rates. Thanks as always to our partners at Science, particularly Jeffrey Brainard and Jia You, who crunched the numbers and developed the package.</span>

<span style="font-weight: 400;">As readers no doubt know, we’ve been working on the database for some years. Some have asked us why it has taken so long — can’t we just pull retractions from existing databases like PubMed, or publishers’ sites? The answer is resoundingly no. All of those databases are missing retractions, whether by design or because notices aren’t transmitted well. That’s why we found more than 18,000, far more than you’ll find elsewhere. And we also went through each one and assigned it a reason, based on a detailed taxonomy we developed over eight years of reporting on retractions.</span>

<span style="font-weight: 400;">Now that you know how much work the database is, please consider thanking Alison Abritis, our researcher, who has done the lion’s share of the work on this project. She had some help — see below — but without Alison’s expertise and painstaking efforts, we wouldn’t have a database. And true to form, Alison has created an exhaustive </span>[<span style="font-weight: 400;">user’s guide</span>](https://retractionwatch.com/retraction-watch-database-user-guide/)<span style="font-weight: 400;"> that we would strongly urge you to review if you’re planning to use the database for anything other than simple searches.</span>

And if you find a retraction that’s not yet in our database, please let us know by submitting it [here](https://docs.google.com/forms/d/e/1FAIpQLSeAsw4i5J8M7sOQ9GiG0_dglkim9gdPPba92yZRLfCq4u-o7w/viewform?c=0&w=1).

<span style="font-weight: 400;">To answer one frequently asked question: Yes, we plan an API. But that will take </span>[<span style="font-weight: 400;">additional resources, as will keeping the database up to date</span>](https://retractionwatch.com/2018/10/12/retraction-watch-readers-we-need-your-help-to-be-able-to-continue-our-work-2/)<span style="font-weight: 400;">. In the meantime, if you’re a scholar who has a research project that you’d like to use the data for, contact us at </span>[<span style="font-weight: 400;">team@retractionwatch.com</span>](mailto:team@retractionwatch.com)<span style="font-weight: 400;">. </span>

<span style="font-weight: 400;">There are some — make that a lot — of other people to thank. With apologies to those we may miss:</span>

- <span style="font-weight: 400;">The John D. and Catherine T. MacArthur Foundation and the Laura and John Arnold Foundation funded this work directly. Back in 2014, when Retraction Watch was just a four-year-old blog by two journalists with day jobs, it was the foresight of these two foundations that gave us the idea to create The Center For Scientific Integrity. Just as important were the generous grants which they have provided us to do this work.</span>
- <span style="font-weight: 400;">The Leona B. and Harry M. Helmsley Trust, whose support has allowed us to create a strategic plan for The Center, including the database, and to continue our other work. </span>
- <span style="font-weight: 400;">Many — at this point, hundreds — of individual donors who have kept us going over the years. We’re deeply grateful.</span>
- <span style="font-weight: 400;">Tanya Rybak, a star developer who coded the database for us: tkrybak@yahoo.com</span>
- <span style="font-weight: 400;">A small army of students, librarians and others who entered thousands of retractions by hand.</span>
- <span style="font-weight: 400;">Jason Rollins, formerly of Clarivate Analytics, who helped us think through the structure and taxonomy of our database, and how it might be integrated with other resources.</span>
- <span style="font-weight: 400;">Brooke Rosenzweig, formerly of the Helmsley Trust, who worked with us on sustainability plans for the database.</span>

<span style="font-weight: 400;">And of course, you, dear readers, without which none of this would have been possible. Your support, encouragement, story tips and more have kept us going.</span>

<span style="font-weight: 400;">While this is, as we’ve noted, a big day for us, our work continues. The database is updated every day, and our reporting and writing also go on.</span>

<span style="font-weight: 400;">Now go check out the [package of stories in Science](https://www.sciencemag.org/news/2018/10/what-massive-database-retracted-papers-reveals-about-science-publishing-s-death-penalty), and the [database](http://retractiondatabase.org).</span>
