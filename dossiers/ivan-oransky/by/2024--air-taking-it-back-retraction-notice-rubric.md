# Taking it back: A pilot study of a rubric measuring retraction notice quality

## Full text

The frequency of scientific retractions has grown substantially in recent years. However, thus far there is no standardized retraction notice format to which journals and their publishers adhere voluntarily, let alone compulsorily. We developed a rubric specifying seven criteria in order to judge whether retraction notices are easily and freely accessible, informative, and transparent. We mined the Retraction Watch database and evaluated a total of 768 retraction notices from two publishers (Springer and Wiley) over three years (2010, 2015, and 2020). Per our rubric, both publishers tended to score higher on measures of openness/availability, accessibility, and clarity as to why a paper was retracted than they did in: acknowledging institutional investigations; confirming whether there was consensus among authors; and specifying which parts of any given paper warranted retraction. Springer retraction notices appeared to improve over time with respect to the rubric’s seven criteria. We observed some discrepancies among raters, indicating the difficulty in developing a robust objective rubric for evaluating retraction notices.

In the scientific literature, retractions are written notices documenting the withdrawal or removal of a published journal article. Papers can be retracted by their own authors or by their respective journal and/or publisher for any number of reasons, many of which have been enumerated by the Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE) (Council Citation 2019 ). These include: substantive errors; suspected plagiarism; falsified data; conflicts of interest; and/or other ethical, methodological, or experimental lapses (Barbour et al. Citation 2009 ).

The first recorded retractions date back at least to the 1700s, when Benjamin Wilson wrote to The Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of his concerns over Benjamin Franklin’s work on electricity and called for a retraction of his own (Wilson’s) work (Wilson Citation 1755 ). However, journals did not begin actively publishing retractions until the 1970s, when peer review became a more prominent element of scientific evaluation (McCook Citation 2016 ). Like peer review, retractions are intended to add a layer of quality control to academic literature: readers should be able to assume that peer-reviewed published information is truthful and trustworthy insofar as the authors know (Fang and Casadevall Citation 2011 ).

From 2001 to 2010, the number of published retractions grew by more than 16-fold, while over the same period the number of published papers grew by less than 1.5-fold (He Citation 2013 ). Though some observers have worried that an increasing number of retractions must equate to increasing misconduct, others have argued that, if anything, retractions should be more frequent today, as journals’ and their publishers’ oversight procedures are now more clearly defined (Fanelli Citation 2013 ). For example, citing one’s own work without a citation and independent publication of a single paper in multiple languages are now viewed as unacceptable, while in the past these were common practices (McCook Citation 2016 ). Additionally, as more papers have become available on the internet (albeit often behind a paywall), it may be easier to detect plagiarism or duplicate submissions, which, together, have accounted for as much as 40% of retractions in the last 15 years (Brainard Citation 2018 ; He Citation 2013 ). While the increasing rate of retraction of unreliable research is undoubtedly good for research, it is likely that we need even more retractions if we are to achieve robust self-correction in empirical research (Oransky Citation 2022 ).

As we scrutinize the growing body of retractions more deeply, we will inevitably elicit more questions about how retraction notices are composed, published, formatted, and shared. In particular, retraction notices lack uniformity and standardization among publishers, which can confuse readers (S. B. Xu and Hu Citation 2023 ). Retraction notices may be formatted and written completely differently by different publishers. This can be due to differences in templates used to produce retractions on a large scale, in changing editorial styles, and/or in author involvement, among other reasons. Another likely simple explanation is journal preference; some large society journals may have their own separate branding, workflows, and/or article templates, which might well be distinct from other journals from the same publisher. Perceptions of the crucial elements in retraction notices have changed over the years. Expectations have risen, as evidenced by scholars calling for retractions to share certain key pieces of information, regardless of journal publisher. Quan-Hoang Vuong, the Director of the Centre for Interdisciplinary Social Research at Phenikaa University in Hanoi, for example, has argued that retractions should: disclose who initiated the retraction; include information about post-publication review; identify the cause of the retraction; and indicate whether there is agreement about whether to retract among editors and authors (Vuong Citation 2020 ). This four-component retraction requirement would likely be difficult to implement because retraction formats are determined independently by publishers, who are encouraged to follow but not legally bound by best-practice general guidelines from COPE (which itself includes primarily journal editors and publishers’ representatives [ https://publicationethics.org/about/governance ]). As things stand, publishers lack incentives to disclose the inner workings of failed peer review processes and the like. Indeed, the same criticism regarding incentive structures could be leveled at the rubric we present here. Nor can authors necessarily solve this problem; while retractions can be initiated by readers, authors, or editors, actual authorship of retraction notices is often opaque and the final word rests with the journal/publisher (Chen and Fu Citation 2023 ).

Retractions should highlight not only the cause of a retraction and the interaction between the editors and authors, but the larger impact a retracted paper might have on subsequent research and the original authors (Oransky Citation 2015 ); such impacts have implications for downstream research, e.g., the ongoing citation of retracted work can diminish the validity of a field and perhaps even pose a harm to patients (Marcus, Abritis, and Oransky Citation 2022 ; Wang et al. Citation 2023 ; Williams and Wager Citation 2013 ). Retractions can also be improved by documenting whether there are any legal or other sanctions regarding the retraction (Oransky Citation 2015 ; S. B. Xu et al. Citation 2023 ). While there are, as of yet, no mandatory standardized criteria for retraction notices, there is some evidence that, since the publication of the COPE guidelines in 2009, retraction notices are likely to include more information (Decullier et al. Citation 2013 ; S. B. Xu et al. Citation 2023 ), although this trend is by no means universal (Balhara and Mishra Citation 2014 ; Grey, Avenell, and Bolland Citation 2022 ).

The increase in and evolution of retractions were the impetus for this paper, in which we attempt to answer two primary research questions: 1) Are retractions different between two particular publishers, and if so, how?; and 2) How did those retractions change over the decade spanning 2010–2020 based on a three-year sample? These questions provided the opportunity for our team to develop and pilot a rubric to evaluate retraction notice completeness and quality.

This study utilized data from the Retraction Watch database (retractiondatabase.org) to evaluate the quality of retraction notices published in 2010, 2015, and 2020. One of us (AA), a researcher at Retraction Watch, identified a total of 768 retractions across two journal publishers, Springer (now Springer Nature) (Anonymous Citation 2015 ) and John Wiley & Sons (“Wiley”) from the three target years. These two journal publishers were chosen because of their large size and wide range of scientific journals.

We note that within the initial Wiley data set there were 158 Cochrane Reviews entries that were withdrawn. Cochrane Reviews are shorter meta-analysis papers that evaluate existing research findings that meet specified criteria for validity and relevance (Higgins et al. Citation 2019 ; Anonymous Citation n.d. ). Such research is used to answer a particular research or clinical question (e.g., Does wearing masks reduce the spread of SARS-CoV-2?). Cochrane Reviews were outliers in our dataset because such reviews are oftentimes retracted due to new research that renders an existing review outdated if not obsolete. In other words, the majority of Cochrane retractions are regular updates (although some may occasionally be withdrawn for other reasons such as conflict-of-interest concerns among review authors) (Higgins et al. Citation 2019 ). Nonetheless, because the majority of Cochrane reviews are not retractions per se, these entries were removed from the Wiley dataset.

Retractions from each of the two publishers were divided into 2010, 2015, and 2020, with each year split into thirds. The six primary researchers (AS, BB, CP, LS, DW, AW) broke off into three teams of two (AS-AW; BB-DW; CP-LS), and each pair evaluated one-third of the retraction notices. Each member of each pair independently evaluated each notice in their group, so that each of the 768 retraction notices was evaluated twice (prior to the removal of the Cochrane entries).

A rubric containing a series of questions was used to evaluate each retraction notice’s quality based on a scale from 0 to 2. The rubric questions were developed collaboratively by all members of the research team, synthesizing criteria from COPE guidelines (Barbour et al. Citation 2009 ; Council Citation 2019 ) and a 2015 Retraction Watch article (Oransky Citation 2015 ) outlining the optimal components of a retraction notice. Points allocated to individual questions were averaged between each pair of researchers (AS-AW; BB-DW; CP-LS) who evaluated each retraction. This approach was adopted to minimize researcher bias.

Finally, the data were analyzed in R Studio, primarily by using the tidyverse, gtsummary, and flextable packages (R version 4.2.2, 2022). First, the data were grouped by doi and the rubric ratings were averaged. For both Springer and Wiley retractions, the average score for each rubric item was then calculated for the years 2010, 2015, and 2020. A p-value is reported for each rubric item using analysis of variance.

As seen in Table 2 , at a high level, Springer retractions appeared to improve over time from 2010 to 2020, per our pre-specified criteria. The metrics with the highest average ratings for Springer retraction notices included openness/availability, accessibility, and identification/candidness, while the metric with the lowest overall ratings was acknowledgment of institutional investigations. All rubric items had a p-value less than 0.05, indicating at least one year with a statistically significant difference in average rating. By contrast, in an admittedly smaller dataset ( n = 320) due to the removal of Cochrane withdrawal notices, Wiley retractions did not necessarily appear to improve over time, with some categories decreasing in ratings over the three years ( Table 3 ). For example, identification/candidness decreased from an average rating of 1.94 in 2010 to 1.75 in 2020; acknowledgment of investigations decreased from 0.55 to 0.09; and “states cause” decreased from 1.73 to 1.61. Overall, Wiley retraction notices rated lower than Springer when evaluated with our rubric, with every category in 2020 being rated the same or lower compared to Springer. Previous studies on retraction notices have tended to focus on identifying and quantifying reasons for article retractions. These studies have found that the most common reasons are related to misconduct such as compromised peer review, plagiarism, and data falsification (Campos-Varela and Ruano-Raviña Citation 2019 ; Hwang et al. Citation 2023 ; Moylan and Kowalczuk Citation 2016 ; Steen, Casadevall, and Fang Citation 2013 ; S. Xu and Hu Citation 2022 ). Other studies have analyzed changes in the content of retractions before and after COPE guidelines were published, which have found that retractions were more likely to disclose information on investigations leading to retractions after the introduction of the COPE guidelines (Balhara and Mishra Citation 2014 ; Decullier et al. Citation 2013 ; Moylan and Kowalczuk Citation 2016 ; S. B. Xu et al. Citation 2023 ). However, while these studies have been useful for examining particular aspects of retractions, few scholars have attempted to synthesize the multitude of factors that contribute to an informative retraction notice or hold up published notices to an ideal (Bilbrey, O’Dell, and Creamer Citation 2014 ). This study seeks to help fill this gap in knowledge by creating a rubric based on COPE guidelines and Retraction Watch recommendations (Barbour et al. Citation 2009 ; Council Citation 2019 ; Oransky Citation 2015 ) to rate retractions on the metrics of openness/availability, accessibility, identification/candidness, acknowledgment of investigations, transparency on cause of retraction, specificity of information, and transparency on consensus. We used this rubric to compare two well-known publishers to determine any differences in retraction quality between Springer and Wiley across multiple years.

There are many possible explanations for the wide variation in retraction notices seen in our results. For example, different disciplines may have different stylistic approaches to retractions, independent society-owned journals may have different notice criteria than those owned by commercial publishers, and liability concerns might have a profound influence on the way retraction notices are written and disseminated.

One key limitation to the findings presented here is that our retraction dataset was confined to relatively small samples from 2010, 2015, and 2020 (note that the Retraction Watch database now contains more than 40,000 retractions). Therefore, we cannot necessarily generalize our findings to other publishers and/or other years. Additionally, we only looked at retractions from two publishers. It is worth noting that with the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic in mid-2020, a multitude of papers were published in a short period of time, many of which were not subject to the same scrutiny they might have received pre-pandemic; any corresponding changes in retraction content and activity would not be captured by our dataset.

Another critical limitation we discovered is inter-rater variability. Although we created a rubric based on expert, commonsensical criteria, it may have been applied differently based on different rater interpretations of the retraction and rubric. To analyze this, analysis of variance was conducted on each rubric item and each year to see if the evaluators had, on average, similar ratings. These tables are available in the Appendix (Tables 5–9) and indicate that in many cases the raters showed statistically significant differences in average score ( p < 0.05), indicating potential inconsistencies in ratings. For example, one can see a sharp contrast in reviewers’ assessment of whether 2020 Springer retraction notices fully stated the causes of retractions, acknowledged the existence of institutional investigations of retracted papers, or offered insight as to which part of any given paper warranted retraction (Appendix Table 6). Occasionally, even seemingly binary questions about accessibility were discrepant between reviewers, e.g., whether redirection to a retracted article’s pdf merited a “1” or a “2.” In any case, this limitation exemplifies the challenge of manually rating retractions objectively using a novel standardized rubric.

An additional qualification is that we gave equal weight to each criterion, e.g., we valued accessibility of a retraction notice just as highly as transparency with respect to the reason(s) for retraction. This decision is in accord with both the COPE and Retraction Watch recommendations, neither of which ranked or weighted criteria (Barbour et al. Citation 2009 ; Council Citation 2019 ; Oransky Citation 2015 ). Publishers, journals, and readers might well value certain aspects of our rubric more than others.

Our research provides preliminary data about trends in retraction styles. To gain a better understanding of retractions in total, further research is necessary. Since we only analyzed data from two publishers, future research on other representatives of the so-called “Big Five” that controls much of scholarly publishing would be beneficial (Butler et al. Citation 2023 ; Sever Citation 2023 ). Also, we only analyzed data from three years (2010, 2015, and 2020) out of a possible eleven. Therefore, looking at retractions before and after that time span and from intervening years could allow for more granular conclusions about the content and quality of retraction notices.

Despite its limitations, however, our analysis suggests that current retraction notices are falling short in providing readers with necessary information as to whether, when, why, and by whom a given paper was retracted from journals originating from two major scholarly publishers. Indeed, the fact that our rubric allowed for such divergent interpretations of retraction notices, supports the idea that without adherence to a set of proposed normative criteria such as those articulated by COPE and Retraction Watch (let alone a set of binding rules), self-correction in research will continue to be impeded.

Finally, we would take pains to emphasize that retractions are not mere footnotes to the corpus of empirical knowledge. When a paper includes data that has been collected unethically, whether under inadequate consent or as a tool for state surveillance, for example, timely and transparent retraction can become an urgent matter of human rights (Lewis Citation 2024 ). Given the previously mentioned consolidation in scholarly communications (Butler et al. Citation 2023 ; Sever Citation 2023 ), we posit that there has never been a better time for those who publish research to agree to a set of consensus standards for letting the world know how, when and why any peer-reviewed article or dataset is withdrawn from the literature.
