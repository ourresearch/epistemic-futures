# One on One: Anil Dash of Expert Labs

## Full text

Nick Bilton/The New York Times Anil Dash has a new role as director of Expert Labs.

In this installment of One on One, I sit down with Anil Dash, formerly of [Six Apart](<https://www.sixapart.com>), which created the popular blogging software [Movable Type](<https://www.movabletype.org/>). Mr. Dash is now the director of [Expert Labs](<https://expertlabs.org>), a Government 2.0 initiative that aims to connect United States government projects with citizens who want to become more involved in the political discussion. 

Mr. Dash started blogging more than 10 years ago and was an integral part of the discussion and creation of some of the popular blogging software used today.

**Nick Bilton: Do you think people can be a bit insensitive when leaving comments on blogs?**  
Anil Dash: I think comments can become negative depending on the type of conversation you allow to happen. For example, you’ll rarely see negative comments on a site like Flickr, where they’re discouraged.

**O.K., valid point, but you will always see negative comments about a controversial topic on nytimes.com.**  
There are a few reasons for the preponderance of negative responses on the nytimes site. First, there’s very little filtering except for outrageously offensive comments or language. Second, there is a large pool of dedicated detractors who are negative because the New York Times represents something to them, separate from what the story’s about.

**What about anonymous comments?**  
Anonymous comments were never inherent to the Web. We originally had to allow people to comment anonymously because we didn’t have the infrastructure, technology or resources to build identity into commenting systems. But today, it can be important to start linking to people’s [Twitter](<https://topics.nytimes.com/top/news/business/companies/twitter/index.html?inline=nyt-org> "More articles about Twitter.") or [Facebook](<https://topics.nytimes.com/top/news/business/companies/facebook_inc/index.html?inline=nyt-org> "More articles about Facebook.") accounts so that we can apply some consistent identity (even if it’s pseudonymous) to these comments.

**Do you think Twitter has taken away from the conversation on blogs?**  
Apes will always need to groom each other, and Twitter is great for that. So while Twitter is important, a lot of what it’s used for today is not new content. It’s re-Tweeting or sharing links to content outside of Twitter. Gestures like re-Tweeting or “liking” something on Facebook are more akin to applause than to dialogue. I don’t just write for applause, I like to write to start a dialogue.

**So is blogging making a comeback?**  
Don’t call it a comeback, we’ve been here for years.

**Was that a Run-DMC lyric?**  
[LL Cool J](<https://bit.ly/7bnDi5>), actually. But I really don’t think blogging has gone anywhere. Ten years ago, blogging consisted of a few of us pushing a rock uphill. Then on Jan. 20 last year, the White House launched a blog on their Web site.

**You recently started a new job as the director of Expert Labs. What kind of experts are you?**  
Well, we’re called Expert Labs because we think we can help folks in government, the policy makers, tap into the expertise that’s out there in the public. Whether you call it the “wisdom of crowds” or crowd-sourcing or whatever, I think we’ve all realized the potential of getting a large group of willing people to provide their expertise collectively. Expert Labs will help government agencies tap into people, the same way private companies do today.

**What has the response been?**  
These government agencies have a million ideas, they really get it. They have iPhones and use Twitter. They want to build open-source applications. A great example was the recent launch of [go.usa.gov](<https://go.usa.gov>) as a safe and reliable URL shortener for the government. (This launched two months before [Google](<https://topics.nytimes.com/top/news/business/companies/google_inc/index.html?inline=nyt-org> "More information about Google Inc")‘s URL shortener.)

**Can ’t the government just use a URL shortner like [Bit.ly](<https://bit.ly>)?**  
Bit.ly ends in .ly, which is actually a Libyan domain name. Probably not the best idea for the U.S. government to use. If the White House is sending me a Tweet, I need to know that it’s going to a trusted government resource.

**What ’s the big-picture goal here?**  
The difference between now and a year ago is one year ago, people had something to do, they would try to get people to vote. Now they have nothing to do. Our goal is to take up the pent-up civic demand and help the public find something to do with it.

**So what can we expect from Expert Labs**  
The first major projects we’re pursuing right now are to let the White House or other agencies ask questions to a broad set of citizens and then do something intelligent with the responses that come back.

**As the director of Expert Labs, are you a government employee?**  
No, we’re an independent nonprofit that’s part of [AAAS](<https://www.aaas.org/>) (American Association for the Advancement of Science). We don’t have to be part of the government because we’ll be able to distribute our work through [Apps.gov](<https://apps.gov>). Without a resource like this, we could make the best applications in the world but wouldn’t be able to get them deployed by government agencies.

**Has Apps.gov been successful?**  
Before Apps.gov, it used to be a very laborious process to distribute applications to the federal government. Now, the entire process is actually more open than the iTunes App Store. It illustrates the huge radical shift that’s happening in what people call Government 2.0.

**What ’s next with Government 2.0?**  
The White House just issued its Open Government Directive, which mandates its first results happening within 90 days. This new initiative says that every single cabinet-level agency has to have an open government section on its Web site.

**Does government 2.0 feel different than other 2.0s?**  
It feels like fertile ground. It feels like the early days of Web 2.0, and we’re seeing the very beginning of this major fundamental change. It’s incredibly exciting. It means we’re going to save money, get things done in one-50th of the time and create solutions that will work across all government agencies. I’m not a Pollyanna about it, because I know it’s going to be hard work, but I am very optimistic about the potential.

**Why did you decide to work with the government and not go to Google or Facebook?**  
Helping the government is big enough to be interesting.
