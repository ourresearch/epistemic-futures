# Anil Dash on Choosing the 'Instagram of Video'

## Full text

Because social apps require so much of our time and energy, choosing among similar offerings can be nerve-racking. Is one of them going to get bought out and shut down? Will another roll out jarring changes that cause the community to flee? You build your profile, give up whatever privacy the app asks of you, and begin sharing your stuff. Do that on the wrong platform and you've not only wasted time, you're now left trying to catch up to the early adopters who chose correctly. (Good luck getting that username you wanted.)

This dilemma is currently playing out in video sharing, where the apps [Viddy](<http://viddy.com/>) and [Socialcam](<http://socialcam.com/>) are locked in a tight race to become "the Instagram of video." There are small variations between them—a design choice here, an input method there. But aside from the fact that Viddy limits users to 15 seconds per clip, the two are strikingly similar.

So how do you choose? Will Viddy be the Gowalla to Socialcam's Foursquare, or will all those vacation clips you upload to Socialcam eventually be as useless as your Friendster profile?

When it comes to social apps, your best bet is to go with founders who have a history of being obsessed with a single problem. The entrepreneurs who create the most successful social apps often have a record of sticking with an idea through repeated failures before finally hitting on that "overnight success." [Jack Dorsey](<https://www.wired.com/business/2012/06/ff_dorsey/>) started sketching out ideas for a microblogging platform like Twitter in 2000. His cofounders, Evan Williams and Biz Stone, had been trying to simplify blogging since 1999. Foursquare's win over Gowalla isn't all that surprising when you know that long before launching Foursquare, Dennis Crowley worked at a company called Vindigo, where he helped build apps that dished up location-based information. And Instagram founder Kevin Systrom built his first photo-sharing service in 2004.

"The love for the space should manifest itself through the product," Crowley says. "Artists do this all the time: pick a theme, explore it, reinvent it, explore it again. When you look at art in hindsight, you can usually see a linear progression through the work."

Copycat apps chasing the market can succeed, but too often their users end up getting the kind of dreaded email that users of the Android photo-sharing app Lightbox received in May, when the company informed them that it had been purchased by Facebook and that they had one month to download their photos.

Consider [Color](<http://www.color.com/>). It launched to much fanfare in 2011 as a location-based photo-sharing app but never caught on. There's no question that founder Bill Nguyen is an innovative force: Color is his eighth startup, and he has gotten absurdly rich from his previous efforts. But no two of those were alike. Nguyen isn't obsessed with solving a specific problem; he's obsessed with entrepreneurship itself. Case in point, he relaunched Color in May ... as a video-sharing app.

That brings us back to Viddy and Socialcam. Viddy's cofounders have been working together for a decade, on file-sharing startup XDrive and the music community site PluggedIn. Socialcam's founders came from Justin.TV, a six-year-old web service designed for one thing: sharing video. Both apps definitely get the job done. And both boast celebrity endorsers. (Viddy counts Shakira and Will Smith among its backers; Socialcam has Ashton Kutcher and Hollywood superagent Ari Emanuel.) But going by who's been obsessing over video sharing the longest, I'll give the edge to Socialcam.
