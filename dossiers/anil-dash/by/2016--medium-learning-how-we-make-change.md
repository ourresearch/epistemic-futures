# Learning how we make change

## What does it take to actually make meaningful progress in solving society’s problems? It seems like the first step is simply believing that we can have an impact.

Sep 30, 2016

I’m lucky enough to have gotten to work in the technology industry for the last 20 years, which means I’ve gotten to watch people create amazing inventions like social networks and smartphones. Along the way, almost every time that these folks are asked about what they’re trying to do, they answer with some variation of “we’re trying to change the world”.

Change the world! It’s a pretty ambitious goal. But techies using that phrase get parodied on TV shows like Silicon Valley because some of their definitions of “changing the world” seem a little bit, well… underwhelming. Adding new funny filters to our selfies is entertaining and a nice distraction, to be sure. Ultimately, though, if we accept this somewhat cynical definition of “changing the world” we end up not really changing much at all.

By contrast, there’s a vision of “changing the world” that means big things like improving wages and working conditions for all workers, or fighting for criminal justice reform, ensuring that everyone has the freedom to marry, or simply making sure that our basic civil rights are extended to everyone. Issues so important, that they’re central to who we are as people. Yet a lot of us aren’t as comfortable talking about addressing these issues as folks in Silicon Valley are about making new apps and tools. Why don’t more of us who care about these issues feel the same willingness to saw we want to “change the world” as people do when building new technology?

**One big answer is cynicism.** The most pressing social issues seem almost so big that it’s hard to imagine that any one person to have a real impact on fixing them. So we fall back on thinking, “that’ll never change” or “nobody can fix that” or “everything is too broken to really make any progress”. That’s especially true if we think we have to wait for government, or business, or other institutions to make meaningful changes. Like everyone else, I know what it’s like to feel powerless in the face of seemingly insurmountable challenges. But some folks manage to make change happen. Can we learn from them?

# How change happens

On Monday, I’ll be leading a conversation at the White House’s [South by South Lawn](<https://www.whitehouse.gov/sxsl>) festival. We’ll be talking with three people who are both extraordinary and strikingly ordinary: [Brittany Packnett](<https://medium.com/u/e9eb99c8f5d4?source=post_page-----fcd606430db7-------------------------------->), the Vice President of National Community Alliances at [Teach For America](<https://www.teachforamerica.org>) and a co-founder of [Campaign Zero](<http://joincampaignzero.org>); [Carmen Rojas, PhD](<https://medium.com/u/2db10a51a796?source=post_page-----fcd606430db7-------------------------------->), the CEO of [The Workers Lab](<http://theworkerslab.com/>); and [Evan Wolfson](<https://medium.com/u/2ca3c90be219?source=post_page-----fcd606430db7-------------------------------->), founder and president of [Freedom to Marry](<http://www.freedomtomarry.org/>).

They’re three people from wildly different backgrounds, each working on a spate of complex issues. But what they have in common is that they rejected cynicism, they saw that they could have a role in leading change, and they’ve worked to make it happen. In our discussion, we’ll find out how they took that leap, and maybe learn how each of us can focus on the issues we care about and maybe even think about how we can “change the world”.
