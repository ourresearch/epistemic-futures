# Safe in Its Shell

## Full text

Users howled when Microsoft tried to lock down Windows. Now Apple is upping its own security — and everyone loves it.

When Microsoft announced its Trustworthy Computing Initiative in 2002, the response was immediate and consistent. Developers, users, press, and pundits alike decried the effort as an Orwellian attempt to limit freedom on what was then the overwhelmingly dominant operating system.

Of course, Microsoft had perfectly good reasons for undertaking the security overhaul. Increasingly numerous and sophisticated threats — from the then-recent 9/11 attacks to viruses that were exposing more and more vulnerabilities in Internet Explorer and Outlook — had pushed the company to reevaluate its OS. While the initiative had laudable components, the signature proposal, a technology codenamed Palladium, would have locked down a system's hardware and software, allowing Microsoft to enforce strict rules about which applications were allowed to run on a given PC.

Though the company promised to use these new powers only for good, the swift and visceral reaction to the very idea of locking down our computers left a clear impression. By 2004 Microsoft had abandoned the Palladium initiative. (Though it did rename some of the remaining pieces under the unwieldy moniker Next Generation Secure Computing Base.) The notion of a locked-down PC was consigned to the recycle bin of history.

Until Apple brought it back this year.

The upcoming release of Apple's flagship OS, called Mountain Lion (or 10.8 for those who don't count in cats), features a new protection system named, with unusual candor, Gatekeeper. It prevents applications from running on a Mac unless they carry a special certificate from Apple — much like the approval process for iOS apps. Desktop software programs sold through Apple's Mac App Store already have the proper credentials, but independent developers will now have to seek out the necessary signed paperwork if they want to sell outside of Cupertino's marketplace. (Advanced users will be able to turn off Gatekeeper, but they'll have to dig deep into the system preferences to do so, meaning most casual users will simply leave it on.) Unapproved apps, or those that run afoul of Apple and have their certifications withdrawn, will be shut out.

The reaction from loyal Apple developers when Gatekeeper was announced was immediate and consistent: They approved. But it's not because they're brainwashed fans of anything that comes out of Cupertino. It's because the threats that Microsoft saw in 2002 have only accelerated, accompanied by an explosion in software annoyances big and small. (Just how many pointless browser plug-ins is too many?) After the OS clutter of the past decade, most users are willing to surrender a bit of control in return for the knowledge that a given program isn't malware, spyware, adware, or just plain annoyware. Plus, the walled garden of iOS has perhaps made the public more accustomed to such lockdowns.

Even chastened Microsoft may be dipping its toes back into these waters. Applications for the smartphone-style Metro interface on its upcoming Windows 8 platform will be distributed through the Windows Store, and if the developer preview version of the OS is any indication, those apps will have to go through a certification process called SmartScreen. We'll soon know whether attitudes toward locked-down computers have shifted enough over the past decade to give even Microsoft a pass.
