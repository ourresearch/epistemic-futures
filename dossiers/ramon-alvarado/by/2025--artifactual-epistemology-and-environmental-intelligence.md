Introduction
Recently, Sabina Leonelli (2025) proposed the concept of environmental intelligence (EI) as an alternative
framework for the design, development, and deployment of artificial intelligence (AI) technologies. Unlike
conventional views and established imaginaries currently guiding the development of these technologies, this
concept, she argues, can account for the fact that intelligence, human or otherwise, is something distributed,
embodied, and situated amid complex sociotechnical interactions. Leonelli’s critique of conventional views
guiding the current development of AI—as well as the public and academic discourse surrounding it—
insightfully points out in detail the ways in which motivating assumptions behind what she calls “automation-
centered” AI neglect these features of our cognitive and epistemic abilities. Through the imaginary of EI,
Leonelli seeks to provide a framework that demystifies AI in a way that further acknowledges the complex
socioeconomic implications of its materiality, as well as the indispensability of human judgment in data
gathering, curation, and analysis endeavors at its technical foundations. By demystifying these often-hidden
assumptions and offering a broader and more accurate notion of the kind of social and interdependent
intelligence that takes place with and beyond the human, Leonelli hopes to further elucidate how the former
often serve to hide issues of sustainability and inequity and how the latter may inform sustainable design and
help craft better-informed policy.

Leonelli’s criticism is insightful and signals an important move away from conventional paradigms related to
the design, development, and deployment of AI. Nevertheless, I will briefly explore two important
considerations that, in my view, simultaneously enrich and challenge Leonelli's and similar projects, especially
as they rely on a strategy to demystify and contextualize AI that appeals to a more comprehensive conception
of intelligence. In particular, I explore the following two fundamental considerations that continue to limit our
understanding of epistemic technologies (Alvarado, 2023b) associated with AI:

a) the unwarranted use of the term intelligence and other biocentric imaginaries, and

b) the inescapable uniqueness of artifactual affordances.

I take these points not only as conceptual obstacles that are in fact counterproductive to the kind of
demystification required to understand both the powers and the limitations of these technologies but also as
sources of serious misgivings concerning the potential enhancing capacities that they could in principle afford
to our epistemic endeavors. In short, I argue that a key conceptual move in helping to demystify AI—and
hence reroute, as Leonelli suggests, the discourse toward a deeper and more accurate understanding of their

place and role in the sociotechnical architecture of our modern world—is to start treating it seriously as the
artifact that it is along with recognizing its unique characteristics and capabilities.

Leonelli’s treatment of EI already does a lot in this direction. Yet, the full picture of the implications of this
move is missing. Particularly, the recognition that whatever it is that these technologies do, they often do
without appeal to whatever it is that we or other organisms do when carrying out equivalent tasks. It is also
missing the further acknowledgment that not only can these technical artifacts carry out some of these tasks
more efficiently than we can but they can also accomplish tasks of a similar nature—say epistemic tasks such
as some of those in scientific inquiry—in ways that neither we nor any other biological entity could.1 Lastly,
we must acknowledge that in virtue of being technical artifacts, they may also accomplish tasks, epistemic or
otherwise, that no other things can. This will prove important to assessing the implications of their design,
development, and deployment.

Getting Away From Intelligence
Leonelli provides a careful critique of those widespread views of intelligence that perpetuate misguided
motivations and serve as foundational visions for the development of AI. She does this in order to motivate an
alternative understanding of the term that avoids these problems. Namely, a broader conceptual framework, EI,
to understand intelligence in a way that can enrich not only our philosophical debates around these
technologies but also our practical approaches to their use, development, and governance. For example, she
deems current assumptions guiding the development of these technologies as perpetuating the myth that “AI is
grounded on robust evidence” and “can discriminate what is relevant to which problem.” Leonelli contrasts this
with the fact that discriminating what is or is not “relevant information for decision-making is a human
responsibility” (Leonelli, 2025). She further adds that it is “in exercising such judgments that humans remain
indispensable participants in EI, both to ensure the soundness of relevant interpretations of [relevant concepts,
such as health, etc.] within EI systems and to retain awareness and responsibility vis-à-vis EI processes and
outcomes.” Importantly, according to Leonelli, exercising the kind of responsible intelligence inherent in the EI
framework is likely to require more distributed forms of human–machine interactions that will ultimately
elucidate the fact that it is “pointless to compare human and machine intelligence” and that “the key question
becomes, in this approach, to understand their entanglement” (Leonelli, 2025).

I agree with Leonelli and I am strongly sympathetic to the direction her work is veering the discourse toward.
In this section, however, I take on a different argumentative strategy to interrogate the notion of intelligence
altogether. Rather than arguing for the inadequacy of the term, or the inadequacy of current accounts of
whatever ‘intelligence’ may be, I will argue that whatever it is, it is simply inadequate to use it in the first place
in the context of epistemic technologies such as the ones associated with AI. That is, whatever intelligence may
be, it does not play a part in these technologies. Furthermore, the continued use of the term is indeed
misguiding in a fundamental way.

I see the comparative question—from philosophy to neuroscience and computer science that discusses the
similarities and dissimilarities between humans and machines—as irrelevant and unfruitful as asking for a
definition of ‘living thing’ to someone who asserts of a submarine that it is able to do some things similar to
what whales do without being a living thing. In other words, I take the successful construction of technical
artifacts that can carry out these functions as evidence that these functions can be carried out in different ways,
particularly in ways that do not need the thing we thought we needed for such tasks to be carried out in the first
place—for example, judgment, intelligence, and so on. We can think of simple computations, but we can also
easily think of search, chess, and other tasks that have been demystified through mechanization. More recently,
of course, we can also think of sophisticated and complex representative and communicative tasks such as
interlocution and image generation in which the prediction of a next token can produce patterns we deem as
meaningful.

In particular—and in part due to the criticism in Leonelli’s (2025) work—I offer a series of considerations to
sidestep the use of this term to begin with. My hope is that these considerations not only challenge and enrich
Leonelli’s project but also further some of the goals therein.

Intelligence vs. Complex Information Processing Systems
There are many ways in which one can begin to let go of the term ‘intelligence’ when speaking of the
technologies associated with AI. The easiest way is to simply refer to the fact that most of what we deem to be
AI in the 21st century mainly refers to machine learning methodology that emerges directly from data science
practices. Hence, calling the technology what it is—that is, machine learning algorithms, generative pretrained
transformers, large language models, deep neural networks, stochastic gradient descent, and so on—when we
are talking about it should be an obvious choice for those of us acquainted with the technical details of these
diverse yet interrelated technologies. For example, not too long ago the term ‘big data’ (analytics) was the
preferred nomenclature. Then, these analytic tools were taken to be carrying out many of the same epistemic
operations now associated with AI—for example, pattern extraction, prediction, and so on—without much of
the mystique associated with intelligent behavior, human or otherwise (Alvarado, 2023b). Simply by referring
to specific technologies in a clear and direct way one can sidestep misrepresentations about their properties and
hence about the implications of their use. Philosophers and ethicists interested in elucidating the implications
related to the design, development, and deployment of these methods must take precautions to make explicit
the specific technology they are referring to (Alvarado, 2023a).

Nevertheless, despite such an easy way out, there is a deeper question to be addressed regarding the nature of
computational methods and their relationship to both our cognitive abilities and our epistemic endeavors. It is
true, for example, that the use of the term AI and the comparison of computational methods with human
intelligence precedes the statistical turn at the start of our century. Yet, it is precisely in the early history of the
term that one may find even more fundamental interrogations of the terms involved. Because these
interrogations are not necessarily tied to specific material implementations, they get to the core of the

conceptual problems we now face as we try to demystify these terms, these technologies, their capacities, and
their limitations. Take the following example. As far back as 1969, Herbert Simon, in an inconspicuous
footnote of his influential book The Sciences of the Artificial laments the use of the term artificial intelligence
by his colleagues by saying the following:

    I shall disclaim responsibility for this particular choice of terms. The phrase “artificial intelligence” (…)
    was coined, I think, right on the Charles River, at MIT. Our own research group at Rand and Carnegie
    Mellon University have preferred phrases like “complex information processing” (…) At any rate,
    “artificial intelligence” seems to be here to stay, and it may prove easier to cleanse the phrase than to
    dispense with it. In time it will become sufficiently idiomatic that it will no longer be the target of cheap
    rhetoric. (Simon, 1969/2019, italics mine)

Part of the problem that we now face, of course, is that even after three quarters of a century of use, cleansing
the phrase has proven to not be any easier. In fact, one could easily argue that the opposite has been the case as
more methodologies and industry-driven noise is piled on to the concept. It is precisely its continued uncritical
idiomatic use that makes it a target of cheap rhetoric from developers, users, and even critics.

What this quote illustrates, however, is that when we use descriptive or operational language, we demystify not
only the function of an artifact but often also the ways in which such function is achieved. We can, for
example, think of the partially misguiding term of ‘machine learning’ and ask what it is. If we say that it is a
computationally implemented statistical pattern extraction method that can parse, cluster, and hence plot,
predict, or retrodict data trajectories and behavior in a data set, then notice that there is already a lot of
information in this characterization that tells us how machine learning methods carry out their functions. We
can further ask the same about each one of the methods in question and mention regression lines, vector
multiplication, supervised reinforcement techniques, and so on. All this without using the word intelligence.
Notice that in doing so many philosophical and ethical predicaments can be dissolved: for example, we would
hardly ask questions regarding sentience, social relationships, or legal rights of predictive statistical models
with a text output, even if the output were coherent and seemingly meaningful linguistic structures, say words,
sentences, and so on. At the very least, Simon’s (1969/2019) lament here serves to illustrate that use of the term
‘intelligence’ was not immediately obvious or even desirable for some of the pioneers of the technical efforts
on this front of computational methods.

Intelligence vs. Computational Methods
Yet another consideration that I take to be even more fundamental has to do with the way we have historically
understood computational methods in general, and goes back to their early applications. Consider the following.

It is important to understand, for example, that the possibility of computing mathematical functions through
machinery, as Alan Turing envisioned it, did not emerge in a vacuum. Rather, the kind of general discrete
computation he sought to formalize was modeled after the effective methods (Cleland, 1993)—specified

Harvard Data Science Review • Issue 7.4, Fall 2025                        Artifactual Epistemology and Environmental Intelligence




instructions with finite steps and definite solutions—that allowed human computers to carry out complex
mathematical operations in a distributed manner. Prior to the advent of the machinery theorized by Turing’s
work, computational processes—mainly reserved for industrial and institutional purposes such as finances,
government, and war—were carried out by meticulously distributed operations done in time step sequences by
individuals called ‘computers.’ In these settings, an individual would follow instructions to carry out an
operation on some input and then pass it along for another person to continue the process with the next
procedure. Importantly, any divergence from the specified procedure risked derailing the assembly-line style of
the whole effort. Hence, as Grier (2001) describes, some of those involved in computing were required to know
“little more than basic arithmetic.” To be clear, their judgment, or intelligence, was not only not required but
actively prevented from entering the scene. This is in a sense in the definition of effective methods, that they
can be carried out solely by following instructions (Horsten & Roelants, 1995).2 Furthermore, one of the
central implications of the Church-Turing thesis regarding computability, roughly understood, is that if a
process can be functionally described and operationalized, then it can be carried out mechanically (Horsten &
Roelants, 1995)—that is, computed.

This is important because somewhere along the way the notion of complex human judgment in these processes
was inexplicably reintroduced into the discourse about these processes. Since then, much ink has been spilled
on the relationship between the kinds of computation tasks that Turing formalized in his early work and the
kinds of cognitive properties that allow us to carry them out. A full cottage industry of considerations and
responses has thrived in the past three quarters of a century in academic circles regarding machine intelligence,
thinking machines, and minds and machines, as well as about what is now known as the Turing test, for
example (Mitchell, 2024).

While I will not address any of the aforementioned debates directly, there are a few considerations about the
latter—the Turing test—that are related to our discussion. The first one is the fact that whether the test is a test
or a measure of intelligence at all is not immediately obvious. There are important debates that disagree as to
the nature, scope, and subject of the test (Copeland, 2000, p. 522; Gonçalves, 2022; Moor, 1976). Turing
himself offers little on the subject beyond the title of his paper. In the first sentences of his paper on the subject,
he is cautious to shift the focus and limit his discussion to a machine’s ability to successfully imitate behavior
within the parameters of a game and its rules. While he does at times mention that some of these tasks could be
understood as something “like thinking,” he is quick to recognize that, whatever they are, they are different
from “what man does” (Turing, 1950, as cited in Copeland, 2020, p. 442). Here, it is worth noting yet another
furtive shift on the part of Turing: thinking is a verb, while intelligence is not. As an activity, in principle,
thinking could be functionally described and hence expressible in something a machine can undertake, that is,
computable (Turing, 1952, as cited in Copeland, 2000). These seemingly innocuous shifts add unnecessary
confusion to a question that is extremely sensitive to initial inquiry conditions. That is, what you put in at the
start of the inquiry—‘thinking,’ ‘intelligence,’ ‘knowing,’ ‘processing,’ and so on—will determine the nature of
the output, even if not the result. Even the order in which we ask the question matters. For example, notice that

Harvard Data Science Review • Issue 7.4, Fall 2025                      Artifactual Epistemology and Environmental Intelligence




while the answers to the questions ‘can some kinds of intellectual tasks be mechanized?’ and ‘can a machine
carry out some kinds of intellectual tasks?’ may prove to be the same— say ‘yes’—the images their respective
ordering or formulation elicit may be more suggestive of ‘intelligent’ behavior in the latter than in the former.
That is, the latter can be interpreted as implying that machines can behave intelligently, while the former
simply deflates and demystifies intelligent behavior into a mechanism.

Some of the relevant implications of these points can be extrapolated when considering the novel capacities of
more contemporary AI such as machine learning, large language models, or their derivatives such as generative
pretrained transformers. And even more particularly, they can be applied to the artifactual interlocutors with
which we can now interact in the form of multimodal interfaces that process our text, voice, image, and video
inputs into dynamic and iterative commands: that is, rather than representing evidence that machines can
behave intelligently, they prove that these tasks can be accomplished through machine-implemented statistical
methods. The quicker we move away from appealing to intelligence imaginaries, the quicker we can begin to
understand these technologies as the complex technical artifacts that they are. In this sense, I see this latter
move as a more efficient way to achieve the corrective conceptual demystification of AI sought after by
Leonelli.

However, if there is still a need for a unifying term that allows us to coherently refer to these technologies
while acknowledging their important and unique positionality in relation to anything like knowledge
production, knowledge acquisition, or knowledge creation, then perhaps we can use the term epistemic
technologies (Alvarado, 2023b). This is a term that recognizes AI as the distinct kind of infrastructural
technology that is primarily designed, developed, and deployed in epistemic context like inquiry to deal with
epistemic content such as data through epistemic operations such as analysis. Importantly, as I argue in detail
elsewhere, the term recognizes the fallibility of technical artifacts and acknowledges the role of the technology
as a potential epistemic enhancer (Humphreys, 2004) without being a success term. Furthermore, as we will
see in the following section, the use of the term epistemic in this context is something that transcends
anthropocentric boundaries and acknowledges the unique role of artifacts in this domain.

Acknowledging the Artifactual
As mentioned above, one of the main motivations for Leonelli’s conception of EI is to elucidate the
interconnected, interdependent, infrastructural nature of AI technologies, which are embedded in a complex
network of heterogenous nodes and actors that includes environments, data gatherers, data sets, and a vast
ecosystem of storage and analytic computing servers. This elucidation works in two directions. One is in the
reimagining of the concept to acknowledge the fact that models of intelligence can be broad and diverse
beyond limited anthropocentric imaginaries. The other is to provide a forward-looking framework that will
ensure that considerations regarding ecosystems beyond the human are taken into account as we design and
integrate digital technologies into our world. By emphasizing the fact that we need a broader understanding of
intelligence that recognizes the social aspect of our relations with technology and the fact that we are not the

only intelligent creatures within a complex network of actors, Leonelli’s work provides a way for the AI
discourse to move away from the misguided—and potentially harmful—ideas behind the development of
current AI technologies. In her words:

    In an [environmental intelligence] perspective, the governance of digital technologies and their role in
    political system needs to reflect the cultural, socioeconomic, and biological diversity found in human
    populations and their habitats. (Leonelli, 2025, italics mine)

Leonelli continues by emphasizing the material and social grounding of intelligence “as emerging from
interactions between biological processes, different forms of life (organized in varying degrees of complexity
ranging from superorganisms to social institutions), physical entities, and artifacts such as computational and
other technologies.” Again, Leonelli suggests this view as a direct conceptual corrective to conventional views
guiding the current development of AI, which perpetuate the myth that “intelligence is generalizable and
disembodied” and are aligned with “studies of intelligence focusing on human brains working in relative
isolation.” This is an important and timely contribution that also involves a potentially operational conceptual
framework as an alternative, particularly given the state of both scholarly and policy-oriented discourse in AI.
Nevertheless, here I want to emphasize some final cautionary considerations as we seek to provide a broader,
more inclusive, and still useful framework to understand the role, the place, and as many of the potential
implications—good and bad—of AI technologies as we can.

The Implications of Artifactual Knowledge
The first cautionary consideration is that in broadening our definition of intelligence in the way Leonelli
suggests, we must continue being careful not to smuggle notions that may misguidedly limit our understanding
of the unique role and properties of the epistemic technologies at the center of our critique. For instance, we
must be careful to not import notions that blur technical artifacts and their specific functions into the
background by entangling them indiscriminately along with many other kinds of technologies or societal and
environmental factors. We want to retain some individuating explanatory element in our understanding of their
capacities and hence of the implications of particular technological developments.

Given these considerations, notice, for example, that we cannot simply rely on a straightforward broadening
strategy that relies on accounts of social epistemology to make sense of the ‘I’ in AI. This is because when it
comes to the acknowledgment of the role of computational artifacts, these accounts of distributed and social
knowledge creation are at best limited and at worst misguided (Humphreys, 2009, 2020). As Paul Humphreys
noted:

    Social epistemology, like individual epistemology, is infused with anthropocentric concepts—beliefs,
    propositional attitudes, intentional states, and many others. This orientation is outdated, if only because
    we must include scientific instruments and computers amongst the many sources and processors of

    knowledge. These things are sources not just of scientific knowledge, but of broader classes of
    knowledge. (2009, p. 221)

Notice that Humphreys is saying two things here about the role of computational methods in knowledge
creation. The first one is that, as instruments with properties that transcend human epistemic limitations,
computational methods and the instruments in which they are embedded are sources, in and of themselves, of
knowledge. The second one can be interpreted in at least two ways, both of which he is known to have
advocated: one is simply that these artifacts can provide other kinds of knowledge that are not necessarily
scientific: say about facts and states of affairs in everyday life; the other more complex sense is that the kind of
knowledge that they are capable of furnishing may also be of a distinct kind than the kind to which we may
have access without them. This is because Humphreys believes that computational artifacts can enhance our
epistemic abilities in at least three ways: by expanding some existing human capacity, by translating one kind
of data into a more human-legible one, or (perhaps) by opening worlds previously beyond our reach (2004).

This brings me to the second cautionary consideration, namely, that certain motivating assumptions behind AI
targeted by Leonelli’s work—for example, that human limited epistemic capacities ought to be aided or
transcended with epistemic technologies—are more reasonable than they are made to sound. This is
particularly when it comes to desirable rigorous epistemic settings.

While Humphreys’s points above may initially sound as overstatements, it would serve us to understand that
they are grounded in what he takes to be a straightforward and commonsense observation. Namely, that the
“superiority of instruments over human senses is widely recognized” (Humphreys, 2004, p. 10). This is not a
presumption on Humphreys’s part or even a misguided assumption on the part of the modern tech industry, as
Leonelli suggests. Rather, according to Humphreys, it is something that you and I, whether we know it or not,
already rely on being the case. This is especially the case if there is anything we value at stake: according to
Humphreys we can just think of the many times we uncritically accepted the role of video replays to correct
faulty arbiter judgment in sports, photographs as evidence in horse racing, thermometers in cooking, or
multiple precision measurement instruments in the sciences. While no method of detection will be perfectly
reliable, he concludes, “barring strange chains of events, instruments trump the human senses here, there, and
almost everywhere” (2004, p. 4). Nowhere is this more the case than in the medical sciences, for example. This
point is furthered deepened, according to him, in the case of computational methods when it comes to either
our epistemic enhancement or their independent epistemic prowess. Computational technologies such as
computer simulations simply carry out epistemic operations—say computation, calculation, analysis, and so on
(Alvarado, 2023b), better than we ever could or in ways in which we never could.

Although I am sympathetic to Leonelli’s efforts in keeping the role of humans in sight as we develop future
technology, notice that the considerations above directly challenge Leonelli’s second premise, which wishes to
prioritize the role of human judgment “in establishing what counts as relevant evidence for decision-making”
and to establish that “discriminating what counts as relevant information for decision-making is a human

Harvard Data Science Review • Issue 7.4, Fall 2025                        Artifactual Epistemology and Environmental Intelligence




responsibility.” Although this may be the case in many instances, framed as a counterfactual, Humphreys views
entail that there is a straightforward sense in which it is true that much of our scientific knowledge and
experimentation would not even be possible without the aid of these computational methods, which by now
include things like machine learning. But further, there are some kinds—or classes, as Humphreys calls them—
of knowledge that would not be available to us at all without these technologies. Not only this, but there are
certain epistemic operations, certain patterns, and certain dimensions of analysis that are in fact only available
to the instrument and come to us only through indirect or simplified ways. This also implies that at least in
some nontrivial instances, in a technical sense, it is our artifacts that will determine what counts as evidence in
decision-making and in which the responsibility of discriminating what counts as relevant information cannot
be but delegated to them.

While this does not completely invalidate Leonelli’s normative corrective aspiration—particularly if we
consider the complex epistemic pipeline in data-driven methods—it does signal toward the fact that we are
going to have to contend with these points if—as philosophers, epistemologists, ethicists, and policymakers—
we want to have a comprehensive understanding of the full picture of the implications related to automating
procedures in AI, such as machine learning and AI agents in the future.

Now, as far as epistemology and science goes, one may think that Humphreys’s views only apply narrowly to
instruments used in software-intensive sciences (Symons & Horner, 2014) such as astrophysics or particle
physics. However, the general point and degree of veracity concerning the role of technical artifacts in science
transcending human limitations should be of no surprise to any historian or epistemologist of science. Consider
the following quote by Francis Bacon, roughly 400 years ago:

    Neither the bare hand nor the unaided intellect has much power; the work is done by tools and assistance,
    and the intellect needs them as much as the hand. As the hand’s tools either prompt or guide its motions,
    so the mind’s tools either prompt or warn the intellect. (2000 p. 33)

It is important to note here that Bacon is also nontrivially distinguishing between physical and epistemic tasks.
And he believes that human epistemic limitations do need to be transcended through technological means to
advance. Of this he is clear and offers a compelling analogy:

    If men had tackled mechanical tasks with their bare hands and not without the help and power of tools, as
    they have not hesitated to handle intellectual tasks with little but the bare force of their intellects, there
    would surely be very few things indeed which they could move and overcome, no matter how strenuous
    and united their efforts. (Bacon, 2000, pp. 28–29)

And yet, when it came to epistemic tasks at the time, Bacon continues, “men are motivated by a similar insane
impulse and an equally ineffective enterprise when they expect much from either a cooperation of many minds
or simple brilliance and high intelligence” (Bacon, 2000 p. 29). That is, there was a widespread unreasonable

refusal to accept that something else, namely, something external was needed. At the same time there is an
unwarranted overreliance on either the potential genius of individuals or the prowess of social efforts.
However, it is “utterly obvious,” he concludes, “that in any major work that the human hand undertakes, the
strength of individuals cannot be increased nor the forces of all united without the aid of tools and machines”
(Bacon, 2000 p. 29). Hence, according to Bacon, the only way to reform serious scientific inquiry is to make
sure that “the entire work of the mind be started over again; and from the very start the mind should not be left
to itself, but be constantly controlled; and the business [of scientific inquiry] be done (if I may put it this way)
by machines” (p. 28, italics mine).

This is, of course, what Humphreys is echoing when he states that “one of the principal achievements of
science has been to transcend the limitations of humans’ natural epistemic abilities” (Humphreys, 2004, p. 6).
We may now be at a moment, given the advent of powerful computational artifacts, where this point has
ramifications beyond scientific inquiry.

Whether or not one agrees with the deeper implications concerning the role of humans in the future of
epistemology in Humphreys’s points, it is important to not lose track of the fact that the main gist of both his
quote and those of Bacon are meant to acknowledge that technical artifacts in general and computational
artifacts in particular have a unique role in our epistemology, particularly our epistemology of science. While
this may be a technical point, notice that this has important and direct implications to many other areas of our
lives: our ethical considerations now have to contend with accepting the fact that in some crucial instances
instruments may have a better access to the relevant evidence for us to use in our decision-making. One can
immediately see this at play when instrumentation plays a central role in policymaking. While it is true that
institutions and corporations have often played loose with the extent and the primacy of this fact without
appropriate warrant (Leonelli, 2021; Winsberg & Harvard, 2024), there is a sense in which Leonelli’s call to
acknowledge the interconnected sociotechnical environment of human–machine cooperation that broadens the
community of actors compels us to take artifacts seriously as well and not blur or minimize their potential
agency in our sociotechnical environment.

Another related point that follows from this discussion is to elucidate the fact that Leonelli’s criticism
observing that AI technologies work under an unwarranted assumption that they have to transcend human
epistemic limitations may not be all that fair. At the very least, it risks glossing over an extremely important
aspect of our relationship with technology that can and often does enhance our natural capacities, both in our
individual perspectives or in our social endeavors. Furthermore, the discussion of both Humphreys and Bacon
aims to show that this is not something that necessarily needs lamenting. At least not uncritically so. That is,
we should first carefully consider the fact that technology does play this role, explore its implications, and not
assume that it is always something suspect, forcefully introduced into the equation, or inherently something to
resist.

That some of the points above can be usurped and weaponized to justify sales pitches of contemporary tech
giants or unwarranted government powers, as Leonelli warns, does not invalidate them as fact. While one may
think that this is an unfortunate imaginary for the development of epistemic technologies into the future, it is
also worth considering that there are some elements of the framework that are trivially true about the function
of these artifacts: they are in fact often better than we are at what they do. This is in part the reason why we
develop them. While there is a lot of work to be done concerning the accuracy and reliability of current AI
methods (Alvarado, 2022, 2023a), we can accept that as epistemic technologies there will be places of use in
which they will excel. If this is so, we had better take note as philosophers, ethicist, and technologists.

Taking Artifacts Seriously
This brings me to a final consideration. Elsewhere, I have argued that the way to take instruments and their
implications philosophically seriously is to account for what they are in virtue of their designed functions
(Alvarado, 2023a, 2023b). What they are designed to do in a way determines not only how they are to do it, but
also what they can do, including unintended uses. Ultimately, under this view, I conclude that AI is an
epistemic technology. That is, a technology that is primarily designed, developed, and deployed in epistemic
contexts to deal with epistemic content through epistemic operations. Putting this together with some of the
elements of the discussion above—particularly those regarding the fact that some of the capabilities of
computational methods transcend or can surpass the capabilities of both human individuals and of concerted
human-driven epistemic projects such as scientific inquiry by doing things differently—we can also conclude
that this is the case for expanded nonhuman considerations such as the ones suggested by Leonelli. That is, we
can also draw from these points the fact that computational methods like AI and their artifactual and
infrastructural properties are also nontrivially distinct—and often superior at specific tasks—from other
biological phenomena, whether it be an individual organism or a distributed conglomerate of social and
biological processes carrying out what we deem to be intelligent tasks or participating in the general
environment in nontrivial ways. This further implies that our considerations regarding the artifactual nature of
AI ought to be somewhat decoupled from the latter, at least conceptually, in order to fully understand not just
the true nature of the technology but also its effect. In short, we ought not forget that these things are artificial
and that as such they often are, represent, and bring with them unprecedented challenges and opportunities that
cannot be addressed by only considering either our conceptual, natural, social, or political status quo.

Hence, here, as a final point, I want to highlight a different aspect of this artifactual uniqueness. Rather than
highlighting any kind of comparative superiority or inferiority, I want to highlight the difference itself. Again,
machines such as the kinds behind AI are fundamentally distinct: that is, they carry out familiar operations in
fundamentally distinct ways, and importantly, they can carry out fundamentally distinct kinds of operations. In
order to better illustrate the implications of this difference, let us consider a recent development in the
philosophy of robotics.

Harvard Data Science Review • Issue 7.4, Fall 2025                        Artifactual Epistemology and Environmental Intelligence




Recently, Formánek (2025) has suggested a framework to understand what she calls the novel and versatile
affordance mixtures of robot capabilities, which in turn bring about novel and dynamic human–robot
interaction opportunities. One straightforward way of understanding affordance mixtures and their relevant
differences to either us or other biological agents is by noting that in order to carry out certain tasks, “instead of
fingers, robots may use suction cups, soft grippers or parallel jaws to manipulate objects. They can use
different navigation and sensory equipment: infrared, pulsed lasers, magnetism or soft, shape-shifting
structures. They can be designed to roll rather than walk,” and so on (Formánek, 2025, p. 199). In other words,
robots, as technical artifacts, do not need to mimic us or any other organism to carry out functions. In fact,
while useful in some instances, we could be limiting their potential affordances by forcing our biological
imaginaries upon them in others.

Importantly, according to Formánek, even the development of these distinct affordances in the artifactual world
is nontrivially different from the way similar affordances or clusters of capacities develop in the natural world.
For example, unlike in the natural world, the affordances in artifacts are characterized by gaps, or jumps,
between one ability and another in a way that reflects the fractured, often incoherent, interests and resources of
the developers. These gaps can also be due to logistical or resource constraints or the nature of the context for
which the technology is being developed in the first place. That a robotic arm has one kind of joint or another,
that it swings or tilts, that it uses a camera or a laser, that it can hold softly or tightly, does not happen gradually
or cumulatively in a necessarily coherent way. Rather, each of these affordances is tacked on as engineers deem
necessary or interesting.

This illustrates yet another consideration of artifacts that elucidate their metaphysical, physical, and historical
distinctions from other natural systems. This in turn, as Formánek elucidates, also highlights the ways in which
we can use them, cooperate with them, and so on.

While Formánek develops the concept of affordance mixtures in the context of robotics, there is a sense in
which the implications of her view apply directly to many other technical artifacts including computational
ones, and hence, in many ways to the designed and emerging capabilities of epistemic technologies. This
mirrors important work in the philosophy of engineering that has tried to take technical artifacts seriously in
their own right. In the context of AI, Schiaffonati and Verdicchio (2013), for example, suggest that when we
ask “how is it possible for a certain system, that has some specific features, to do x?” what we are really asking
is about required engineering properties of both the task to be carried out and those of a plausible artifact that
can carry them out (Alvarado, in press). They put it the following way:

    [in such instances we are asking] how is x possible in general, and not only for human beings (where x
    can be perception, knowledge, or reasoning)? To answer such questions, AI as engineering adopts a
    peculiar approach: to check whether x is possible, the AI way is to design a specific artificial system able
    to do x and then to analyze which of its features are essential in doing x. In this sense, AI is devoted to




                                                           13
Harvard Data Science Review • Issue 7.4, Fall 2025                       Artifactual Epistemology and Environmental Intelligence




    performance and its essential questions are posed in an engineering fashion rather than a scientific one.
    (Schiaffonati & Verdicchio, 2013, p. 1)

As we can see, Schiaffonati and Verdicchio suggest that in designing AI tools, we are unavoidably wrestling
with engineering questions. This is independent of whether we or some other natural entity can carry them out
and hence independently of how we or they may do so. While we may draw some initial inspiration from
observed phenomena, the inspiration is only a starting point.

Notice that the strongest and most relevant point of the quotes above is in the fact that often, as Formánek
(2025, in press) elucidates, artifacts allow us to not only reimagine possible ways of carrying out familiar tasks
but also allow us to imagine functions that were not even possible prior to the artifact’s advent.

We can extrapolate some of these considerations to specific kinds of artifacts. In particular, and of interest to
our discussion, we can apply it to epistemic technologies. When we do, we get something very similar to what
Humphreys was trying to elucidate. There are very specific ways in which computational methods are
epistemic enhancers. In other words, these epistemic technologies, by virtue of what they are and what they do,
provide novel and versatile epistemic affordance mixtures, which in turn—as Formánek would suggest—bring
about novel and dynamic human–machine epistemic interaction opportunities, for example, new ways to
pursue knowledge, new ways of knowing, and perhaps new knowledge. In the context of societal advancement
in a sociotechnical civilization, these affordances take on a broader meaning and their implications are much
more complex, yet also undeniable.

Considering these enhancements and not just the limitations, failures, or potential harms of a technology
should be of interest to ethicists and policymakers, but they should definitely not be neglected by philosophers
that wish to fully understand the implications of a novel technology. I hope these considerations enrich the path
already drawn by Leonelli’s reconceptualization of EI, as well as her critique of AI.

Conclusion
It is clear that anthropocentric concerns, on their own, without full consideration of artifactual affordances will
fail to account for the full role and capacities and implications of the design, development, and deployment of
technical artifacts such as those associated with AI. This is not just a blind spot for the discourse of artificial
intelligence as a kind of intelligence, or debates in the epistemology of science. Rather, it could also represent a
fundamental disservice to efforts in policymaking and regulation. Importantly, as Leonelli suggests, it can also
be a disservice to the kinds of imaginaries we wish to motivate our future technological environment. Notice,
however, that some of the considerations above also suggest that Leonelli’s efforts in offering a broad
framework that expands into a larger set of biological entities and systems may prove insufficient to account
for the properties, capabilities, and, hence, the implications of the use and development of AI technologies.
This is because we must also be wary of frameworks that continue to center biocentric imaginaries that can
limit our understanding of the affordances of technical artifacts, including epistemic technologies such as AI.

While it is understandable to want to keep some of these considerations in sight in a world in which
corporations and governance actors seek to detach themselves from social responsibility, it is also important
that we keep philosophical discourse as accurate as possible because, as Leonelli concludes: philosophy
matters. The technical artifacts behind technologies that we associate with AI must be taken seriously as the
artifacts that they are. This includes, of course, considerations about their infrastructural materiality, as Leonelli
tells us, but it also involves acknowledging their enhancing capacities and not only their problematic
implications or their limitations (Symons & Abumusab, 2024).

The point I tried to make here can be roughly captured by the following analogy: whatever we have
conventionally been taking—thinking of, speaking of, and so on—as intelligent in artificial intelligence is as
related to human or biological intelligence as a fighter jet’s flight is to whatever it is that hummingbirds do
when they are not touching the ground. Simply put, they do different things—and even if anyone concedes that
what they do is equivalent under certain frameworks, one must at least concede that they can—and perhaps
cannot help but—do it differently. If this is the case, then a change in conceptual tools may be more fruitful
than simply expanding existing ones. While we can continue calling what the fighter jet does and what the
hummingbird does ‘flying,’ it is not clear that they have much in common except the fact that neither is
touching the ground while doing it. The methods by which this is achieved, the properties that allow it on
either, and, hence, the affordances at play in each case are nontrivially distinct. When we understand each of
these elements in either case, we get a deeper and better understanding of both. Similarly, individuating and
identifying whatever it is that AI does through the complex procedural web of data, computing, and automated
infrastructure at its core can elucidate a more accurate picture of both its powers and limitations.

Importantly, this analogy is not only meant to highlight the limitations of the fighter jet in relation to the
hummingbird’s complex abilities. Rather, there is a sense in which if we wish to understand the capabilities and
implications of the design, development, and deployment of fighter jets we must also acknowledge their
artifactual properties and hence the things at which they will actually or eventually excel, either in their own
right or in relation to any other bird’s abilities.
