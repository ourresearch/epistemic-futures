# ChatGPT’s Relevance for Bioethics: A Novel Challenge to the Intrinsically Relational, Critical, and Reason-Giving Aspect of Healthcare

## Excerpt

The rapid development of large language models (LLM’s) and of their associated interfaces such as ChatGPT has brought forth a wave of epistemic and moral concerns in a variety of domains of inquiry, from academia to healthcare and to bioethics (London 2019; Dur an and Jongsma 2021; Alvarado 2022). As Cohen (2023) correctly argues, while some of the issues related to the use of ChatGPT will be recognizable as similar to other medical and bioethical concerns—such as privacy, ownership, etc.—some other issues may prove to be somewhat “new-ish.” As an example of the latter, Cohen points out that bioethicists should pay additional attention to questions of informed consent and how they relate to a patient’s right to know whether they are “treated” by apps or chatbots that deploy AI technologies. The sheer novelty here comes from the fact that “informed” consent must now include information about the specific technology involved in framing medical care. For this reason, one must not conflate Large Language Models (LLMs), generative pre-trained transformer (GPT) models, and other more conventional AI applications in medical AI with the text-generating interface of chatGPT (see Xue, Lei, and Cho 2023) since they not exactly the same. When making reference to ChatGPT, one should clearly note if they are referring to the chat interface and its text-generating capabilities or to the underlying stacked deep neural networks (transformer models) for which text-based interactions are just one of their many analytical dimensions. Following similar epistemic and ethical considerations, Cohen (2023) shows that ChatGPT could easily produce “medical deepfakes” and raise the risk for medical misinformation. And, given the LLMs’ largeness in terms of data & hardware requirements, one could foresee a series of concerns related to competition law, user challenges and a misleading sense of empowering information, sheer homogenization and algorithmic monoculture, as well as environmental impacts given the carbon footprint of such technologies. Cohen’s strategy in assessing the impact of ChatGPT for bioethics consists of detailing the downstream effects of this technology, i.e. from usage to a patient population, and to identify the most credible ethical concerns that could emerge in the near future. However, that text-generating interfaces such as ChatGPT do in fact introduce genuinely new concerns in bioethics in a way that Cohen’s analysis does not seem to account for. We argue that ChatGPT-like tools, when positioned as direct or indirect intermediaries between caregivers and patients, constitute a genuine challenge to the intrinsically interpersonal, critical and reason-giving aspect of healthcare. So, how does ChatGPT short-circuit these important features of clinical care? We start from a non-controversial assumption: namely, that clinical care requires—at its core— interpersonal, critical and reason-giving exchanges between patients and healthcare providers, without which healthcare would lose its “caring” character. Although there is an asymmetrical saliency in the medical ethics literature concerning crisis care (e.g., emergency treatment or catastrophic scenarios in which lives—often many—are immediately at stake), here we focus on the more normal, everyday healthcare received in medical consultations and visits to a clinic.
