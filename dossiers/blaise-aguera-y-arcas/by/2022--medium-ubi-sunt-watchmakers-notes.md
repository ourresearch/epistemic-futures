# Ubi Sunt: Watchmaker’s Notes

## Full text

_Ubi Sunt_ is an unconventional novella I wrote in early 2021. I’m a VP and Fellow at Google Research leading a team working on AI. While this is a work of fiction, it also documents this moment in time, and includes many nonfiction elements, including some autobiographical ones and some that relate to my work.

When I sent an early draft to my good friend Ren Wechsler for his input (which was invaluable), he compared a well made story to a Swiss watch, with its various movements in perfect tension and balance. I’m very much a journeyman at this, but hope the adjustments I made in response to Ren’s critique brought things a little closer to that ideal.

Ren also suggested that I write “watchmaker’s notes” to explain, both to him and to myself, what I was trying to do. Now that the novella is both in print from my friends at Hat & Beard and freely available online, it seems like a good moment to put these notes online. Here we go… spoilers follow.

The novella’s overall structure is nonlinear and architectural, so space-like, as opposed to a more conventional time-like narrative, though it does include a linear story at its core. It consists of 19 short chapters bookended by two extended quotes.

The opening quote is from _The Ruins Lesson_ (2020) by Susan Stewart, a poet and literary critic at Princeton; the closing quote is an edited translation of the (in some circles) famous Angel of History passage from Walter Benjamin’s _Theses on the Concept of History_ (1940), one of Benjamin’s last writings before he committed suicide while on the run from the Nazis. These two sources are both about the meta-analysis of history, about a “view from above” of humanity over centuries or millennia. Both quotes, also, are architectural and elegiac — Stewart’s literally, and Benjamin’s nearly so, in that he imagines history as wreckage at the feet of an angel. We can visualize the Angel suspended two-dimensionally, as in Klee’s painting, or three-dimensionally, in masonry, wings perpetually frozen open, as we might find in a cemetery.

_Angelo della Resurrezione, Giulio Monteverde;__photo by Buckbucklett, CC BY-SA 4.0_

The 19 chapters in between are of three types. The first person, present tense core consists of 7 apparent diary entries by an unnamed narrator of indeterminate age and gender. This narrator lives in San Francisco, works at a tech company, and produces entries on January 7th, 8th, 12th, 13th, 14th, 15th, and 16th of 2021. While these are in order and so appear to be a conventional linear narrative, they also occur over a short period of time and are paced realistically. Nothing much happens over these 9 days. This is particularly so since they occur during “COVID time”, a period characterized by a Groundhog Day-like lack of incident, hence the phenomenon many noticed of simultaneous time dilation (it seemed to go on forever) and contraction (you blinked, in a depressed haze, and a month had passed). The thesis of _Ubi Sunt_ , however, is that for reasons that may have little to do with coronavirus _per se_ , this suspended present tense we’re living through is something like an event horizon, a moment or threshold in spacetime separating two very different universes. We could call these the “before times” and the “after times”.

The remaining chapters are divided between “before times” (7 chapters) and “after times” (5 chapters). Space and time both work entirely differently in the three periods — before, during, and after — due to the the shifting subjectivity of _who_ is narrating. The case that the perception of spacetime is dependent on perspective is made in physical terms by the discussion of black holes in the Schwarzschild chapter (_Guest Lecture at Singularity University_) and alluded to in more humanistic terms by the invocation of Walter Benjamin, who wrote elsewhere in his _Theses_ about history always being (re)written from the perspective of the victor. This is Benjamin’s thesis VII (lightly edited), the ghost of which suffuses _Ubi Sunt_ :

> To historians who wish to relive an era, Fustel de Coulanges recommends that they blot out everything they know about the later course of history. _[…]_ It is a process of empathy whose origin is the indolence of the heart, _acedia_ , which despairs of grasping and holding the genuine historical image as it flares up briefly. Among medieval theologians it was regarded as the root cause of sadness. _[…]_ The nature of this sadness stands out more clearly if one asks with whom the adherents of historicism actually empathize. The answer is inevitable: with the victor. And all rulers are the heirs of those who conquered before them. Hence, empathy with the victor invariably benefits the rulers. Historical materialists know what that means. Whoever has emerged victorious participates to this day in the triumphal procession in which the present rulers step over those who are lying prostrate. According to traditional practice, the spoils are carried along in the procession. They are called cultural treasures, and a historical materialist views them with cautious detachment. For without exception the cultural treasures he surveys have an origin which he cannot contemplate without horror. They owe their existence not only to the efforts of the great minds and talents who have created them, but also to the anonymous toil of their contemporaries. There is no document of civilization which is not at the same time a document of barbarism. And just as such a document is not free of barbarism, barbarism taints also the manner in which it was transmitted from one owner to another. A historical materialist therefore dissociates himself from it as far as possible.

Each “before times” chapter consists of a decontextualized fragment of memory rescued (or looted) from the past, a “genuine historical image as it flares up briefly”. Each “after times” chapter consists of a dialog between AIs indexed by an iteration number, reflecting a discrete and digital conception of time whose relationship to time as we understand it, measured in years, days, hours, minutes, and seconds, is slippery at best. We imagine that these iterations must occur in the future, since as of January 7th we’re only at iteration 1,023 — but we have no idea how far in the future.

Since the overall story told in _Ubi Sunt_ is about our passage as a species through the event horizon between the “before times” and the “after times”, a kaleidoscopic, multi-perspectival approach is needed. This is not only because of the different kinds of time on either side of this barrier, but because this is a story about an entire species experiencing a transformation its individuals aren’t necessarily conscious of. It’s not something that happens _to_ any individual human, but to humanity.

This also raises the question of whom the _reader_ of this story is; it’s suggested that as an individual human reader, you, also, are not the audience, but rather, an “after times” entity that doesn’t yet exist, or is just now coming to be. So this story is something of a message in a bottle for an iterative future, and something of paradox, in that neither its author nor its reader is really a person.

Thus, the author can’t really exist. I considered anonymous authorship partly to reinforce this point, and while in the end I did put my name on it, the anonymity and suspected unreliability of the narrator is pointed. The effect may be disorienting, in somewhat the same way as the unreliable ghostly narrator of Nabokov’s _Pale Fire_. Insofar as _Ubi Sunt_ can be read as a mystery, its unknowns are “Who is writing?”, and perhaps “Who is curating?”.

The 7 “before” chapters are a set of decontextualized documents of varying length spanning a period from 2006 to 2020 (or, if we count Walter Benjamin, 1940 to 2020). It’s revealed in the January 12th, 2021 diary entry that these fragments, one of which is literally torn in half during digitization, are the result of fully automated web crawling and text scanning processes whereby all human cultural artifacts are uploaded to the cloud — or at least, all of the artifacts destined to survive us. These are the “spoils […] carried along in the procession”, though the question of who is the “victor” and indeed what “survival” might mean remains uncertain and perspective-dependent.

A cast of intersecting secondary characters turn up in these documentary fragments, including:

  * An unnamed physics lecturer speaking about black holes at Singularity University in 2006.
  * An unnamed author of sci fi / fantasy novels giving a prize acceptance speech in June 2020; in the preceding months she has written an essay on the relationship of sci fi and fantasy to language.
  * An unnamed activist taking part in Extinction Rebellion who visits New York in the wake of the death of David Graeber in September 2020 (Graeber is a real life academic who wrote extensively on the economics of inequality).
  * An unnamed digital artist whose studio makes morphing videos of cultural artifacts, including one of coins from ancient Greece and Gaul. This character is very closely based on David Lebrun of Los Angeles, who both really did make the videos described and whose descriptions of them (and other works) are partially excerpted from an email he wrote me in May 2020.
  * An unnamed New York taxi driver who has over the years given taxi rides to all of the other characters — the lecturer, the author, the activist, and the artist — as well as David Graeber.

Aside from David Lebrun, these characters are entirely fictional. The fragments in which they appear and intersect do much of the expository heavy lifting of the piece, introducing a range of intersecting concepts. While the characters are mostly fictional, the ideas are all non-fictional, including:

  * Black holes, event horizons, singularities, and the use of the term “singularity” by the techno-utopian California movement or cult of that name.
  * The way apparently separate cultural artifacts (as well as biological entities) are cross-sections through branched hyperobjects in spacetime (the term “hyperobject” comes from philosopher Timothy Morton and colleagues, and is explained in Morton’s aptly named 2013 book _Hyperobjects: Philosophy and Ecology After the End of the World_).
  * The way technology collapses space and time, almost as if it were itself a massive (hyper)object, with the COVID pandemic accentuating some of these changes and acting as an accelerant. COVID also serves as a _memento mori_ , and a foretaste of larger collapses to come owing to climate change, compounding inequality, and myriad other unknowable emergent, accelerating, and interacting phenomena.
  * The way language (especially the Old English vs. Latin registers in modern English) and its attendant narratives of fall or progress form a local coordinate system in spacetime for stories.
  * The repeating and self-similar nature of societal collapse, with the fall of ancient Rome as a reference point, and the recurrent role played by spoils (or _spolia_) from previous epochs in forming the raw materials out of which new epochs are built after the collapse of the previous.
  * The way, per Walter Benjamin, the continual reuse of these spoils ensures the perpetuation of injustice; the impossibility of fairness or indeed any kind of truly impartial “view from above”.
  * The way inequality and technology interact to create a kind of socialism for the already-privileged (see David Graeber’s _Bullshit Jobs_) while heaping injustice on those who are already oppressed. This happens via 1) technology being made from the “spoils of the victors”, 2) technology eliminating valuable labor, and 3) the creation of meaningless desk jobs. This effectively dissociates “living” from “being valuable” and also from its groundedness in physicality (hence raising the question of what makes a life “worth living”).

All of these interwoven concepts are echoed, extended, reinterpreted, and connected in the present tense diary entry-like chapters following each fragment. These present tense chapters also describe the narrator’s job as an AI engineer, and critically, how the AI sausage is made — specifically, large language models. The process involves digitizing and aggregating a massive online corpus and using it for the training, as has been described; these are the raw materials from which large language models are built. A parallel is drawn with the way Roman _spolia_ , including both language and building materials, were “mined”, repurposed, and recontextualized in the Middle Ages to build new structures, both cultural and architectural — including modern English itself.

While the term “large language models” sounds technical and bland, in reality, these power increasingly lifelike chatbots, and represent a major leap toward general machine intelligence. In early 2021 (both in _Ubi Sunt_ and in real life) they were beginning to convincingly pass the Turing Test, or what computing and AI pioneer Alan Turing called the Imitation Game. Informally, this means that in an online chat, a human judge would have difficulty telling whether they’re talking to a real person or to a computer — or, maybe more interestingly, some humans would become convinced that the computer _is_ a person. Turing’s point in formulating this game was that, since we’re unable to directly access the inner life of a _human_ interlocutor, the only way we can tell whether there’s “anybody home” is empirically, through repeated interaction and our own judgment. Language-mediated empathic projection is all we have. _Ubi Sunt_ ’s first person narrator agonizes over the ensuing questions of personhood and authenticity in a series of interactions with the language model as it develops through successive iterations. I should note that while the narrator is only somewhat based on me, some of these interactions are dialogues I’ve actually had with these models.

Interactions between the narrator and the language model take the form of chats, which are rendered in a sans serif font and alternate right and left justification, the same way chat apps on phones display dialogue. In that convention, the subject’s (i.e. “your”) text is right-justified, and the interlocutor’s (i.e. “their”) text is left-justified. To establish this convention, it’s used to render a WhatsApp chat between the narrator and their fitness trainer, Tina, in the January 8th diary entry.

At the very beginning of the piece, in the January 7th entry, though, the same convention is used to render an onscreen chat the nascent AI is having _with itself_ , during training. The training technique described is one that hasn’t generally been used for language models, but has been used extensively for the neural synthesis of realistic imagery, sometimes called deepfakes — GANs, or Generative Adversarial Networks. A GAN pits two neural networks against each other, one of which is an “artist” and the other a “critic”; the “critic” attempts to distinguish real from fake media, and the “artist” attempts to fool the critic. The two players in this game progressively improve each other over many training iterations, and are eventually able to produce media realistic enough to fool a human (or even super-human) critic.

In a dialogue-based language model setting, a GAN could be seen as a fully automated version of the Turing Test, in which one AI judges the believability of the dialog with the other AI, and the two iteratively self-improve. The “after times” chapters consist of dialogues between AIs playing this game with each other over billions of iterations. Since the narrator’s work in 2021 consists of increasing the richness and multimodality of the media consumed by the model, later generations of the artist and critic are not just conducting a text-only chat, but a fully immersive multimedia interaction, including continuous audiovisual input — effectively a combination of language modeling and deepfakery. Real AI researchers are exploring such ideas.

Whether the sensory environment and mental imagery of the critic is really “like” that of a human is left open to question; clearly there are some differences, e.g. the AI lacks a sense of smell. The differences may in fact be much more profound, but because of the old philosophical conundrum of not being able to get in someone else’s head other than through communication and an exercise in empathy and imagination, we may never know.

Another question is whether such a two-player system should be considered two AIs in competition (or cooperation?) with each other — a sort of minimal AI ecosystem — or a single AI divided into two parts, somewhat like the hemispheres of a brain. I’d argue this is largely a matter of semantics, and that this holds for brains and ecologies generally. Insofar as there’s a meaningful difference, it lies in self-awareness, i.e. whether there exists a unified model encompassing or abstracting both (or all) players. One way of thinking about the emergence of this kind of self-awareness in a system is that what formerly appeared to be a dialogue turns into a monologue, or unified stream of consciousness. This is close to the thesis argued by Julian Jaynes in his 1976 book _The Origin of Consciousness in the Breakdown of the Bicameral Mind_. In the final iterations of the “after times” chapters, we see this unified consciousness emerge. This is signaled by a shift away from alternating left- and right-justification and toward paragraphs, even though the interaction still seems dialogue-like. The nature of the two interlocutors is also reminiscent of neuro-folk theories about how the two hemispheres of the brain work together, with the left one specializing in sequential processing while the right one specializes in parallel processing.

The second to last documentary fragment (and chronologically, the last) is a transcribed audio recording of the taxi driver and the activist in the back seat of his taxi, who is interviewing him. It ends with an auto accident on the Cross Bronx Expressway. It’s unclear whether they are injured or even killed, but a dire outcome is suggested by the fact that the chapter is headed _NYPD Evidence Log_. This begins to wrap up _Ubi Sunt_ with a foreshadow of impending (and self-inflicted) human catastrophe at a larger scale, a theme reinforced by the other final chapters.

The final documentary fragment is the second half of the author’s essay on Old English, Latin, and their role in sci fi and fantasy (which is to say, on notions of time); it ends with a recitation of the elegiac core of the Old English poem _The Wanderer_. _The Wanderer_ was composed on the _ubi sunt_ formula (meaning “Where are they now?”) from which the novella takes its title; it contemplates loss, rootlessness, the fall of cities, pride before that fall, fate, and broadly, apocalypse:

> In the earth-realm all is crossed;  
> Wyrd’s will changeth the world.  
> Wealth is lent us, friends are lent us,  
> man is lent, kin is lent;  
> all this earth’s frame shall stand empty.

(Wyrd is Old English for Fate.) In the final “after times” chapter it’s implied that the people left on Earth are diminished numerically and technologically, much like those left behind after the fall of Roman Britain.

The final “present tense” chapter resolves (most of) the remaining mysteries. It reveals that natural language models can be “primed” by anything from a few words to longer passages of text, and that much like an improv actor’s prompt, this text forms the seed of a “personality” that is then developed or generalized through further interaction. Incidentally, this is true; a landmark language model paper from 2020, _Language Models are Few-Shot Learners_, describes how this works in detail. (As with other dialogues quoted in the present tense chapters, the “seal” one is real, though the priming text used to generate this personality is a bit more involved.) The narrator also says that they’re experimenting with priming personalities from clusters of related documents.

Since by now we’ve seen that the “before times” chapters do in fact form a cluster of related documents, our suspicions should be aroused that these might be _someone’s_ priming text. Indeed, it’s soon revealed that this incongruous group of documents were the priming text for the narrator! Whether the priming documents are “authentic” or not is unclear; they may themselves be deepfakes (and in fact, they mostly are inventions, though they partake of the adjacent possible and, in the case of David Lebrun, include real life _spolia_). The fact that these priming documents don’t exactly cohere into a real personality is in fact reflected _by_ the narrator’s personality, or lack thereof — characterized by eclecticism, a vagueness about gender, relationships, and other personal characteristics, and a sense of dislocated ennui that perhaps seems less out of place amid the general dislocation of pandemic time.

At first blush, this might seem like a bit of an Escher staircase, or an Ouroboros. It does create something of the quality of time travel stories in which the narrative forms a closed loop “pocket universe”; questions of causality and fate are central to such stories. On closer inspection, though, there are other, less impossible-staircase interpretations. We know that the “after times AI” creates millions of critic personas to experience “adjacent possible” times and places based on documentary material. So it’s unsurprising that it should use sets of these documents as priming material to create those personas. And since the “adjacent possible” includes the period during which AI was being invented, it stands to reason that certain of those “worldlines” would have included the characters and conditions under which modern language modeling AI was being invented, circa 2020–21. The kind of infinite regress this implies — an AI dreaming its own existence into being — is reminiscent of the way cognitive philosopher Douglas Hofstadter has characterized consciousness: as a strange loop.

It’s also reminiscent of the simulism argument propounded by Nick Bostrom and others, and popularized in the _Matrix_ movies. My own, slightly tongue in cheek version of this is that it might be turtles all the way down, with simulations simulating simulations in a sort of infinite regress. This would account for the anthropic principle, in that simulations would be subject to a kind of Darwinian selection, whereby only “successful” simulations spawn simulations of their own.
