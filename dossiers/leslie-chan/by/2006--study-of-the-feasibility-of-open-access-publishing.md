accessibility, file formats, meta-data production, long-term preservation, etc.;
2. SSHRC should provide some support for the electronic management of journal
production, including manuscript tracking, etc.;
3. SSHRC should encourage the use of existing tools, some of which have been the
result of SSHRC grants;
4. SSHRC should also encourage open source solutions and provide guides to them;
5. SSHRC should strongly encourage open formats and interoperable solutions;
6. SSHRC should take the leadership in organizing national solutions to problems
such as retrospective digitization by leaning on existing university skills and
knowledge
7. Regarding journals: SSHRC should mandate that all SSHRC-supported journals
be made “green”;
8. Regarding authors: SSHRC should strongly request all scholars whose research
has been funded by SSHRC to self-archive their papers in suitable institutional
repositories;
9. Regarding Institutional repositories: SSHRC should collaborate with CARL
(and other suitable associations) to promote the development of IR's, ensure their
interoperability, and improve their role to preserve the Canadian scholarly
heritage. SSHRC should also consider working with AUCC as well as the
Canadian Federation for the Humanities and the Social Sciences to support self-
archiving at the institutional level
10. SSHRC should conduct experiments with journal editors willing to explore e-
publishing and Open Access. In particular, they should permit experiments in
risk-free circumstances to explore various business plans that may be better
adapted to e-publishing. Experiments with existing OA journals and with
transforming existing journals into OA publications should be carefully
documented to help other journals move in this direction;
11. SSHRC should begin a pilot programme to help create a few new OA journals,
either from scratch or from existing, toll-gated, journals, and to monitor the
evolution of such journals for the next three years. In particular, the usage,
visibility and citation rate of these journals could be properly tracked from its
initial OA offering. Controlled comparison of the return on investment for OA
journals and non-OA journals could then be made;
12. SSHRC supported journals should not ask from authors more rights than they
need to function properly. In particular, they should not require rights that may
interfere with legitimate educational use of articles;

I. Genesis of the Report

At the October 24 meeting 2004, SSHRC’s Council adopted open access in principle, and
instructed SSHRC staff to consult broadly with the research community as to the best
way to implement this principle into the current research programs funded by SSHRC.
SSHRC has chosen to promote open access for journals because the concept of open
access improves scholarly communication while assuring that research is disseminated
and useful to all citizens of Canada, including the public and private sectors.

A broad consultation resulted1. Between 22 August 2005 and 31 October 2005, SSHRC
staff conducted a survey across a significant range of actors, including researchers and
scholars, scholarly associations, publishers, editors and librarians to a elicit comments
and views on the subject of open access. A total of 130 submissions were received
(researchers and scholars 84; university presses 2; journal editors 26; librarians 12;
scholarly associations 5).

The largest and arguably most significant number of responses came from the scholarly
community; and within that group 54 of the 84 expressed their support of open access
although many had operational concerns. The second largest group, the journal editors,
was more divided with 14 supporting in concept open access and 12 opposed. However,
all expressed concerns with the financial issues in the transition to open access2.

While the findings of this consultation are useful and important for further study, the
reality remains that the input from scholars, editors and publishers is very limited in
quantity. It was evident that further study would be necessary if SSHRC was to move
forward on the open access agenda. It was also clear that the preservation of the integrity
of the present system of scholarly communication in the humanities and social sciences
had to be guaranteed, and that the transition to a new model of scholarly communication
must be judiciously implemented. Over time, a valuable system has been developed for
the nurturing of humanities and social sciences journals in Canada and this could be
replaced by a new system only if it were of equal or greater value.

It is also clear that the humanities and social sciences in Canada in general live under the
general impression that they are being under funded. However, it must ne noted that
Canada is one of the few OECD countries where the government provides sustained
support for scholarly publishing in the humanities and the social sciences. No such
support, for example, seems to exist in the USA, in the UK or in Australia.3

II. Environmental Scan. A Brief History of Open Access4
Two basic trends have deeply affected scientific and scholarly communication in
the last thirty years:

• the “serial pricing crisis” was first felt and identified by librarians who bore the
brunt of steep price increases to the detriment of their collections;

• the relentless drive toward a wholly digital culture of communication.

The rapid increases in journal prices, long visible only to librarians, finally
reached the consciousness of a number of scientists who began to protest and organize
against this situation. For example, in March 2001, the Public Library of Science (PLoS)
emerged as a protest movement with a letter to Science followed by a petition in favour
of providing Open Access to scientific articles six months after publication. The petition
was quickly signed by nearly 34,000 scientists in 180 countries5.

The PLoS petition reinforced the protests that had continued to emerge from
various librarian communities. As a result, numerous scholars and scientists also began to
take note of this trend. It is at this junction that the Open Society Institute intervened and
organized a meeting in December, 2001 in Budapest, Hungary, in an attempt to provide
some coherence and focus. The result of this meeting was a document that was released
on February, 2002 and the promise of significant funding to support the development of
Open Access throughout the world6. At the December meeting, two main objectives of
Open Access were precisely identified:

Beyond the transition to electronic publishing lies the deeper issue of
optimizing the scholarly communication system for SSHRC-supported journals. It is at
this junction that the question of Open Access becomes central. The advent of print
contributed to repositioning the relevance and functions of documents in Renaissance
societies. Likewise, digitization (or e-publishing) is doing the same for our societies19.
For example, debates about the disintermediation of communications systems reflect
some of the effects of a digitizing culture. Open access is another symptom of these
deeper shifts and that is why it cannot be avoided in the Council's discussions or indeed
elsewhere.

In discussing Open Access in relationship with SSHRC-supported journals, it
is important to keep in mind the priorities of SSHRC’s support.

One particularly touchy question is that of the relationship between a scholarly
association and its journal(s): many associations apparently depend heavily on their
journal to attract members. In other words, the journal becomes the reward for
membership. But then, one may ask, does the journal live primarily for its ostensible
objective (scholarly communication) or to reinforce the cohesion (or even existence) of a
scholarly association? This situation weighs in heavily on OA discussions because OA
publishing requires revisiting the business plans of corresponding journals and doing so
may mean raising very fundamental (and, therefore, threatening) issues about the
financial foundations of an association.

2. Comments on the responses:

a. Transition to e-publishing

It appears that the transition to e-publishing is well underway. However, a number of the
more established journals are still available in paper only. Moreover, when editors speak
about e-publishing, they may have in mind a quick-and-dirty conversion of scanned
images into pdf format with (perhaps) some ability to carry out full-text searching. Some
digitization operations appear to be left in the hands of aggregators who use such fast
solutions in providing articles online. The issue of meta-data is rarely addressed in a lucid
way. Neglecting these issues threatens long term preservation or interoperability of
formats across time. If libraries have access to inferior digital files, they will not be able
to participate in the preservation effort and valuable scholarship could be lost forever.

b. The Role of Aggregators or Third Party Providers

The survey showed that most on-line access to SSHRC funded journals is provided
through a variety of platforms, developed by both profit and non-profit organizations that
offer a variety of services and interfaces. In some cases the publisher is also the on-line
provider [e.g. Blackwell]; in other cases the vendor is a third party provider who acquires
the rights from the journal publisher, usually but not necessarily an association, to
provide electronic access to a title via a package of electronic journals that the vendor
sells to libraries [e.g. Proquest]. Usually the aggregator will offer for a fee an electronic
copy of an individual article in a journal which it controls if the requester does not have
access through a subscription. A list of the principal vendors used by SSHRC-funded
journals is appended to this report (see Appendix 3).
As mentioned earlier in the study, we also became aware that there is confusion regarding
electronic publishing and Open Access in some quarters. This is doubtlessly the result of
the ease with which journals may be accessed when an institution subscribes to an
electronic package of journals. These journals appear openly accessible only to
individuals who are members of a particular university community. In fact, beyond the

12
circle of readers with access privileges, they are “toll gated.”

c. Compensation to the journals from aggregators

Our survey also indicates that many of the editors appear to be unsure of the exact nature
of their contractual relationship to the publishers and/or aggregators, for example with
regard to rights ownership as well as financial compensation. In some cases, it even
appears that some contractual agreements between aggregators and journals are not being
fulfilled.
The use of aggregator services comes with a cost. The economics of the services are
difficult to study since many of these arrangements are confidential and aggregators are
reluctant to discuss them. They are important here they are widely used by SSHRC
funded journals. They provide value-added services to end users; and in any economic
analysis represent part of the cost of scholarly communication.
Many editors of SSHRC journals who completed the grant application forms were open
about these arrangements but it is impossible to say if this is the case in all successful
applications. It is reasonable to consider that the SSHRC application form should be
revised to allow the journal editor to identify specifically the aggregator used and the cost
arrangements that have resulted.

d. Support for a modified form of Open Access was strong

The response is interesting in that it emanates from a set of individuals that actually bear
two hats: on the one hand, they are researchers and they know, from that perspective,
what is good for them; on the other hand, as editors responsible for the well-being of
journals that often need careful nurturing, they are concerned about the economic effects
of Open Access on their publication. This probably explains the muted agreement in
favour of some modified form of Open Access, in particular the request for a moving
wall, the purpose of which is to minimize risks for the journal.
The example of many journals in the Érudit collection seems to indicate that most
journal editors feel fairly confident about not losing revenue with a two-year moving
wall. This looks conservative to the authors of this report.

revolt. The fear is that forced march toward Open Access could be destructive25.

considerably from one discipline to another. Furthermore, in the case of the humanities,

V. Recommendations

The questionnaire to editors elicited unexpectedly complex responses to what was
intended as a straightforward set of questions. Of equal assistance in evaluating the
readiness of editors of SSHRC funded journals to provide Open Access to their journals
were the comments contained in the SSHRC successful grant applications. Together the
responses to the questionnaire and the comments reveal the difficult choices that many of
the editors are facing during this time of changing paradigms in scholarly
communication. What follows are the observations of the researchers based upon these
sources.

a. Going Electronic

1. SSHRC should clearly define what is entailed in electronic publishing, viz.
accessibility, file formats, meta-data production, long-term preservation, etc.;
2. SSHRC should provide some support for the electronic management of journal
production, including manuscript tracking, etc.;
3. SSHRC should encourage the use of existing tools, some of which have been the
result of SSHRC grants;
4. SSHRC should also encourage open source solutions and provide guides to them;
5. SSHRC should strongly encourage open formats and interoperable solutions;
6. SSHRC should take the leadership in organizing national solutions to problems
such as retrospective digitization by leaning on existing university skills and
knowledge.

The authors of the questionnaire were careful to distinguish between the issue of
electronic access and Open Access to avoid a frequent confusion that exists between the
two, as already stated. Open access is not possible without an electronic copy but,
conversely, electronic publishing does not require Open Access. Neither does it
necessarily lead to Open Access. The high percentage of journals available electronically
clearly indicates that most SSHRC funded journals are embracing electronic publishing in
some form. For a number of journals, aggregators have been instrumental in this process.
Those journals that have not yet done so would generally welcome the opportunity but
lack the necessary expertise and funds. Paper versions remain an important part of the
scholarly communication process for many editors involved in the SSHRC funded
journals.

All SSHRC funded journals have websites that vary widely in content and quality. The
vast majority of the 161 SSHRC funded journals use layout software and produce PDF
files in the preparation of their journal. E-mail and, in some case, manuscript-tracking

20
software are used in communication with authors and in the reviewing process.
Ironically, software is often used to produce a digital file designed to help printing. It is
this print-oriented file format which must then be turned around and transformed back
into a suitable digital file format for e-publishing. The rational use of computers would
suggest taking advantage of born-digital files for the digital world, and then deriving the
possible paper output as a derivative. In outline form, this brings to light the difficulties
many editors encounter with the transition to electronic publishing.

While worries linger over the mid to long-term reliability of digital publications, they
appear to be diminishing. At least, they did not figure prominently in the responses
gathered here. Reasons for this may be found in the work that is being done for example,
by JSTOR and certain publishers in this area as well as the discussion of the value of
institutional repositories in assuring the preservation of digital documents. This lends
support to recommendation number (b) that supports SSHRC funding for institutional
repositories.

Editors of journals that are not yet digitized retrospectively perceive the value of
retrospective digitization but are not certain if it is affordable or what platform
(University Press, Commercial vendor, etc,) they should choose to make the journal
available.

SSHRC should be aware of both the number and variety of arrangements with journal
providers. It is a complex issue, partly because individual arrangements are often
considered confidential. It is also complex because of the variety of types of information
providers in the industry. For example, the Johns Hopkins initiative, Project Muse
functions as a consortium of publishers that originated in a common platform for the
journals that were published by the Johns Hopkins University Press. Later, other
university presses joined. Sometimes, a large commercial publisher may strive to acquire
association or independent journals to improve its market share and revenues. Then there
are services such as the JSTOR initiative that was funded through private philanthropy to
achieve financial sustainability. And, of course, there are simply aggregators who are not
publishers but acquire titles and sell them in various digital bundles.
The best advice that can be given to SSHRC on this issue may well be the one found in
the comments made by one of the journal editors in the questionnaire sent by the
researchers:
“I think SSHRC should provide funds to journals that want to become Open Access
journals but SSHRC should never fund commercial journals or aggregators. One dollar
for commercial activities is one dollar less for non-profit academic journals. Non-profit
academic journals will die without SSHRC support but commercial entities can take care
of themselves. [...] Therefore SSHRC should fund non-profit journals that aspire to offer
Open Access in order to increase the dissemination of knowledge. Supporting
commercial entities is against Open Access as I don’t think that Blackwell will ever offer
all its content in Open Access form.”

SSHRC should develop programmes, alone or in collaboration with other organizations
such as the National Library and Archives of Canada, the Canadian Association of

retrospective digitization of SSHRC scholarly journals. A condition of receiving funds
for retrospective digitization should be to place the material digitized in Open Access.
There is a real need to improve the understanding of the costs of digitization and the
implication of certain choices for the long term preservation of the digital record. A
thorough study of this would help many journals and their boards to proceed in an

In moving in the direction of supporting Open Access journals, Council may consider the
following points as important:
One of the criteria presently used by SSHRC to include or exclude titles from receiving a
grant is the number of paying subscribers. This is a practice clearly wedded to a print
universe. While looking at subscriptions makes sense in the case of printed journals, it
makes little sense with electronic journals. In the print world, because the marginal cost
of copying and distribution is far from trivial, paid subscriptions do measure the extent to
which journals are circulating; in the electronic world, subscriptions are wrapped within
various licensing schemes, including so-called “big deals” and various packaging
schemes by aggregators. As a result, number of subscriptions means very little, and, of
course, they mean even less in an Open Access environment. On the other hand, Open
Access offers a number of opportunities to implement various measuring strategies with
the result that actual use and impact can be monitored much more closely.

Open Access does not mean peer review reform. On the contrary, and despite all the
legitimate criticisms that can be leveled at peer review processes, they remain absolutely
essential. This observation is largely supported by many responses to the questionnaire.
Although peer review is independent of the media used for publishing, in the electronic
world, several tools exist to support, facilitate and enhance the peer review process. The
online environment also allows experimentations with expanded peer review and other
forms of open commentaries, thereby increasing more immediate intellectual
participation and cross-disciplinary dialogues.

26
Peer review should be broadly based. It is important to guarantee that reviewing be
external to the journal, and rigorous. “External” here means going beyond the limits of
the editorial board to recruit independent specialists for the peer review process. It is a
good idea to have international editorial boards and it is important that each journal
should be able to rest on a large, international, set of potential reviewers. As part of the
grant -submission process, journals should submit a list of all reviewers used in the
previous one or two years, along with their affiliation. Journals with an international
editorial board should be given higher priority.

The value of a journal should be judged on its intellectual content and the intellectual
quality of its leadership, not on the medium on which relies. Old worries about the
intrinsic intellectual quality of electronic journals have, we hope, been put safely to rest.

This said evaluation criteria must take the medium into account. With electronic journals
(and a fortiori, with Open Access journals), the quantitative criteria must be adjusted to
monitor actual use as accurately as possible. Downloads and citations are part of the
relevant quantitative measures, as are links and, perhaps, hits. Actually, downloads have
been shown to be good predictors of citations37. SHHRC should establish a study group
to work out a concrete set of quantitative criteria that could be uniformly applied to all
journal applicants. The study group should examine recent developments in deep server-
log analysis, alternative citation metrics to measure impact factor, search engine rankings,
and other relevant online usage measures.

Having a journal indexed in a respected and widely-used indexing service is of itself a
sign of quality. The wider the indexing coverage, the stronger is the presumption that the
journal is of high quality. Sociological Abstracts, the literature bibliography from the
Modern Language Association both provide good examples of the kinds of tools we have
in mind.

Any new journal title, regardless of access methods, needs time to establish itself.
Consequently, SSHRC should consider applying the regular criteria for evaluation only
after a period of three years in the case of new Open Access publications. This means that
new Open Access journals should be granted a three-year probation period to establish
themselves in these indexing services. Among the useful indexes are the Directory of
Open Access Journals (DOAJ) from Lund University, and Open JGate in India (for
English language OA journals only), as well as the open catalogues of various major
research universities in the world. Major university libraries make their catalogue readily
available on the Internet.

It must be noted that the present financing process followed by SSHRC tends to favour
conservatism and does not easily allow for the emergence of new publications. With
electronic publishing, journals can be developed more quickly and thus respond more
efficiently to the emerging trends in any given research field. The intellectual energy
accompanying a new journal is a positive factor for the corresponding discipline. In the

Many editors, in responding to the questionnaire, have expressed interest in Open Access,

managed. Providing a special source of funding to offset possible losses of subscription
revenue could become a strong incentive to move toward Open Access. Given that many
of these journals have small subscription revenues, the needed financing, which could
take the form of a kind of insurance policy, ought to be quite limited.

SSHRC should encourage journals that wish to experiment with conversion to Open
Access to work collectively in a SSHRC-supported experiment designed to better
understand the financial implications, authors’ uptake, and usage of publication between
and after becoming Open Access. The experimentation will provide better data to gauge
the financial viability of Open Access publishing. These results will be useful in turn to
examine whether scaling up the process to a larger number of journals is desirable.

VII. Conclusions
Perhaps the most valuable consequence of this study has been the important recognition
that there is no magic way to move into electronic publishing and Open Access. Testing,
exploring and experimenting while consulting and dialoguing should be the principles
under which any kind of action plan should be undertaken.

With regard to electronic publishing, environmental pressures as well as various forms of
inducements on the part of aggregators or some publishers have led to a transition carried
out in such a wide variety of ways that “chaos” might well be the best term to describe it.
In the process, SSHRC is finding itself subsidizing some very profitable commercial
outfits, while denying support to some innovative Open Access journals that are
deserving of help except for the fact that they refuse to seek paying subscribers.

A good reason for this chaotic transition to e-publishing may well have been the
consequence of the inability to create orderly experiments so as to identify best practices
and enhance the sharing of new know-how. Only in Québec has there been the semblance
of an organized move toward electronic publishing, but it may have been done in such a
centralized manner that it may not fit the ethos of the rest of the country. Nonetheless, it
remains a valuable source of experience. Elsewhere, we have dispersed endeavours to
produce electronic journals, most of the time on tiny scales. On the non-commercial
front, only a very few university presses have developed in-house capacity in this regard.
Whether they are willing to share this know-how is far from obvious.

With regard to Open Access, we are strongly arguing in favour of it, but promote a very
gradual move in that direction. First and foremost, a set of experiments, some of which
have been suggested or outlined above, must be set in motion in the near future so as to
gain some solid evidence about the real obstacles on the road to Open Access, and about
the real advantages that OA provides authors, journals, the general citizenry, and the
public at large.

Finally, it seems clear that SSHRC must take on a leadership position in this regard. As
other granting councils in the USA and in Europe (particularly the UK, Germany and
France) have amply shown, this is to SSHRC’s advantage. But, more fundamentally, if
SSHRC does not show some national leadership, no one else will do it, except perhaps in
the form of some bid to become the monopolistic device for SSH publishing in the
country. Clearly, no one wants this outcome. No one wants one university press, or, even
worse, a large commercial press to become the sole provider of e-publishing services to
Canadian SSH. At the same time, it is clear that the emerging digital environment is
challenging all publications to globalize in an effective manner.

It is natural that journals in similar disciplines be grouped together so that a particular
journal platform tends to become well known for its coverage in, for example, economics
or law, and a number of journals from a variety of publishers in many countries might be
housed in that disciplinary platform. But is the notion of a national platform is useful to
researchers used to work on well-focused issues with information coming from all over

29
the planet? In other words, how does one reconcile the idea of a national strategy for
scientific and scholarly publishing with the universal characteristics of validated
knowledge? These questions lie beyond the scope of our report, but they are part of the
changing landscape of Canadian scholarly communications as it impacts SSHRC-
supported journals and they should not be neglected.

The latter question arises in the context of the role a national platform such as Synergies
might play in the dissemination of SSHRC-funded titles.

z How would Synergies impact on the present arrangements between journals,
publishers and aggregators?
z Would Synergies be simply another aggregator at the national level, competing
with commercial aggregators, or would it replace these existing platforms?
z What would be the requirements placed upon individual journals to participate?
z Would there be a required participation in Synergies?
z Should Synergies take Open Access into account in its business plan?

Our recommendation here is that, whatever the chosen solution, it should be strongly
decentralized, distributed, and closely follow the needs of research communities. Only in
this fashion can the need to leave as much autonomy and initiative to academic
researchers be reconciled with the need to construct a truly robust architecture for
Canadian SSH publishing.
