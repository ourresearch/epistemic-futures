Scholarly communication and scholarly publishing, in particular, play a critical role not
only in the dissemination of knowledge, but also in the production of scholarly careers
and in the reproduction of scholarly communities. With the turn to the digital, innovative
and diverse forms of scholarly discourse and research practices are fast emerging.
Enabled by a variety of powerful computing tools and social media, these practices are
typically collaborative, network-based, and increasingly data-intensive. This is true not
only for the sciences, but for the humanities and social sciences as well.

These new practices also co-exist to varying degrees with the norms, values and practices
of traditional scholarly publishing. What will this mean for scholarly communication and
knowledge production in the near and long term? The answer, in large measure, could, or
more strongly, should, depend on scholars. But will it?

It is scholars who create the content, scholars who determine how and in what ways they
will disseminate their research, and scholars who are the gatekeepers of control through
mechanisms such as peer review. Increasing numbers of scholars are actively involved
with the design and implementation of next-generation scholarly communication
infrastructure (refs eprint archives, journal platform such as PKP, knowledge sharing
tools such as Zotero). But while they play a critical role, scholars are part of the complex

scholarly communication ecosystem that includes funders and funding agencies,
university administrators, publishers, librarians, technical staff and others. The interests
of the individual groups comprising the system do not always align, and the profit motive
of commercial publishers, who increasingly dominate the control and production of
“certified” knowledge in journals and monographs, are especially at odds with scholars’
interest in seeing the result of their work as widely disseminated as possible. It is out of
the interactions and tensions among the various players that the scholarly communication
system is being reshaped. The results of activities in one part of the system will impact
others, albeit in different ways.

To what extent are scholars aware of emerging trends in research and publication
practices, or aware of activities of other parts of the ecosystem that will impact them?
And what actions should they take to ensure that new practices that expand the impact of
research are valued, adopted, and credited?

The panel will explore these questions from three perspectives. First, Gale Moore, a
sociologist, who has recently completed a survey of University of Toronto faculty on
their awareness, attitudes and practices regarding scholarly communication, will draw on
results of her research and the literature of scholarly communication to situate the
questions in the context of the broader intellectual community, and the recent history of
engagement with these ideas. Leslie Chan, a scholarly publishing specialist, will review a
number of converging trends, such as CIHR and SSHRC’s increasing interest in
knowledge translation and mobilisation and the interest of these funding agencies in
demonstrating the impact of research beyond traditional measures. He will also highlight
alternative metrics of relevance and impact being developed by researchers, and the
implications of the broadening of open access from publication to the underlying data.
Clément Arsenault will bring the perspective of an information science scholar and editor
of CJILS, the leading Canadian journal in the field. Nadia Caidi, professor in the iSchool
at the University of Toronto and President of CAIS/ACSI will actively moderate the
session, with probing questions and challenges for the panelists and the audience.

The dramatic changes in the nature of scholarly research and practices; new policies
developed by those who fund the research; the view of university administrators, and the
activities of monograph and journal publishers – both scholarly and commercial, all play
a role in reshaping scholarly communication. The technical and material conditions offer
up a set of possibilities, but the challenges are social and institutional. In the context of
the CAIS/ACSI annual meeting, there is a great opportunity to examine collectively the
role of those whose object of study is information: What is the role of scholars and
information professionals in these debates? How might the Canadian information and
library science community respond?

What is clear is that there is a need for leadership and research on these issues. We hope
the panel will spark debates and action, leading to collaborative research, system design,
tools development, community building, and policy development.
