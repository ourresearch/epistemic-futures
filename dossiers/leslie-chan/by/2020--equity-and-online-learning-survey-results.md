# Equity and Online Learning Survey Results

## Full text

## Abstract

What is a university? In Canada, as elsewhere, it is a centre for research and teaching, supported in part by public funds. It is also an employer, a producer of images, a subject of rankings, a real estate owner, a generator of revenues, and a hub in global networks of value and aspiration. But how does a university work? What exactly does it do? What are the powers and pressures, the practices and networks that constitute contemporary university worlds? An interdisciplinary team of faculty at the University of Toronto, we seek to discover the many worlds of our own institution, in collaboration with graduate and undergraduate students. We foreground the everyday experience of people who work or study in different corners of the institution, who live in its shadow, or respond to its public face.

### Executive Summary

This memo outlines key findings from the survey on the University of Toronto's (UofT) recent shift to online learning in March, 2020 due to the COVID-19 pandemic.

Although our study has a number of limitations, the results suggest that this emergency move to online learning was experienced unevenly by faculty and students and that in many (but not all) instances the shift has been disproportionately detrimental to equity seeking groups (as has been the case with the spread of COVID-19 itself).

### Background

Discovering University Worlds is a SSHRC-funded interdisciplinary team of faculty and students who undertake research at UofT on matters that include finance, ethics, diversity, and mobilizations. Shortly after courses were moved online in Spring 2020 a small group of faculty and graduate students from this research group developed a brief survey to consider experiences with this emergency shift to online classes. This survey is part of a wider set of studies looking at experiences of students and faculty at UofT. We distinguished our survey from other similar ones being undertaken at the time in two ways: first, we placed equity issues at the centre, second, we sought to understand the different experiences between faculty and students, and third, we committed to providing the results directly to respondents and disseminating them widely. (Note: the study received ethics approval from the University of Toronto; authors' names are listed alphabetically.)

The survey was conducted in May-June, 2020 and had 410 responses, with 277 people completing every question, and 830 written answers. Because this research group operates independently of the UofT administration, we distributed the surveys mostly through students' organizations and faculty networks. As a result, the sample cannot be seen as representative of the UofT community as a whole. Other limitations of the study need to be acknowledged. Because of our relatively small sample size and the underrepresentation of some equity seeking groups at UofT, our analysis of equity issues relies, at times, on small numbers of respondents (for instance, we do not analyze data for those who identify as Indigenous since this group represents only 1% of respondents). While our findings must be seen as suggestive rather than conclusive, they raise critical questions:

(1) Why UofT does not itself collect some of this data, especially with a focus on equity seeking groups who seem more adversely affected by the recent shift to online learning? (2) Why does UofT not collect comprehensive race-based data on students --a concern that has already been raised --which would allow it (and others) to better understand the systemic processes at work? Below are key findings of the survey, and in the document that follows we provide a more detailed breakdown of the results.

### Key Findings

### 1.

There is evidence that the shift to online learning has had a disproportionately negative impact on equity seeking groups. Respondents identifying as Black, low-income, person of colour, LGBTQI2S, and religious minority were more likely than others to feel that their needs were not considered during the shift to online learning. Twelve out of fifteen Black respondents reported that the shift to online learning had caused them significant distress. Respondents identifying as Black, living with a disability, LGBTQI2S, or religious minority particularly reported that their living situation made it difficult for them to provide focused attention for online classes. The wider context, difficult to disentangle from the shift to online learning, is the disproportionate burden that the effects of the virus has placed on equity-seeking groups. Around three out of every four Black and low-income respondents had suffered a drop in income. Many students' family members were also adversely impacted by the pandemic, but its effects were not evenly distributed. For example, respondents at UTSC compared to UTSG were almost twice as likely to be dependent on those who had suffered a drop in income. Respondents living with a disability were particularly hindered in accessing accessibility services on campus.

2. Almost half of respondents (47%) took on additional care responsibilities due to the pandemic. Respondents living with disabilities and low income respondents were more likely to see their care responsibilities increase. Students and not just faculty reported that they were taking on significant care responsibilities. For example, 40% of undergraduate students took on new care obligations as a result of the pandemic. These included increased childcare and eldercare responsibilities, homeschooling and caring for sick relatives. In some cases, international respondents also reported investing significant time in caring for relatives remotely.

3. The responses raised concerns about the inferiority of learning in the shift to online learning as well as concerns with data security. The statement that elicited the strongest response (agreement) was that "Online teaching provides an inferior learning experience compared to inperson classes." Most respondents expressed considerable concern about whether their private information was adequately protected during online learning. However, some comments from respondents suggested a positive side to online learning, including its greater flexibility in comparison to in-person classes.

### 4.

Responses suggested that the shift to online learning took place in a haphazard way and with great variation by division, department, and class was evident in the survey results. Students report very positive and very negative experiences, with the difference often noted in the specific approach of individual instructors. Faculty were more likely than students to say that the university did not give them adequate time and resources to switch to online learning, and to feel that they were not adequately consulted about this shift.

5. Even though we did not ask a question about fees, in the comments section, a number of students stated that fees should be reduced for online courses, in light of significant drops in income and in view of the upcoming academic year.

### Experiences With the Shift to Online Learning:

Question 1: The university gave me adequate time and resources to switch to online classes in the Spring.

• 36% of respondents either strongly disagreed or disagreed, with 53.3% either neutral, agreeing, or strongly agreeing. • Faculty disagreed more than students with this statement. The majority of faculty (59.4% of tenured and 56.3% of pre-tenure faculty) strongly disagreed or disagreed, with 31.3% of pre-tenure faculty strongly disagreeing. • Black respondents tended to disagree or strongly disagree (50%) more than respondents overall. • International students agreed or strongly agreed (45.4%) more than noninternational students (28.9%).

Some themes raised in the comments section:

• Recognition of the quick onset of the pandemic: "It just had to be done.

There was no time to give. When it happened we had to pivot to complete our courses for students." • Technology: Those switching to teaching online described the challenges with learning online platforms with only a weekend to make the shift. • Inconsistency: Some students felt supported by their professors in the transition, while others suggested that the actions of their professors exacerbated stress levels. Professors who made themselves available to discuss course material and demonstrated flexibility with deadlines were viewed more positively by students. unpaid hours to manage the transition. Some faculty spent money out of pocket to transition to particular software they needed for online teaching.

• Take More Time: Many suggested that taking a week for transition would have been helpful. Several respondents noted that they had only a couple of days notice (over a weekend) before classes were expected to be delivered online.

Question 2: The university gave me adequate avenues to provide my feedback during the transition to online learning.

• 45.4% of respondents either disagreed or strongly disagreed, with 18.9% neutral, and 26.8% either agreeing or strongly agreeing (only 3.1% strongly agreed). • Faculty (tenured and pre-tenured) agreed with this statement the least of all the roles surveyed. Of faculty, 6.3% of tenured faculty and 12.5% of pretenured faculty, with 0% of faculty strongly agreeing. Among students, 27.9% of undergraduate students, 23% of graduate students who were TAing (working as a Teaching Assistant), and 41.2% of graduate students who were not TAing agreed or strongly agreed with this statement. • Of the respondents who identified with an equity seeking group, respondents who identified as Black (56.3%), LGBT+ (58.3%), religious minority (56.4%), low income (52.3%), language minority (50%), and persons of colour (47.2) disagreed and strongly disagreed with this statement at a higher rate than the overall respondents. • Almost half of international students agreed with this statement (48.5%), whereas only 22% of domestic students agreed.

Some themes raised in the comments section:

• No avenues for feedback. (e.g. "This is the first opportunity I have been given to address my experience with online learning at U of T as a result of the pandemic.") • Students' experiences: Students expressed that their avenue for feedback was through their professors, international students expressed that they received support through international student associations, (e.g. "My professors welcomed, for the most part, any feedback on courses. Some were also available to talk and support students in general.")

Question 3: I feel that my needs were considered during the shift to online learning.

• Overall, 36.8% strongly disagreed or disagreed, in contrast to 32.7% that agreed or strongly agreed, with 23.4% neutral. • Among faculty, only 22.6% of tenured faculty agreed or strongly agreed, while 32.3% of tenured faculty strongly disagreed and 12.9% disagreed. Among pre-tenured faculty, 25% agreed and 43.8% either strongly disagreed or disagreed. • The majority of respondents who identified as Black (68.8%), LGBT+ (58.3%), religious minorities (51.2%) either disagreed or strongly disagreed with the statement. Respondents who identified as low income (47.7%) and Persons of Colour (39.1%) disagreed or strongly disagreed above the average. • The responses for international and non-international students were fairly similar, but slightly more domestic students disagreed (30.33% of international students and 37% of domestic students disagreeing or strongly disagreeing).

Some themes raised in the comments section:

• Accessibility: Some students felt that their access needs were met, where others did not. (e.g. "And I would usually never choose to do online learning for the reason of trouble with comprehension." and "As an AccessAbility student, I did not feel that my needs were considered.") • Inconsistent support from professors: (e.g. "One of my two professors cared about our needs. She was sensitive and approached the transition with a lens to equity. She cared. She did her best. That acknowledgement went a long way. We were all struggling, it wasn't easy;" and "Depended completely on the course instructors rather than the institution of UofT itself since the different measures taken by professors were not guided by university policy or announcements.") • Stressful for students to transition to online assessments.

• Lack of extra TA hours to support the transition.

• Good support offered by the online teaching support services at U of T.

Question 4: Academic integrity (e.g. efforts to prevent plagiarism etc.) was adequately protected during the shift to online learning.

• 37.5% of respondents agreed or strongly agreed, with only 25.9% of respondents disagreeing or strongly disagreeing • Pre-tenure faculty were the only group with a majority disagreeing (68.8% disagreed or strongly disagreed). In the other groups, 38.7% of tenured faculty, 14.7% of graduate students who were not TAing, 29.2% of graduate students who were TAing, and 18.7% of undergraduate students disagreed or strongly disagreed. In fact, the majority of undergraduate students agreed or strongly agreed (55.1%). • Several equity seeking groups agreed at a higher rate than the average (51.3% of religious minorities, 50.1% of Black respondents, 44.8% of persons of colour, 44.7% of language minorities, 43% of women, and 38.6% of low income respondents either agreed or strongly agreed).

Some themes raised in the comments section:

• Some respondents felt they received information on academic integrity (e.g. "Guidance was given on how to maintain academic integrity."). • Many students and faculty expressed scepticism that it is possible to have academic integrity with exams online if they are supposed to be "closed book". These types of exams were generally based on what many respondents called the "honour system" (e.g. "There was no way to ensure students were not cheating on online final exams."). • Some respondents noted that academic integrity was not a priority during the transition, as other things were the focus (e.g. "Plagiarism is a big concern for me. But it's not my concern right now. I believe that the physical and mental health and well-being of our students needs to be the top priority during this unprecedented time.")

### Implications of Online Learning:

Question 5: I am concerned that my private information will be retained by online platforms without my permission.

• Almost half of respondents agreed or strongly agreed (46.9% total, 15.5% strongly agreed and 31.4% agreed), as opposed to 29.7% who strongly disagreed or disagreed. • Tenured faculty agreed at the highest rate (41.% strongly agreed, 25.8% agreed). • Responses were similar across all campuses and for international/noninternational students. • Of the respondents who identified as an equity seeking group, respondents living with a disability had the highest level of "strongly agree" (28%), followed by Black respondents (25%).

Some themes raised in the comments section:

• Several responses expressed confidence in U of T affiliated software like Bb Collaborate (e.g. as opposed to zoom). • Several responses noted that this is "part of life" now in general.

• Many comments did express concern with monitoring and surveillance (e.g. "As someone who comes from a community that is already oversurveilled, it is scary that platforms like zoom have already created online profiles for me without my permission among other privacy concerns.") • Many responded that this question actually prompted their concern.

• Many professors responded that their lecture content is now online and can be used without their permission. (e.g. "I'm a bit concerned with students taking the lecture content and posting it online, where it could be taken out of context and used in ways I do not approve of.") Question 6: It takes longer to prepare for and undertake online classes compared to in-person classes.

• 59.8% either strongly agreed or agreed, with only 25.4% disagreeing or strongly disagreeing

• The majority of faculty at all levels agreed or strongly agreed (e.g. 66.7% of sessional faculty, 68.8% of pre-tenure faculty, and 64.5% of tenured faculty strongly agreed). • Around half of students at all levels agreed or strongly agreed (55.6% of undergraduate, 60.4% of graduate students TAing, and only 44% of graduate students not TAing). • The responses were similar across all three campuses.

• Some equity seeking groups agreed or strongly agreed above the average respondents (e.g. 73.5% of LGBT+ respondents, 65.7% of language minority respondents, 74.3% of religious minority respondents, 68% of respondents living with a disability, 62.5% of Black respondents, 61.1% of persons of colour respondents strongly agreed or agreed).

Some themes raised in the comments section:

• There were divergent comments, with some really preferring the online environment and others saying it was very difficult to learn online. • Time is saved when commuting is cut down with online classes.

• Many mentioned the distractions and lack of space at home meant that it took more time (e.g. "The focus at home is different with impending pressures from work/family life that don't exist when you walk into a lecture hall for an hour.") • Several faculty commented that, as it was their first time teaching online, it took a lot longer to prepare. (E.g. "The learning curve was steep." and "My teaching workload doubled (perhaps tripled?) this past semester.") • Respondents raised concerns about accommodations (e.g. "There has been no consideration for appropriate accommodations for students registered with accessibility services and eligible for them, ESPECIALLY graduate students without supervisory support (i.e. enrolled in professional programs)").

Question 7: I encounter problems in online classes because of my insufficient access to the internet (bandwidth, access to a computer etc.).

• Half of respondents disagreed that insufficient internet created problems for online classes. However, over a third of respondents (35.2%) did agree or strongly agree that their access to the internet created problems for online classes. Overall, 17.6% strongly disagreed, 32.4% disagreed, 12.3% neutral, 26.4% agreed, 8.8% strongly agreed. • Responses were relatively similar across all roles and levels. 33% of undergraduate students, 39.6% of graduate students who were TAing, 37.9% of graduate students who were not TAing, 31.3% of pre-tenure faculty, 32.3% of tenured faculty strongly agreed or agreed. • Several equity seeking groups agreed at a higher rate than the average, including respondents who identified as living with disability (56%), religious minorities (46.2%), language minorities (44.7%), LGBT+ (41.4%), low income (38.6%), persons of colour (37.3%), and women (36.6%). • International students tended to agree and strongly agree (48.5%) more than domestic students (36.8%).

Some themes raised in the comments section:

• Many respondents raised issues with the platforms lagging, crashing, or stopping in the middle of lectures. • Many respondents did not have reliable internet or electricity. Even if respondents had access to internet, it was slow and unreliable due to whole households and neighbourhoods all working from home (e.g. "Videos crashed and I often had to restart them. This is due to everyone else in the house doing video conferencing for work, or online classes, or doing day trading online. Thus, the broadband was limited, making a 2 hour lecture, 2 hrs 45min or longer.") • Many students commented that they did not have access to a good laptop. • Unreliable and unpredictable access to the online classes and materials due to technological challenges created stress in the learning environment.

Question 8: My living situation makes it difficult to provide focused attention to online classes (limited space, childcare responsibilities, etc.).

• 59.8% of respondents agreed or strongly agreed, 31.3% disagreed or strongly disagreed. • Faculty seemed to have higher levels of agreement than students. 62.6% of pre-tenure faculty, and 58.1% of tenured faculty agreed or strongly agreed. Among students, 54.8% of undergraduate, 68.7% of graduate students who were TAing, and 59% of graduate students who were not TAing either agreed or strongly agreed. • Proportions of respondents who agreed and strongly agreed were generally higher among those who identified with an equity seeking group (e.g. 72% of respondents living with a disability, 71.8% of religious minority respondents, 68.8% of Black respondents, 67.7% of LGBT+ respondents, 63.6% of low income respondents, 63.2% of language minority respondents agreed or strongly agreed). • International students agreed at a slightly higher rate than domestic students (69.7% of international students compared with 58.3% of domestic students agreed or strongly agreed).

Some themes raised in the comments section:

• Shared living spaces, making internet connectivity and focus challenging (e.g. "I have 8 other housemates living in the same house. Having 9 people at home all the time is not conducive for studying, plus having 9 people connected to the WiFi means Internet speeds slow to a crawl.") • Childcare duties for students and faculty (e.g. "I live in a one plus den apartment with a 2.5 year old and no outdoor space. My partner's job requires him to be on conference calls for the majority of the day, making it extremely difficult to manage childcare and online learning. Teachers were accommodating and welcomed having my toddler sit on my lap during classes when he needed extra attention from me, but this did not allow me to have focused attention on the lesson being delivered."). • Eldercare responsibilities (e.g. "I have elder care responsibilities. I feel that faculty with childcare responsibilities are (rightfully) given sympathy and support. However, those with elder care responsibilities are ignored (I note that this is not even mentioned in this question, despite the fact that COVID-19 impacts the elderly tremendously)."). • Lack of dedicated space to work at home (e.g. no desk).

### Question 9: Online teaching provides an inferior learning experience compared to in-person classes.

• This question elicited the highest proportion of respondents strongly agreeing, with 41.2% of respondents strongly agreeing and 27.8% agreeing, bringing the total of respondents who agreed to 69%. Only 7% of respondents strongly disagreed. • Among respondents, 66.9% of undergraduate students, 72.9% of graduate students who were TAing, 68.2% of graduate students who were not TAing, 81.3% of pre-tenure faculty, and 67.7% of tenured faculty agreed or strongly agreed. In contrast, 18.3% of undergraduate students, 10.5% of graduate students who were TAing, 18.2% of graduate students who were not TAing, 12.6% of pre-tenure faculty, 9.7% of tenured faculty disagreed or strongly disagreed. • Some equity seeking groups agreed or strongly agreed more than the average respondents (e.g. 75% of Black respondents, 70.6% of LGBT+ respondents, and 70.5% of low income respondents). • Responses were similar across all three campuses. • Responses were similar for international and domestic students. Some themes raised in the comments section:

• Comments were divergent in this section (e.g. "In fact, learning experience is better through online teaching" and "I'm proud of my lectures. I have great energy, am an excellent facilitator. I am not proud of my online lectures. They feel like low quality youtube rants." vs. "The inperson experience is a richer experience than online learning."). • Some commented that the statement was too general and it depended on the topic and accommodations. • Many expressed concerns and frustrations that students were being charged the same tuition fees for online learning (e.g. "As an international student, paying over $60K to watch previously recorded lectures, some even dating back to 2016, and the lack of interaction from our professors makes me think that my money is going to waste."). • Several noted a lack of access to professors (e.g. office hours, email responses, answering questions, etc.). • Many noted that the challenge was due to the fact that the courses were not designed to be online and did not incorporate online teaching pedagogies into their development.

Question 10: The transition to online learning caused me significant distress.

• 53% of respondents agreed or strongly agreed with the statement, while 24.1% disagreed or strongly disagreed. • There was a higher rate of respondents who agreed and strongly agreed among respondents who identified with some equity seeking groups, including: 80% of Black respondents, 66.7% of religious minority, 64.7% of LGBT+, and 64% of persons living with a disability, and 61.3% of low income respondents agreed or strongly agreed. • Distress was most common among faculty, as 81.3% of pre-tenure and 77.4% of tenured faculty agreed or strongly agreed with the statement. Among students, 53.1% of undergraduate, 41.7% of graduate students who were TAing, and 40.9% of graduate students who were not TAing agreed or strongly agreed with the statement.

Some themes raised in the comments section:

• For faculty, distress was most often the result of poor communication from departments, quick rollouts, and fears about the implications of their decisions for students. As one faculty member put it, "I felt I could not offer students a proper educational experience, which troubled me greatly". • Faculty members also worried about how to adapt particular pedagogical approaches to online platforms. As one respondent put it, "My teaching style emphasizes mutual learning amongst students, using breakout activities in lectures. This has received consistent positive reviews ---I have no idea how to transition this to online lectures in a meaningful way (I know several technical solutions, all of which seem inadequate)". • The transition was compounded by additional care responsibilities, which were shouldered overwhelmingly by faculty members (especially tenured) and caused stress (see other sections). • Many students reported distress in relation to the communication (or lack thereof) they received from their instructors. As one student wrote, "It was hard to have my questions answered on time. For example, I always need to email and wait for a reply etc. When I had classes in person I could ask questions before/after class I think instructors should still ensure office hours".

• Some respondents thought distress was too strong a word to use, as one put it, "I wouldn't say distress. Perhaps just a little bit of stress and added work to do. But distress, no, that's an emotion too far".

Question 11: Online learning hinders students' access to accessibility services

• 48.4% of respondents agreed or agreed strongly that accessibility services were compromised as a result of the shift to online learning. 8.5% of respondents disagreed or strongly disagreed. 43.1% of respondents had no opinion or did not agree or disagree. • Some equity seeking groups agreed or strongly agreed above the average respondents (e.g. 68% of persons living with a disability, 56.4% of religious minorities, 56.3% of Black respondents, 55.9% of LGBTQ+ respondents, 53.2% of persons of colour, 51.3% of women, and 50% of language minorities). • Responses were relatively evenly distributed amongst faculty and students.

Some themes raised in the comments section:

• This question generated a range of comments, many of which spoke to the mixed outcomes of online learning. Some respondents felt the answer was self-evident. As one respondent put it, "Obviously. The need for this question is laughable". • Several respondents said that Accessibility Services did not respond to the specific needs of particular students, such as simultaneous captioning for deaf students. • One TA commented that making individual accommodations online was more challenging, and done with little direction from instructors, departments, or university staff. • Several comments were provided about participation in online classes more generally, not about accessibility services specifically. For example, some respondents pointed out that students from minority backgrounds were more likely to speak in online classes. Others mentioned that it was easier for students for whom English is a second language to follow along in class, especially if classes were recorded. This was also true, some felt, for students who juggled work and/or care responsibilities with school. Some respondents therefore commented that online classes were helpful because they allowed them to attend on their own terms, while still fulfilling other obligations.

Question 12: Online teaching has undermined the University's stated commitment to equity and diversity.

• 42% of respondents agreed or strongly agreed, while 35% of respondents had no opinion or did not agree or disagree, and 22.3% of respondents disagreed or strongly disagreed. • Among equity seeking groups, agreement was highest among LGBT+ respondents (58.8%), persons living with disability respondents (52%), Black respondents (50.1%), and religious minorities (48.7%). • Responses were for the most part evenly distributed amongst faculty and students. Faculty had slightly higher rates of agreement than the average (50% of pre-tenure faculty and 48.4% of tenured faculty agreed or strongly agreed).

Some themes raised in the comments section:

• Comments were mixed for this question. Some respondents believed that online teaching actually fulfilled the commitment to equity and diversity. One faculty member reported that minority students were more likely to contribute in class during online sessions. Others mentioned that online learning broke down economically and geographically rooted barriers to education, allowing students to participate in ways they previously had not been able. • Most of the concern about undermining the university's commitment was concerned with community members living with disabilities, who may face particular challenges with online learning platforms.

### Further Context:

Question 13: My internet access at home is:

• 27.1% of respondents described their internet at home as "excellent", 39.3% as "good", 28.9% as "satisfactory", 4.3% as "poor", and 0.4% as "very poor". Therefore, a third (33.6%) of respondents rated their internet as satisfactory or lower.

• Internet quality was lower than the average (satisfactory, poor, or very poor) among the following equity seeking groups: persons living with disability (48%), and LGBT+ respondents (50%), and low-income respondents (37.2%), language minorities (36.9%), and religious minorities (35.9%). • Graduate students had slightly lower rates of internet quality and were more likely to describe their internet as satisfactory or lower (35.5% of graduate students TAing, 37.1% of graduate students not TAing) than undergraduate students (32.2%) or faculty (31.3% of pre-tenure faculty and 29.1% of tenured faculty). • Domestic students tended to have better internet quality than international students (51.5% of international students as opposed to 69.1% of domestic students described their internet as excellent or good).

Question 14: Do you have access to a desktop or laptop computer?:

• 98.6% of respondents answered "yes" to this question. The only respondents who answered "no" were all domestic students. • As has been mentioned elsewhere, the main issues with technological access seemed to revolve around internet access, the quality of technology available, and the surrounding learning environment. The comments in question 7, for instance, note that although a student may have access to a laptop, the quality of their laptop (for example, camera) or internet may hinder their ability to use online platforms and software for learning.

Question 15: Have you taken on additional care responsibilities as a consequence of the Coronavirus pandemic?

• 47.3% of respondents took on additional care responsibilities.

• Additional care responsibilities were most common for tenured faculty members (74.2%) and pre-tenure faculty (56%). However, many students also shouldered new responsibilities, with 45.8% of graduate students (TAing), 43.5% of graduate students (not TAing) and 40.4% of undergraduate students answering "Yes". • "Yes" responses were higher than the average among some equity seeking groups, including: 60% of persons living with disability, 55.8% of low income respondents, 53.3% of Black respondents, 51.3% of religious minorities, 50% of language minorities, and 49.7% of women. Some themes raised in the comments section:

• Some respondents reported dedicating significant time to caring for family members outside Toronto virtually. As one respondent put it, "I have family who experience much harder circumstances in Turkey. The emotional labour that goes into those relationships and the urge to help financially is exhausting".

Question 16: Have you experienced a drop in income as a result of the Coronavirus pandemic?

• 51.6% of respondents experienced a drop in income.

• Particular equity seeking groups were disproportionately impacted. For example, 74.4% of low income and 73.3% of Black respondents reported that their income had dropped. 64.1% of religious minorities also experienced a fall in income. • The majority of students experienced a drop in income. Undergraduates were most likely to experience a drop in income, with 64.9% of students answering "Yes". Among faculty, 19.4% of tenured faculty and 31.3% of pre-tenured faculty experienced a drop in income. • Respondents from UTSC had the highest rate of drop in income (56.7% respondent yes, compared to 48.6% of UTSG respondents).

Some themes raised in the comments section:

• Many reported that announcements in relation to CESB have taken away a high degree of uncertainty and stress in relation to income. • Several concerns were raised in relation to tuition rates, often with regard to the anticipated quality of education in the fall term. Respondents did not feel that it was fair to keep rates constant. If incomes have dropped significantly across the student population, tuition will be comparatively less affordable.

Question 17: If you are dependent on others, have they suffered a drop in income because of the Coronavirus pandemic?

• 42.2% of respondents answered "yes", 34.3% answered "no", and 23.5% answered "prefer not to say/not applicable". • Religious minorities (64.1%), persons of colour (57%), language minorities (50%), and low-income respondents (46.5%) were most likely to have a dependent who experienced a drop in income. • Undergraduate students (64.6%) and graduate students (37.1%) were most likely to have the income of someone they depended on fall, compared to 26.7% of pre-tenured faculty and 9.7% of tenured faculty. • UTSC respondents were almost twice as likely to be dependent on someone who experienced a drop in income relative to UTSG students, with 55.8% of UTSC students and 31.5% of UTSG students reporting a drop in income of someone they were dependent on.

Respondents were asked to provide any additional comments, and the following themes were raised:

• Again, one of the major themes that emerged in this comment section was that students did not enjoy the shift to online learning. However, once again, many students enjoyed online classes if the professor was sensitive to the new challenges facing students, communicated clearly and regularly, and was trained well in using the online platform. One respondent mentioned that, before making decisions on how to proceed, two of their professors surveyed students about the prospective shifts, and adapted their approach accordingly. • Many students mentioned that it was not online classes per se that bothered them about shifting online, but access to campus life, communities, and networking, all of which fell by the wayside. They worried how this would continue to be a problem were courses to shift online completely. • Another theme that emerged pertained to fees for Fall courses. As one respondent put it, "Fees for online courses, absolutely without a doubt, must be reduced". However, the student's rationale was tied to an overall drop in incomes amongst students and their families, which they felt should be reflected in fees, not the quality of education they would receive. Other students, however, felt that the quality of online learning was intrinsically lower, and that tuition rates should reflect this. • One student commented that concerns around documentation for mental health shifted tremendously during the pandemic. As they describe, "there was a profound sense of trust between faculty, staff and students around their emotions, feelings and circumstances. I feel really happy about that since we were missing that prior to this situation". This was raised by several other students as well.
