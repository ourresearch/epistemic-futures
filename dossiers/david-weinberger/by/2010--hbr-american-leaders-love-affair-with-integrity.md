## Full text

Jack Welch, a humble chemical engineer, tells us in his memoir why he was able to make strategic decisions all across GE’s portfolio, from nuclear power to the nightly news. The essential character trait at the heart of Welch’s story is simple: integrity. Integrity was “our No. 1 value,” he writes in this book. “We never had a corporate meeting where I didn’t emphasize integrity in my closing remarks.” Integrity for Welch means much more than upholding an ethical code. In means staying true to who you are. That is the story behind Welch’s story. His mother taught him always to be who he is, and not to think he is more than he is. The chapters of his book are filled with frank admissions of his weaknesses and of the mistakes he’s made. But, he never thought he was better than he was, and he didn’t tolerate the butt kissers who tried to tell him otherwise. It is not just Welch who believes the secret to leadership is integrity. Business book after business book tells us the same thing. Warren Bennis: “One thing that has become clearer than ever to me is that integrity is the most important characteristic of a leader…” Carly Fiorina: “Leadership is about the integrity of one’s character…” The first of John C. Maxwell‘s The 21 Indispensable Qualities of a Leader is character, which includes integrity. He wrote: “Character Is More than Talk: Anyone can say that he has integrity, but action is the real indicator of character. You can’t argue against integrity. Everyone ought to have it. But how did it become the pinnacle quality for leaders? After all, when people point to the great leaders of the past — Churchill, Kennedy, Alexander the Great — they usually point to other qualities. Courage. Vision. Steadfastness. Sure, they have may also have been people of integrity, but that did not used to be the sine qua non of leadership. Was Genghis Khan a person of integrity? How did integrity become the key characteristic of leaders? I have a theory. I think there are two reasons. First, America puts its leaders in a tough spot. You have to be a special sort of person to deserve to be a leader, but American culture is resistant to people claiming to be special. If integrity qualifies you for leadership, then you can be special not because you are so talented or smart, but simply because you see yourself honestly. This enables American business leaders to humbly protest their ordinariness — they are just like you and me — while justifying their being put in privileged position of leadership. The memoirs of modern American leaders are filled with this two-step tango. Second, when Welch talks about integrity, he doesn’t mean it in the post-Enron sense that he doesn’t take bribes. He means it as a type of knowing: You can look the facts in the eye and see them for what they are. Why? Because you know who you are and are not swayed by flattery, BS, or the temptation to see the world as you would like it to be. That’s a lesson that his mother taught him, Welch tells us. That’s a specially valuable type of knowing, because not even the smartest of the smart could ever know enough about a large corporation to be able to master all of its elements. Welch, for example, stresses that he’s just a chemical engineer, yet he ended up running a corporation that included businesses that made everything from toasters to nuclear power plants to television programs. Welch knows that he does not know as much as the dedicated managers of these units. Yet, he believes he’s able to do what he calls a “deep dive” into a business unit and see simple truths that the dedicated managers cannot. What enables him to do this? Integrity: His vision is unblemished by flattery and assumptions. There are times when this works: Welch shut down G.E.’s nuclear plant manufacturing business after Three Mile Island. There are times when it’s pretty unsettling, as when Welch does a deep dive into television news programming. But, it is based on a false and dangerous romanticism: The naive person, pure of heart, is able to see the truth that lifelong professionals cannot. One wonders how Welch would have reacted to a boss doing a deep dive into his own field of chemical engineering. So, here’s the second part of my theory: Integrity has become the central quality of modern leaders because modern leaders with any degree of self-awareness recognize that they don’t know — and can’t know — enough to be qualified to lead a large modern business. The business leader who justifies his or her position because of integrity is acknowledging his or her sense of inadequacy. Our culture’s lionization of this sort of integrity is a tacit recognition of just how far out of control American business is. This of course means the claim of integrity as the justification for a leadership position is self-delusional. (Shouldn’t every theory should end with an irony?)
