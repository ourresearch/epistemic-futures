# Spoiler alert! Why the Internet knows the ending before airtime

## Full text

It took the Internet about 3.5 seconds to figure out what happened to Glenn.

The Internet figured out the ending of "Mad Men" weeks before it happened.

The solution to how Jon Snow will come back was undoubtedly foretold on the Net even as he was drawing his last breath, although "Game of Thrones" is arbitrary enough in its rules — damn that magic — that it's hard to know which of the predicted plot tricks it will deploy.

Fans figured out from the title that "Lost" was going to be about purgatory (probably), forcing the writers to go down an arbitrary path and introducing new impossibilities that couldn't ever be adequately resolved and that required a fan-created site — Lostpedia — to be tracked.

This happens not because the writing is bad (although I'd argue that's the case with Glenn). It happens because networks (not The Networks) are smarter than individuals for three reasons:

First, any network of sufficient size is likely to include people as clever as the writers.

Second, a network can generate more ideas per second than even the best writers rooms.

Third, better ideas emerge from connected, engaged people with different ideas and points of view.

So networks generally are going to beat the story writers at their own game.

There are ways out of this conundrum, but not having fans talk about a series isn't one of them. Not. Gonna. Happen.

Here's one way out: Write clever one-shot episodes. That way only the viewers online at the same time can guess, and it seems that the real networking happens after the show has aired. (Make these episodes longer so that the plot can be more complicated and you just rescued movies. Well done!)

Then there's the opposite: very-long-form storytelling. "The Sopranos" long. These series often have three or more simultaneous layers of narrative: one covering the entire series, often character-driven (what will become of Tony and his dysfunctional family?); one covering a season's arc (how will Buffy defeat this season's Big Bad?); one for each particular episode (who'll win this episode's "The Good Wife" court case?). Each three of these series had all three arcs going on, as does "Orange is the New Black," "The Black List," "Scandal," and many others.

"Game of Thrones" takes a different narrative approach. It might not even resolve with a hero sitting on that pointy throne at the end. For all we know, Daenerys may get eaten by a dragon that then has Tyrion Lannister for dessert, leaving Littlefinger in charge. Even that could be too much resolution, since it would confirm one of the expected narrative arcs of such enterprises: Cynical Greedy Guy Wins. On the other hand, George R.R. Martin says he likes to foreshadow what will happen, which does not happen in life. For example, when Aeschylus was killed, the only foreshadowing was cast by the tortoise dropping onto his head.

So, maybe "Game of Thrones" will imitate life and something will happen because something always has to happen, the same way we might elect Jeb! Bush rather than the more narrative-worthy Donald Trump; President Trump would obviously be a disaster, but it'd make a much better story. So, one way out of the Network of Clever Narratives is to give up on stories. Stuff happens. There's sound and fury. It signifies nothing.

Perhaps that's why for the past decade we've heard so much warmly enthusiastic talk about "stories" and "storytelling." Now that the magic has been taken out of their creation, we are more desperate than ever to believe that life has the coherence and satisfaction of a well-told tale.

Except it doesn't. Not for most of us. You may think you're the gorgeous gallant hero who bravely says, "Today is not the day I die," but 10 minutes later your head may be crushed by a mountain. Or a turtle. That's just how the place works.

David Weinberger is a senior researcher at Harvard University's Berkman Center for Internet and Society.
