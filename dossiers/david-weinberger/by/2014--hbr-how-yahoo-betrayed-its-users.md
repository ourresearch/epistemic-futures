# How Yahoo Betrayed Its Users by Doing What They Asked

_Understanding the outrage of Flickr users._

## Full text

Customers of Yahoo’s Flickr photo sharing service should not have been surprised when Yahoo announced it would be selling prints of their photos. In fact they should have been pleased. After all, Yahoo is only selling photos that the users themselves have explicitly licensed for that purpose. And yet, to many of us it felt like a betrayal. The reason for that feeling tells us about a fundamental divide companies ignore at their peril. Before its acquisition by Yahoo, Flickr had shown itself to be an upstanding citizen of the internet by, among other things, giving its users the option to apply a Creative Commons license to the photos they posted there. Creative Commons licenses grant permission for content to be reused without explicitly securing the copyright holder’s consent beforehand. Flickr lets its members choose one of two types of CC license: either the re-user has to include attribution (CC-BY) or also has to refrain from using the photos commercially (CC-BY-NC). If the work is under a CC-BY-NC license, the owner doesn’t have to worry that some global polluter is going to make a billion dollars selling pro-pollution T-shirts emblazoned with her photo. Either way, a CC license brings advantages to the three stakeholders: For the photo owner: her work can now circulate much more freely. For the internet: the photo joins the tens of millions of photos available for re-use at Flickr, many of them high quality. For Flickr: new users come to find a photo and stay because they like the place. It also builds good will for Flickr. Yahoo has disrupted this virtuous triangle by selling “wall art” prints of any of the fifty million CC-BY licensed photos at Flickr. They’re charging $100 for this, although it’s half off now. How much of this money is going to the creators of these photos? None. How much money is Yahoo obliged to give the creators? None. The prints completely fulfill the CC-BY license by including a tag with the creator’s name on it. So what’s the problem? The first thing to recognize is that there is a problem. At least some of us feel Yahoo is taking advantage of us. What Yahoo is doing just isn’t what we had in mind when we put our photos under the CC-BY license. We were trying to contribute to our culture in some small way. We were participating in the gift economy. We were sharing. Now Yahoo has inserted itself into our enchanted triangle. The connections among the vertices of that triangle had been characterized by sharing and goodwill. But Yahoo has turned our photos − our gifts − into commodities and reduced them to their cash value. The sense of betrayal is made worse by the fact that Yahoo owns Flickr. After all, under the terms of the CC-BY license, any third party could harvest photos from Flickr and offer them for resale. We would probably write that off as a consequence of choosing the CC-BY license. But when it’s Flickr’s parent that does it, the experience goes from feeling exploited to feeling betrayed. And that gets at the essence of it. We thought Flickr was one of us: a company that shares the best values of the web. But, nope. It’s now just part of another megacorp that only cares about one thing, and that thing is not us. Now, you may well ask why I chose CC-BY rather than CC-BY-NC. Excellent question. I can’t speak for anyone else, but for a work that I think might actually have some commercial value, I often do choose CC-BY-NC. For works that seem slighter, or that I want to spread more easily, I choose CC-BY in part because I don’t understand CC-BY-NC. What exactly counts as a commercial use? For example, does it prevent a blog that accepts advertising from using one of my photos? How about someone incorporating a photo into a presentation for which she gets expenses and an honorarium? I don’t know, and that is an impediment to my slapping the NC onto my CC licenses. So, I generally go with CC-BY, and am willing to lose some farfetched commercial value. I therefore have no rational complaint against Yahoo. I nevertheless feel wounded and betrayed. The fact that many of us feel that way even though Yahoo’s actions are in accordance with the license we adopted illustrates the chasm carved by the mighty rivers of the Internet: Some companies are on our side and some are not. Being on our side means not doing everything the fine print may allow, but instead thinking first about what your customers actually want, how they actually feel, how they are treating one another, what is in their interests…and then making common cause with them. Yahoo ignored all of this. In doing so, it revealed not only that it isn’t one of “us”, but that it doesn’t know there is an “us” at all.
