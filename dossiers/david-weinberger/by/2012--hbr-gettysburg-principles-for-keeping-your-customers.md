## Full text

Jeff Vogel is managing to make a pretty good living as an indie game developer, even though his work is easily pirated. That’s likely because he’s come up with an effective anti-piracy technique that he explains in a section of Principles of an Indie Game Bottom-Feeder, subtitled “Piracy is a Thing — Give Up.” Rather than encumbering his company’s offerings with DRM (digital rights management) to keep them from being copied, his post tells you how to illegally download them. “Do you think I’m dumb for revealing this secret? Well, it wasn’t a secret,” he writes. “Everybody who isn’t really old knows how to get all the free music, games, books, and movies they want. . . . It’ll get worse before it gets better.” Then he adds: “Oh. Wait. It won’t get better. Ever.” Given that a hit title for his company, Spiderweb Software, reaches merely toward double-digit thousands of sales, how does he manage to stay afloat? “I don’t really make a living selling games. I sell an ethical life.” He writes: So what does someone get when they pay for my game? They get the knowledge that they are Part of the Solution and not Part of the Problem. They know that, in this case, they are one of the Good Guys. It is well-earned self-satisfaction, and it is valuable. To know they are doing the right thing, some people will happily pay 20 bucks. This is how I stay in business. Vogel counsels: “The goal for an indie developer is to get people to like you. If they don’t want to help you stay around, they will help someone else.” I think Vogel is right about how to fight piracy, but I would put it a little differently. It’s not so much about being an ethical vendor or one that people like. Rather, in the online world, it’s about whether your company meets the Gettysburg principles: Is your business of the people, by the people, and for the people? Of the people means that the company is one of us. Does it share the same values, treat its employees like full-bodied individuals, recognize its own fallibility, have a sense of humor? Jeff Vogel’s Spiderweb Software is clearly of the people. Ben and Jerry’s meets those criteria pretty handily as well. By the people means that we had a hand in creating it. Spiderweb Software is not by the people. But Wikipedia is. And when over 87,000 people kicked in more than $3.3 million dollars at Kickstarter to fund a new game by Tim Schafer, that game took by the people to a whole new level. For the people means that the company is completely focused on satisfying us, not just with its products, but with every aspect of its business. Apple stores are for the people. Sites that obscure their own content with pop-over ads are not. Obviously, your company doesn’t have to get all three right for your customers to feel so loyal to you that they’ll pay you for what they could get for free. For example, Vogel’s Spiderweb Software is of and for the people but not by the people, and it’s doing well. The comedian Louis CK’s $5 DRM-free downloadable video was for us, but not by us (we’re not as funny as he is), and wasn’t of us (he charmingly acknowledged he doesn’t really understand all this “torrent” stuff), yet he still managed to sell a million dollars’ worth in just a few days. These are not perfect points by which to triangulate what gets customers on your side. For one thing, they are each a continuum. For example, Woot.com is not by the people: it’s privately-owned, and users have no direct control over which items will be put forward as the daily deal. But Woot places customer comments prominently to the right of the item offering, above the fold. Those comments are honest and helpful in pointing out limitations of the item. That evaluative function is by the people, and it helps make Woot feel for the people. Having just one of the Gettsyburg trio is usually not enough to earn the sort of loyalty Jeff Vogel is talking about. Sometimes even having two is not enough: Amazon’s excellent customer service makes it for the people, and the openness and prominence it accords customer reviews puts it onto the by the people scale, but its size and corporate-ness makes it feel not much like of us, which means if someone comes along with better prices or better service, we’ll bolt. We the people supply the content to Yelp, but it doesn’t feel like it’s ours. Facebook’s content is clearly by the people, of course, but it doesn’t feel like it’s for us, or much of the people. But a service that hits all three as fully as Craigslist does engenders a loyalty that will keep people there even if a technically better service comes along. Google used to hit all three qualities. Its famously sparse search page told us that Google was all about serving our needs: if it didn’t help us users, Google wasn’t going to put it into our face. Same for its “Don’t be evil” motto and its lobbying for an open Internet. Its treatment of its employees declared that Google was of us, or at least of the dominant geek culture of the Web. And because its search results reflect the link structure that we the users of the Web have created, there’s a way in which it is by the people as well. But, as Google has larded up its pages with advertising, it seems more like it’s not for us but is trying to do something to us. Likewise, as Google has become a mega-corporation, it seems less like it’s of us. Reddit, on the other hand, is flying high in part because it is by us, it is for us, and it is of us. It may of course at any moment disastrously lose all of those characteristics, but right now it is engendering such extreme loyalty because it is staying true to the Gettysburg trilogy. Threadless, likewise, has hit all three Gettysburg marks: the designs for its T-shirts come from us and are voted for by us — those T-shirts are so much for us that they are signals of our us-ness — and the company’s transparency lets us see just how much of us it is. We’ll see if its careful move into mass distribution via Disney will disrupt its for- and of-ness. It is not an accident that these three characteristics are so important to building customer loyalty. They reflect the sense of many Web users that the Internet is ours. We built it. We created much of what gives it value. We are its medium: information passes through the internet because we choose to send it along, always investing a bit of our social selves in the process. The internet is ours, and if a business can’t actually be ours, at least it can be of us, for us, and by us in some meaningful way. If your business is not, customers will drift away on the slightest of currents. If it is, we’ll buy from you even when we’re just one click away from getting your stuff for free.
