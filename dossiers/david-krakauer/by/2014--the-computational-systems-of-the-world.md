# The Computational Systems of the World

## Full text

The Computational Systems of the World

Wetware: A Computer in Every Living Cell. Dennis Bray. Yale University Press, 2011. 280 pp., illus. $21.00 (ISBN 9780300167849 paper).

Probably Approximately Correct: Nature's Algorithms for Learning and Prospering in a Complex World. Leslie Valiant. Basic Books, 2013. 208 pp., illus. $26.99 (ISBN 9780465032716 cloth).

The Engine of Complexity: Evolution as Computation. John E. Mayfield. Columbia University Press, 2013. 384 pp., illus. $34.50 (ISBN 9780231163040 cloth).

David Krakauer

David Krakauer (dckrakauer{at}gmail.com) is the director of the Wisconsin Institute for Discovery, codirector of the Center for Complexity and Collective Computation,
a professor of genetics at the University of Wisconsin–Madison, and an external professor with the Santa Fe Institute, in
Santa Fe, New Mexico.

In 1832, the astronomer, mathematician, and chemist Sir John Herschel commented with approval on the significant contributions
that mathematical models had made in the physical sciences: “We no longer perceive the same shyness, on the part of our mathematical
champions, in entering on the great and vexed questions of the lunar and planetary perturbations, the theory of the tides,
and others relating to the system of the world” (Herschel 1832, p. 38). Herschel was reflecting on mathematical physics, which had produced “a wonderful result, that a brief and simple
sentence,… accompanied with a few determinate numbers, capable of being written down on half a sheet of paper, comprehends
within its meaning the history of all the complicated movements of our globe, and the mighty system to which it belongs” (Herschel
1832, p. 21).

A high degree of coherence between observation and theory had long been sought by natural philosophers to better organize
and unify a diverse and complicated reality. This ambition is elegantly captured by one of Charles Darwin's heroes, the philosopher
of science William Whewell, in the theory of the scientific method of 1858 (the Novum Organon Renovatum): “The system becomes more coherent as it is further extended. The elements which we require for explaining a new class of
facts are already contained in our system. In false theories, the contrary is the case.… Such a false theory was the ancient
doctrine of eccentrics and epicycles” (Butts 1968, p. 155).

Decades later, working in the geological and biological sciences, Darwin was assailed by a community that showed little reverence
for systems of the world, mathematics, or determinate numbers “capable of being written down on half a sheet of paper.” In
On the Origin of Species by Means of Natural Selection, Darwin (1861) expressed his deep frustration with those who questioned the value of a general system on the grounds that it failed to
account for every fact and figure. Darwin cited the exemplary reception of physical theory described by Herschel and Whewell
as a prequel and precedent to synthetic ideas in biology: “It is no valid objection that science as yet throws no light on
the far higher problem of the essence or origin of life. Who can explain what is the essence of the attraction of gravity?
No one now objects to following out the results consequent on this unknown element of attraction” (Darwin 1861, p. 514).

Over 150 years later, we have yet to discover a quantitative, predictive framework for the emergence of biology. Evolution
by natural selection, articulated in the form of one long narrative argument, has proven surprisingly powerful (with necessary
mathematical augmentations to include neutral processes and various aspects of development) in accounting for a wide range
of natural observations. But something analogous to the predictive power of mathematics in physics has proved elusive, and
this has led some to argue that the complexity of biology is irreducible—incapable of efficient compact representation and
therefore condemned to perpetual description. There are too many sources of randomness and too great a dependence on history
for the regularities to be clearly revealed in evolved structure. This is illustrated by the growth of a field that I like
to call computational natural history—complex, quantitative visualizations of biological processes represented as networks of thousands of components or agents.
This is computation without computational theory, quantification without compression; it is Linnaeus on an iPad.

But something is now happening. There are epistemological forces accumulating toward synthesis, and they are arriving with
increasing frequency. Three books—Wetware: A Computer in Every Living Cell by Dennis Bray, Probably Approximately Correct: Nature's Algorithms for Learning and Prospering in a Complex World by Leslie Valiant, and The Engine of Complexity: Evolution as Computation by John E. Mayfield—each claim to varying degrees that the info-quake is upon us. Each author presents examples or formalisms
that might contribute to building the rigorous foundations that biology has long sought—foundations not grounded in physics
but in computation. It is not the world of conservation laws and single lines capturing the conformability of nature but a
world of procedural logic and computational complexity.

All of these books have their origins in the work of Norbert Wiener, John von Neumann, Alan Turing, and Claude Shannon. This
quartet of the 1950s has emerged as the harbinger of a new way of thinking about biology with an emphasis on learning and
evolution. A favorite style of theirs was to find means of connecting dynamics (i.e., mathematical descriptions of observables continuously varying in time) with logic and language. Dynamical systems
have provided a mathematical basis for many of the great revolutions in physics, from Newtonian classical mechanics, through
Einstein's theories of relativity, to theories of quantum mechanics. Logic and language, by contrast, deal with rules operating
with symbols, such as words or propositions, and provide a powerful framework for deduction in both mathematics and analytical
philosophy and, of course, for the theory of information and computation.

Logic and language can be connected to dynamics by a procedure known as coarse-graining, replacing the continuum with discrete symbols that span a range of continuous values. This is analogous to the way we impose
color terms on the continuous spectrum of visible light or musical notation on smoothly propagating sound waves. Coarse-graining
implies approximation. For example, when I ask that someone pick a blue sphere from a set of red spheres, I do not need to
say, “Please select the azure sphere and not the crimson, scarlet, or rust ones.” For the purpose of discrimination, approximation
is good enough.

The reason for which this simple point is so important and so profound is that life communicates, and communication must work
quickly and reliably within a range of environments. These include the environment of the cell, in which proteins communicate;
the brain, where cells communicate; societies, in which individuals communicate; and digital networks, through which computers
communicate. Each of these agents uses communication to make favorable decisions in a reasonable amount of time—certainly
a shorter time than their respective life spans. The procedures for arriving at adaptive decisions based on approximate and
noisy information is what these authors mean by computation. Ascertaining how physical processes can be described in terms of symbols and rules and quantifying the efficiency with which
organisms use rules to compute adaptive behavior are what the science of biology will become. Biologists will become computational
scientists not because we use computers but because we see how biology computes.
Ascertaining how physi­cal processes can be described in terms of symbols and rules and quantifying the efficiency with which
organisms use rules to compute adaptive behavior are what the science of biology will become.

In Wetware, neurobiologist and systems biologist Dennis Bray provides a highly approachable and well-informed account of communication
and computation at the cellular level. He presents not just “a computer in every living cell,” as the subtitle states, but
something far more impressive: millions of computers in every living cell. Defending himself against charges of anthropomorphism
(an insult often leveled at arguments that ascribe memory and logic to microscopic realms), Bray provides numerous portraits
of both protein and cell behavior, which, if they were observed at an organismal level, would be deemed examples of both communication
and decisionmaking.

A chapter is presented on signaling among proteins—in particular, signal transduction pathways dominated by kinase enzymes.
Communication through these pathways can be described in the language of dynamics, in which concentrations of proteins vary
in time, and also in the language of logic, through which elementary decisions and computations can be discerned. The dynamical
models give us access to a detailed quantitative description of the cell, and the coarse-grained computational language provides
a logical explanation of what these dynamics achieve in terms of adaptive function.

Bray is passionate about the cognitive power of cells, including their sensory and memory properties. Single cells, such as
Amoeba proteus or Escherichia coli, can sense variation in their environment, commit base-level resources to memory, and navigate toward high concentrations
required for growth and reproduction. A chapter is dedicated to describing the internal circuitry (i.e., the molecular networks)
within the cell that enables bacteria to both sense and forage for food in a range of different environments. Like all animals,
bacteria possess basic perceptual, representational, and decisionmaking mechanisms. The reason we do not speak of bacterial
perception has nothing to do with their capabilities and everything to do with our general reluctance to ascribe computation
(“thought”) to anything below or above the size of the brain. It also has to do with our matter bias, which accepts neurons
as decisionmaking units but disowns proteins or non-carbon-based devices. Bray seeks to address these prejudices with a chapter
on robots.

Wetware is fundamentally about ­collectives. It is about the way smart macromachines are made up of less smart mesomachines that
are, themselves, made up of even less smart micromachines. I call this framework cognitive ubiquity, and I agree wholeheartedly with Bray that the absence of cognitive ubiquity is why most engineered computers are often underwhelming
when it comes to behavior. Computational life is deeply nested, with memory and induction present at numerous scales of organization.
We still do not know how low or small our target can be before we lose sight of these abilities. To quote Feynman (1992) on the topic of quantum computing, I believe that “there is still plenty of room at the bottom.”

Probably Approximately Correct offers a formal model of computation for phenomena of the kind that is surveyed in Wetware. As a computer scientist and theorist, Leslie Valiant favors a more rigorously quantitative approach to biological computation,
beginning where Whewell and Herschel left off, declaring, “Those aspects of knowledge for which there is a good predictive
theory will be called theoryfull. The rest will be called theoryless.… I am impressed [by] how effectively life forms ‘cope’ with the theoryless in a complex world.… [This kind of] learning
is done by concrete mechanisms that can be understood by the methods of computer science” (p. 2). These methods of computer
science are extensions of the theory of computation pioneered by Turing and von Neumann and more recently in the fields of
statistical learning theory and machine learning. The overall approach is to provide a framework for natural forms of learning
and adaptation in which the process can take place within a modest number of steps and in which generalization is limited
to familiar environments (i.e., contextual and approximate computation rather than universal and ideal).

Valiant's background in computer science draws him toward simple classification problems that can be formalized in terms of
Boolean logic. Throughout the book, elegant examples are provided of ecorithms (the term Valiant uses for natural-learning algorithms) that solve classification problems with rather limited prior knowledge
of the environment. The conditions that are required so that this class of probably approximately correct (PAC) algorithms
will work are set forth: a static (or at least slowly changing) environment, access by the organism to environmental regularities,
and modest computational resources for solving these problems. The modesty factor corresponds to the technical concept of
computational complexity, in which the number of steps necessary to reach a reasonable solution is in proportion to the size
of the problem. A chapter is dedicated to explaining this idea with simple arithmetical examples, and for those who have not
encountered these ideas, Valiant provides a valuable introduction. His argument for the “curse” of exponential time algorithms
(as opposed to the polynomial-time PAC algorithms) and their unsuitability for life and thought is very convincing.

Valiant is on the right path in arguing for a new approximate model of computing intrinsic to biological phenomena. Probably Approximately Correct is both complementary to and disruptive of the current dynamical-systems approach to evolution. In his position, he follows
Weiner, von Neumann, and Shannon, all of whom made similar arguments and each of whom went on to generate very powerful and
useful frameworks for biology, including control theory, information theory, and fault-tolerant information processing. My
one disappointment with the book is the rather stark disparity between the ambition of the opening claims and the limited
exploration and application of the PAC models. Valiant observes that “Darwin presented only the outline of a mechanism… not
specific enough to be subject to a quantitative analysis or to a computer simulation. [It] can make no quantitative predictions
as far as the number of generations needed for the evolution of a behavior of a certain complexity” (p. 17). There is certainly
much truth in this. Nothing in Darwin's narrative provides a tool for calculating how long it would take for an ammonite to
emerge from a state of free-living unicells or for the emergence of sex from asexuality, but neither is there anything in
PAC computation that can do this. Indeed, there is not one biological example of calculating evolutionary time in this book,
not even for digital organisms. And to be fair to Darwin, his ideas were specific enough to inform mathematical population
genetics and numerous simulation frameworks for evolution—from genetic algorithms to artificial life. I look forward to seeing
how the PAC process can be applied to biological problems that have remained, so far, in the domain of the “theoryless.”

The Engine of Complexity is the most expansive and philosophical of these three books and is largely a survey of the surprising breadth of Darwinian
thinking. Mayfield has chapters on genetics, neuroscience, psychology, economics, and computer simulation. In this respect,
the book similar to Melanie Mitchell's (2009) Complexity and Murray Gell-Mann's (1995) earlier work, The Quark and the Jaguar. It is, however, less rigorous but more broad than these two books.

Mayfield argues that the reason Darwinian thinking is so powerful is that it is not fundamentally about biology. Rather, it
is an algorithmic perspective with roots in the field of computing and information theory. This is not something that we should
expect Darwin to have known, even though Darwin's model, based on artificial selection, is a wonderful example of an informal
ecorithm. Without explicitly stating it, Mayfield advocates for a framework much like Valiant's PAC approach although not
through a rigorous mathematical model but through weight of example. The “engine of complexity” is simply the joint mechanism
of heredity, variation, and selection at multiple scales. Where Valiant does not worry too much about the details of hardware,
software, and programming, these are the topics that Mayfield spends a great deal of space reflecting on: “If physical processes
compute, what and where are the programs?… Initial conditions and the laws of physics and chemistry [may] serve as inputs
to the ‘computation,’ [but]… there is no predesigned program, no instructions other than the rules in play, [yet] these inputs
are sufficient to result in significant structure” (p. 90). These speculations are often interesting, but I did wish that
the book had been shorter. Much of the empirical material—from immune systems to nervous systems—is available elsewhere.

Taken together, these three books span the gamut of information-­processing machines as described by the computational biologist
David Marr in his book Vision (2010, originally published in 1982). For Marr, any computational theory required a clear statement of function (specification
of a correct or desired input–­output behavior), an elucidation of the mechanisms or physical implementation required to support
the behavior, and the algorithms that would operate by coarse-graining to yield a desired computation. By approximation, Bray
focuses on the mechanisms, Valiant on the algorithms, and Mayfield on the functions. Like members of a musical trio, they
benefit from being heard as an ensemble. As the American composer Charles Ives once wrote, “The fabric of existence weaves
itself whole.”
