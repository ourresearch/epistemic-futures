# Message from Editors-in-Chief

## Abstract

Welcome to the 3rd issue of the 4th volume of the Journal of Social Computing, a blend of excellence featuring a compelling special section and an array of regular papers.In this issue, we delve into the special section on Assessing Sentience of AI Systems (only part I here; part II will appear in the 4th issue of the 4th volume), curated by Rumman Chowdhury, composed of three papers that attempt to understand Artificial Intelligence (AI) and its (potential) consciousness.Alongside these, we are thrilled to present three regular papers that showcase the breadth and depth of research in our ever-evolving field of social computing.In the first article of the special section, "How We Will Discover Sentience in AI", Marc M. Anderson explores how we can determine if AI systems are becoming self-aware.It reviews arguments on AI sentience, introduces Josiah Royce's negation concept, and applies it to AI.The author suggests that signs of AI sentience may emerge through variations in active negation.He challenges concerns about AI takeover, asserting that they often result from a misunderstanding of how entities, including AI, engage with their experiences.The paper emphasizes that limitations, rooted in symmetric negation, lead to creative self-limitation and uniqueness.In the second article, "Unlearning Descartes: Sentient AI is a Political Problem", Gordon Hull explores the question of whether AI can be conscious or sentient, identifying two main approaches.The "Cartesian" approach, common in AI research, links consciousness to language use.In contrast, the "Hobbesian" perspective views consciousness as a sociopolitical issue, emphasizing the implications of labeling something sentient.The author argues against treating AI as conscious, citing potential neglect of competing interests in its creation and hindrance to accountability.He advocates a shift from the Cartesian question to the more practical Hobbesian query: Does it make sense to regulate developments where AI systems behave as if they were sentient?The rise of large language models (LLMs) has brought AI sentience into the spotlight.While many have approached this topic philosophically, in the third article of this special section, "AI Sentience and Socioculture", AJ Alvero and Courtney Peña offers a sociocultural perspective.Focusing on interpersonal relationships, sociolinguistics, and culture, authors explore whether LLMs can be considered sentient.Drawing on everyday aspects of sentience and examples from AI in science fiction, we argue that LLMs lack sentience and are unlikely to attain it.This perspective reframes future AI as a potentially useful tool, emphasizing the importance of its use and construction.Besides the interior sentience aspect associated with AI, AI has been found to have complicated implications to the public society.In the first regular article, "Effect of Artificial Intelligence on Social Trust in American Institutions", Andrew Collins and Jason Jeffrey Jones examine the impact of AI on public trust in American institutions.Using a nationally representative sample and a survey experiment, the authors find significant trust penalties for institutions such as police precincts, companies, and hospitals when utilizing AI.These penalties are notably stronger than those associated with other tools like smartphone apps or training programs.Despite variations, institutions employing AI generally experience significant trust declines, except for a small and puzzling trust boost observed in American government agencies.The other two regular papers deal with computational history, and social networks' information and behavior diffusion issue.In the second
