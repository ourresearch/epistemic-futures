# Mobilizing Conceptual Spaces: How Word Embedding Models Can Inform Measurement and Theory Within Organization Science

## Full text

## Abstract

Theory within organizational research has increasingly mobilized the notion of conceptual spaces-multidimensional spaces within which shared concepts relate, combine, disperse or compete with one another.We here show how conceptual spaces can be empirically represented and measured through word embedding models, a recent advance in natural language processing and machine learning that now permits scholars to efficiently encode complex systems of meanings with less semantic distortion than before.In so doing, we have a twofold aim.First, to provide the theoretical notion of conceptual spaces with a rigorous and scalable representation and measurement framework, and second, to provide the word embedding family of computational models with a stable conceptual framework upon which its output can be interpreted with theoretical significance by organizational scientists.We start by laying out an explicit definition of conceptual spaces and illustrating their key features.We then describe how embedding models serve as a powerful framework for representing them, describing how these models work and walking the reader through the code required to implement them.Drawing on recent work within organization science and neighboring social sciences, we then proceed to illustrate what we see as a promising set of conceptual space measures, which allow us to theorize and formally trace theoretical constructs based on geometric notions of proximity and distance, breadth and depth, similarity, dimensionality, and hierarchy.Through practical code implementations of these measures, we illustrate how conceptual spaces as represented in embedding models can fruitfully open new and expand existing theoretical terrain in a range of fields and increase the fidelity of Organization science has long relied on conceptual spaces-multidimensional spaces in which concepts ranging from norms and knowledge to ideas and inventions relate to one another.The knowledge-based view of the firm relies on differential coverage of this space by firms in a market (Grant 1996), organizational theorists imply such a space when they describe shifts in norms and institutions (Scott 2003), categories scholars invoke them when deciding which concept an object is to be categorized under (Pontikes and Barnett 2015), innovation scholars directly theorize and seek to measure such spaces in accounting for combinatorial discovery and invention (Fleming andSorenson 2001, 2004), and teams researchers seek to understand how members' diverse positions within the space shape creativity, coordination, and performance (Srikanth et al. 2016).Until recently, however, the implicit notion of conceptual spaces was an underspecified metaphor (Hannan et al. 2019, Hofstadter andSander 2013).

Conceptual spaces were never formally theorized and rarely assessed in a way that accounted for the complexities of conceptual relation required to make sense of individual and collective actions that took place atop them.

Yet, insofar as "good theories work… because of their close connection to empirical observation…, strong theory does not exist without method, and our job as scholars is to make that connection explicit" (Lawrence 1997, p. 20).Consider the exponential growth of knowledge that has taken place within neuroscience, cognitive science, and psychology with the advent of functional neuroimaging (Savoy 2001) and associated models and algorithms (Stephan and Roebroeck 2012).Before the 1990's, scientists were limited to understanding the functions of different brain regions by studying lesions caused by disease or trauma, or by directly stimulating exposed cortical tissue during brain surgery (Savoy 2001), leading to a coarse-grained understanding of brain region functions.However, the introduction of functional magnetic resonance imaging along with associated techniques for modeling the underlying brain activity data (e.g., dynamic causal models and Granger-causality models) led to deeper and more fine-grained understanding of regional brain activity and laid the foundation for studying neuronal connectivity and the causal influences that neural units exert on each other (Stephan and Roebroeck 2012).The consequence of this new set of fine-grained brain representations led to significant scientific advancement in fields as diverse as biology, physiology, pharmacology, cognitive science, neuroscience, and others.

Similarly, as brain imaging technology has shed light on the black-box of brain structure and function, word embedding models are opening the black-box of conceptual spaces from language.In this light, we agree with Hannigan et al. (2019), who note that "new methods can have profound impacts on management scholarship (Arora et al. 2016), as they enable scholars to take fresh approaches to theory and reexamine previously intractable problems and classical questions (Timmermans and Tavory 2012)."

We here propose that organization science stands to undergo theoretical growth and reformulation spawned by the ability to empirically represent conceptual spaces in fine-grained detail through the use of word embedding models and their extensions including document, hyperbolic, and contextual embeddings.These models, built on efficient neural network architectures, have begun to revolutionize semantic analysis by enabling the efficient encoding of complex systems of meanings into dense geometric spaces with minimal distortion based on a recursive operationalization of linguist J. R. Firth's dictum "you shall know a word by the company it keeps" (Firth 1957).Such models represent meanings in spaces of tens to hundreds of dimensions, low relative to the number of words in a language or linguistic contexts in which those words reside, but very high-dimensional relative to the two or three in which formal social and cultural theorists previously sought to render conceptual spaces (Osgood 1964, Smith-Lovin andHeise 1988).

These embedding models create a 'digital double' of the collective knowledge held by individuals within a social system; they can solve culturally implicit analogies (Mikolov, Sutskever, et al. 2013), answer culturally contingent questions (Devlin et al. 2018), generate culturally appropriate discourse (Sabharwal and Agrawal 2020), and predict future knowledge discoveries (Tshitoyan et al. 2019).As two artificial intelligence pioneers note in their encyclopedic textbook on the topic, "one of the most significant findings to emerge from the application of deep learning to language tasks is that a great deal of mileage comes from re-representing individual words as vectors in a high-dimensional space-so-called word embeddings" (Russell and Norvig 2020, p. 834).One would be hard-pressed to find a current language-based application (e.g., language translation, voice assisted digital assistant, chatbot, web search) that does not employ this class of models somewhere within its implementation pipeline.Against this backdrop, a central claim of our paper is that just as brain imaging tools led to a more fine-grained representation of brain activity and a deeper understanding of brain function and its consequences, word embedding tools now enable us to represent conceptual spaces at scale across domains of human action and in much more fine-grained detail than has been possible before.In this light, we will show how embedding models operationalize conceptual spaces in domains relevant to organization scientists as well as put forward several measures that organization scientists can use to both extend and refine existing theory.

We begin by laying out an explicit definition of concepts and conceptual spaces and illustrating their key features.We then illustrate how implicit notions of conceptual spaces have been used in prior research, illustrating this use within the literatures on categories, knowledge, and culture.With concepts and conceptual spaces defined, we provide a brief overview of the evolution of computational text analysis tools used by organization scientists (focusing on dictionaries, semantic networks, and topic models), showing how each subsequent tool helped to ameliorate the limitations of tools that came before.

We then proceed to describe how embedding models serve as a powerful framework for representing conceptual spaces, describing how they are able to overcome limitations of prior tools.In so doing, we guide the reader through the process of implementing a word embedding model using a corpus of patent abstracts.We explain the many model parameters that need to be set by the researcher and walk the reader through validation steps that researchers should take to ensure that their model is effectively representing their conceptual space of interest.Our goal with this section on validation procedures is to show the tight coupling between the conceptual regularities humans carry in their minds and the stable patterns captured by the model, suggesting that these models are effective tools for representing conceptual spaces.

We then proceed to show how embedding models enable theorization and measurement of constructs reliant on concepts and conceptual spaces.We put forward embedding measures across three broad categories.In the first category, which we label within/between measures, we put forward two measures that trace conceptual relationships within and between relevant analytic sets: (1) conceptual breadth and (2) conceptual distance and similarity.In the second category, meaning and its dimensions, we present four measures that provide distinct windows onto meaning and its relation to organizations: (3) conceptual similarity, (4) conceptual meaning, (5) conceptual positions within cultural and knowledge continua, and (6) conceptual dimensionality.In the third category we present some emerging horizons as sources of new measurement possibilities, including: (7) hyperbolic embeddings, (8) contextual embeddings, and (9) embeddings beyond words.To draw out the theoretical possibilities of each of these new measurement opportunities and note limitations, we highlight emerging research within organization science and neighboring disciplines that has begun to use these models and measures.Further, to draw out the practical applications of the measures, we provide a methodological appendix with the requisite code to implement each of the measures in the first and second categories.Our purpose with these illustrations is to exemplify the broad research areas across which these new conceptual space representations can be useful.We expect that this new family of conceptual space representations will continue the theoretical expansion within organization science that has been facilitated by computational text analysis in recent years (Goldberg et al. 2016, Guo et al. 2020, Hannigan et al. 2019, Kovács et al. 2013).

When we think, what are we thinking about?And if we want to share our thoughts with others, what are we communicating with?It is generally agreed that concepts are a fundamental feature of human life, with much of our daily thinking reliant on the information they represent, allowing us to categorize the people, objects, and events around us as well as communicate this information to others (Bergen and Feldman 2008, p. 314, Casasanto and Lupyan 2015, Murphy 2002).Concepts, then, are the glue that holds our mental world together (Murphy 2002).We integrate each new experience and draw meaning from it through concepts.We communicate about our experiences through concepts.And we express our unfolding future plans in terms of concepts.

Concepts, and by extension the spaces within which they relate to other concepts, are fundamental to human thinking and communication (Hofstadter and Sander 2013, Murphy 2002, Sperber and Wilson 1986).This omnipresence means that concepts and conceptual spaces have been used at different levels of explicitness and importance within a broad array of organizational theory frameworks.

Within some frameworks (e.g., categories research) concepts are of central importance and have therefore been explicitly theorized and considered.Within other frameworks, however, (e.g., the knowledge-based view of the firm) concepts are implicitly assumed as building-blocks of the framework and do not take on an explicit role even though they are the underlying constituent parts that determine many of the theoretical expectations.Given the omnipresence of concepts, it is beyond the scope of this paper to provide a comprehensive review of all fields within organization science that have used concepts and conceptual information in important ways. 4Nevertheless, we believe it necessary to suggest some ways in which concepts have appeared in the literature.We therefore focus our brief review on three fields that we see as having engaged in important ways with notions of concepts and conceptual spaces-categories, knowledge, and culture.We focus on these fields because they grapple with conceptual information as it exists in the minds of individuals and communities and with tracing the effects that this conceptual information can have for organizational behaviors and outcomes.As with broad reviews in this vein, the covered literatures "should be seen as illustrative of the connections, but not exhaustive" (Csaszar and Steinberger 2022, p. 2).We orient the reader by highlighting the focus of each of these fields and how this focus relates to our explicit notions of concepts and conceptual spaces.

Categories are groupings of entities with shared characteristics and attributes.As noted above, concepts are the mental representations of categories.Research on categories has focused on whether spanning or ambiguous categories increase or decrease the valuation of categorized entities.

Work since Zuckerman (1999) has focused on disambiguating conditions under which categorical spanning and ambiguous classification leads to positive or negative valuation outcomes.Many studies have shown that spanning categories or ambiguous categories hurt the valuations of entities in them due to perceived deviance (Durand et al. 2007), poor fit with audience expectations (Hsu 2006, Hsu et al. 2009, Kovács et al. 2013, Leung and Sharkey 2014), or decreased categorical contrast (Negro et al. 2010).

Other work has shown that spanning categories creates positive valuation outcomes by showing that atypicality can amplify good performance and buffer bad (Smith 2011), that one category can anchor cognition while another beneficially modifies features of the first (Wry et al. 2014), or that moderate spanning was optimal (Leung 2014).Still other research has shown that the effect depends on the audience, with some preferring category spanning and others not (Pontikes 2012).In these ways, categories can have both positive and negative effects on behavior and performance by affecting the manner in which conceptual information about category membership is interpreted. 5hile the contributions of the categories paradigm have been carried out through the use of set and fuzzy-set theoretic (Hannan et al. 2007) notions of category membership, recent work is beginning to incorporate its multidimensional nature (Hannan et al. 2019) and the gradations of categorical belonging.

Many phenomena of interest to organization scholars are undergirded by gradations in the distance between concepts and the categories they represent.Shifting to a geometric notion of concepts, as we will discuss below, permits the analyst to take into account the ways in which gradations of membership, overlap and continuous distance impacts and influences our evaluative judgment of the underlying entities (Hannan et al. 2019).

Knowledge.Knowledge is notoriously difficult to specify, and has a long-standing and active debate undergirding its conceptual nature across philosophy, cognitive science, and social science (Steup and Neta 2020).Nevertheless, the last few decades within organization science have seen a broad collection of research at the micro, meso, and macro levels tackling questions about knowledge and its role within teams, organizations, and economic activity.From the study of team-member expertise (Srikanth et al. 2016) to the knowledge-and attention-based views of the firm (Grant 1996, Kogut and Zander 1992, Ocasio 1997), from transactive memory systems (Ren and Argote 2011) to innovation processes (Garud et al. 2013), from organizational design (Foss et al. 2013) to search and exploration (Lavie et al. 2010), knowledge plays a central role in much recent organizational theorizing.

Regardless of one's choice in the definition of knowledge, propositional knowledge is fundamentally linked to conceptual information.Propositional knowledge takes the form of "S knows that p" where "S" is the subject that knows and "p" refers to the proposition known (Ichikawa and Steup 2018).To the degree that a proposition is encoded by words in language, and to the degree that words represent conceptual information, propositional knowledge relies on concepts and how they interweave within conceptual space (McGrath and Frank 2020).Take the propositions "Taylor knows that the main industrial application of hydrogen is in the manufacture of ammonia" and "Terry knows that quantum algorithms can have lower time complexities."Each of these knowledge propositions represents distinct conceptual meaning and the different fields noted above would operationalize the propositions in their unique ways.For example, team scholars might highlight that a team composed of Taylor and Terry would have diverse underlying knowledge, scholars taking the attention-based view would note that Taylor and Terry would likely attend differently to the knowledge space in response to organizational change, those who study innovation might note the potential for knowledge recombination if Taylor and Terry shared office space, and those studying search processes might note that in response to any given problem Taylor and Terry would uniquely search the knowledge space for possible solutions.In all these cases, to the degree that these fields have traced knowledge dynamics by any recourse to the propositional knowledge encoded in language, they have engaged with concepts and conceptual spaces in a fundamental way.

Culture.Culture has been variously conceptualized as the shared values, stories, frames, toolkits, and categories of a collective (Giorgi et al. 2015, Lamont and Small 2008, Small et al. 2010).The culture construct has been central to organization studies and has been mobilized at all levels of analysis from individuals and teams up to organizations and nations (Giorgi et al. 2015).From understanding how culture shapes the structure of occupations (Glynn 2000) and organizational fields (Anteby 2010) to how it influences entrepreneurial persuasion (Lounsbury and Glynn 2001) and environmental change (Rao and Giorgi 2006), from its role in storytelling (Lounsbury and Glynn 2001) to its effect on identity building (Ravasi and Schultz 2006), from its shaping of interpersonal communication (Srivastava et al. 2017) to its effect on organizational performance (Corritore et al. 2020), culture is deeply mediated by concepts and how they interact.While each conception of culture noted above encodes a distinct facet, all are undergirded by cognitive processes (DiMaggio 1997, Patterson 2014), many accessible through linguistic traces and the conceptual information they represent.As organizations scholars have noted, language provides us with a window into culture (Corritore et al. 2020, Goldberg et al. 2016, Srivastava et al. 2017).Therefore, with culture understood as a "system of publicly and collectively accepted meanings" (Pettigrew 1979, p. 574), and with concepts identifying and giving meaning to our experience (Hannan et al. 2019), concepts and conceptual spaces have necessarily become an important pillar of the study of organizational culture.

Based on their critical role in forging categories, knowledge and culture, concepts and conceptual space have become an important part of the scaffolding upon which much organizational theory is built.This review is meant to be suggestive and illustrate the kinds of linkages that exist between prior work and conceptual spaces.In what follows, we propose that concepts and conceptual spaces as represented by word embedding models could become an important tool in the organization scholars toolkit.To the degree that organization scholars will seek to operationalize constructs undergirded by concepts and conceptual information, they will be aided by this new class of models from computer science and natural language processing.With this in mind, we now turn to briefly outlining the evolution of the use of text as data within organization science before describing how word embedding models work and why they serve as effective representations for operationalizing conceptual spaces.

The last ten years have seen explosive growth in the analysis of textual data through computational tools and approaches.From sociology (Evans and Aceves 2016) to economics (Gentzkow et al. 2019) to political science (Grimmer and Stewart 2013), text is fast becoming a central observatory of organizational, economic, and social life.Textual data provides rich traces of thought and behavior in domains as diverse as online knowledge communities, earnings calls and company reports, product evaluations, organization emails and discussion boards, historical archives, video transcriptions and movie subtitles, medical records, e-commerce, social media platforms, news articles, scientific disciplines, and many others.Taken together, these sources of textual data reach deeper and more broadly into organizational life than ever before.As Evans and Aceves (2016, p. 33) note, text data now allow us to access deep information "about hidden elements of the social game being played and the social world beneath it."Yet, the large size of these corpora as well as their broad scope means that extracting information signals that are theoretically meaningful is increasingly aided by computational approaches that take these vast troves of unstructured textual traces and converts them into meaningful and relevant measures that map onto relevant theoretical constructs. 6ne major difference between textual data and the kinds of quantitative data that organizational scholars are accustomed to working with is that text is high dimensional.As Gentzkow et al. (2019, p. 535) note, "a sample of thirty-word Twitter messages that use only the one thousand most common words in the English language [...] has roughly as many dimensions as there are atoms in the universe."The central task of scholars using text as data has been to reduce this dimensionality by imposing restrictions on the data.Some of the most common computational tools for reducing this dimensionality within organization science over the last two decades have been dictionaries, semantic networks, and topic models.We briefly review each of these in turn.

Dictionaries.The most straightforward way to reduce textual dimensionality is through the creation of dictionaries, wherein the researcher establishes lists of words that correspond to a particular construct of interest and counts their appearance within documents from a corpus (Krippendorff 2018, Pennebaker et al. 2015).By counting the words relevant to a construct of interest, the dimensionality of a document (e.g., an email, a patent, or an earnings call) is reduced to a numerical value that represents the intensity of that construct within the document (Abrahamson and Hambrick 1997, Huff 1990, Kaplan et al. 2003).While dictionary measures can be useful in some situations, an important drawback is that because words belong to only one list, polysemy reduces the fidelity of these measures.Consider the meanings of the word "good."The meanings of "good" in "market good" and "good performance" are not equivalent.Dictionary measures that fail to take into account the inherent ambiguity in language will be coarse-grained and miss gradations of meaning inherent to language.

Semantic Networks.Another tool to reduce text dimensionality is the use of semantic networks.

Practically, one builds a semantic network by using words as nodes in the network and generating links between them when they co-occur in text (Carley 1994, Kaufer andCarley 1993).Semantic networks are based on a topological representation of conceptual information, allowing access to certain kinds of information derived from the network positions of words, including centrality, influence, structural equivalence, and constraint (Carley 1994, Evans and Aceves 2016, Schank and Colby 1973). 7Early computational uses of text within organization science mobilized the semantic networks approach to map processes of macro-social cognition from large collections of media coverage (Kennedy 2005(Kennedy , 2008)).

More recently, semantic networks have been used explicitly by organizational scholars as tools for tracing many cognitive and cultural dynamics including category adoption (Zunino et al. 2019), the relationship between firms and their legal environment (Giorgi et al. 2019), and institutional logics (Jancsary et al. 2017).One important drawback to semantic networks is that once text data grow sufficiently large, standard semantic network analysis metrics fail to distinguish between concepts that are close or distant by considering topological information alone because everything is connected (Kozlowski et al. 2019), leading to a loss of resolution.Topic Models.Organizations scholars have also increased their use of topic modeling strategies (Blei et al. 2003, Hannigan et al. 2019, Mohr and Bogdanov 2013).Topic models allow scholars to render and represent documents within a corpus, using word co-occurrences within documents as input for identifying latent topics.To train topics, words in a document are treated as an unstructured set, without accounting for the order in which they appear within the document.Word co-occurrences within the documents determine the set of topics that the document will belong to.The mathematical assumption of topic models can be said to be set theory, with primary outputs being word probability distributions that represent topics and topic distributions that represent documents.Words can belong to one or more topics (which are distributions over words) and topics can belong to one or more documents (which are distributions over topics).Because these models were designed for interpretation, their optimization enforces sparsity in these distributions, such that only a small set of words load on each topic, and a small set of topics load on each document.

Because words can belong to multiple topics in a probabilistic manner, topic models overcome one of the primary weaknesses of dictionary-based measures in which each word can belong to only one dictionary list.Further, topic models go beyond dictionaries by allowing topics to be induced from the data without the need for human dictionary definition.As Hannigan et al. (2019) note, this enables the identification of themes that human readers might be unable to discover independently.In this regard, topic models have helped make measures stemming from textual data more fine-grained and offer better information signals for the underlying constructs of interest.These models have allowed scholars to trace the effect of framing repertoires on audience evaluations (Giorgi and Weber 2015), processes of innovation (Kaplan and Vakili 2015), the effect of differentiation on performance (Haans 2019), and the degree of cultural heterogeneity within firms (Corritore et al. 2020), and generated many other insights.

While topic models advanced the fine-grained nature of text representation relative to prior approaches, allowing the scholar to extract a greater information signal and more meaning from text at scale, they are subject to limitations that more recent approaches begin to address.First, when scholars work with textual data, the primary information signal they seek to extract from text revolves around human meaning.Computational linguists note that the statistical distribution of words in local context largely determines word meaning (Lenci 2018).For example, in the sentence "The photon is a type of elementary particle that serves as the quantum of the electromagnetic field" what it means to be a photon is determined by the words that surround the word "photon" within a text, which constrain the semantic role photon plays with respect to those words.Topic models use only global, document-level word context, ignoring many meaningful signals constrained by local work context and word order.In this way, topic model's bag-of-words architecture is limited in how well it can represent fine-grained meaning.

Moreover, by enforcing sparsity in word distributions over topics (most words load with approximately 0 probability) and topic distributions over words (most topics load with approximately 0 probability), the continuous, graded nature of the relationships between words is not taken into account (Kozlowski et al. 2019).To the degree that this information is critical for human interpretation, but left unconsidered by topic models, explanatory value remains untapped.

A related limitation arising from topic models' failure to account for local context is that when individuals and organizations use different words to refer to the same object, document-level context may not be enough to resolve their semantic similarity.For example, in her study of microenterprise differentiation, Carlson (2022) notes that some enterprises use the words "grocery store" while others refer to "food sellers."While these two phrases are semantically similar, the global context of the document may not be enough to resolve their similarity, if they are discussed in the context of different arguments or additional information.Local linguistic context-the words that immediately precede and follow "grocers" and "food sellers"-would much more likely detect their constrained similarity.We can also consider other ways in which language use might differ, such that some individuals or groups might use precise, formal, and sophisticated language while others communicate in vague informalities (Carlson 2022).To the extent that local semantic context of these different words is washed out by document-level context, topic models will overestimate topical variation.

An additional challenge with topic models is that proactive choice of hyperparameters is essential, especially when it comes to choosing topic number.To the extent that different researchers infuse distinct meaning into their output topics, research results could vary in important ways.For example, one scholar might decide that 50 topics best describes a corpus, while the same corpus analyzed by a different scholar might see more relevant meaning in a model with 100 topics.As Hannigan et al. (2019, p. 594) note, the process of validating topic models is "a process that entails substantial interpretive uncertainty."In this regard, model validation is an onerous process requiring a significant interpretive input from the researcher.We note that this is not solved by embedding-based Discourse Atom Topic (DAT) models, described below.

Another issue is that to the degree that a researcher wants to delve deeper into the meaning contained within texts or the corpus as a whole, they are limited by the topics induced by the model.The coarse structure of topics limits both examination of the meaning of a particular word or word class (e.g., all gender references) that may lie below the level of a topic, but also the broad picture of a social or cultural context (e.g., the conceptual breadth of an organization or industry) that lie above them.

In conclusion, each generation of tools has improved upon the prior in the degree to which the reduction of text dimensionality maps to human-inferable meaning.Nevertheless, by focusing on discrete association and human interpretation, dictionary methods and topic models implicitly rely on rigid set theoretic notions of meaning, which fail to account for available local word-context information based on distributional semantics, which implies continuous, geometric notions of distance and similarity (Erk 2012). 8In what follows, we show how embedding models take advantage of more local textual information to train improved representations of conceptual spaces and concept meanings.In this respect, word embedding models overcome some of the limitations of prior approaches to representing meaning encoded in text, allowing for more fine-grained measures of theoretical constructs as well as enabling new theoretical possibilities.

Word embeddings.We explained above that concepts are the mental representations of classes of things and that humans represent stable concepts by assigning them a word or phrase in the lexicon, noting that concepts are only meaningful insofar as they relate to and inform other concepts across a multiplicity of unique dimensions within a dense conceptual space.We here argue that a recently developed class of models coming from machine learning applied to natural language processing allows us to more effectively and efficiently represent conceptual spaces and put these spaces to work in the pursuit of organization science.Word embedding models can be understood as continuous, geometric representations of words within a textual corpus.A central premise of word embedding models is that the meaning of a word is largely shaped by the words that tend to appear in its immediate context, an idea inspired by structural linguists, who have demonstrated that differences of meaning correlate with differences of local distribution (Harris 1954).This idea is now known as distributional semantics, famously characterized by Firth as "you shall know a word by the company it keeps" (Firth 1957, p. 11).

The concept or meaning a word represents can be deduced by the distribution of words that surround it.

The underlying computational architecture for thinking about concepts and conceptual spaces in this distributed manner can be traced back to work in the early 1980s by computer scientist Geoffrey Hinton (Hinton 1986, Hinton et al. 1986) and work by cognitive scientists during this period working on models of parallel distributed processing (McClelland andRumelhart 1989, Rumelhart et al. 1986a, b).This architecture underlies the broad class of language models currently in development within machine learning (Brown et al. 2020, Devlin et al. 2018, Fedus et al. 2021, Liu et al. 2019, Mikolov, Sutskever, et al. 2013, Pennington et al. 2014).Our guiding application below will be carried out with the word2vec architecture of word embedding models put forward by Mikolov et al. (2013), which we see as most accessible and readily useful for even moderate sized corpora.Word2vec has many similarities with near-neighbor architectures such as GloVe (Pennington et al. 2014) and FastText (Bojanowski et al. 2017), and produces outputs that form the inputs to larger, contextual models we describe later.

An example can describe the intuition behind how the algorithm works.Consider that we are interested in creating a representation of the conceptual space of innovation over the last 50 years.The first thing we would need is textual data from within that domain of conceptual activity.The United States Patent Office provides a biased trace of innovative activity in the form of a freely downloadable dataset that includes the text of all patents, their abstracts, descriptions, and claims.We will use a corpus of patent abstracts throughout the paper to guide the reader through the process of training this conceptual space and constructing associated conceptual measures.

Imagine that each unique word in the patents corpus begins as a randomly positioned "word magnet" placed on an enormous refrigerator (Hovy 2020).As the continuous bag of words (CBOW) algorithm scrolls through the corpus, it uses the sliding context window around each target word to predict it (see figure 1).If words have appeared in similar context windows, they are nudged closer together and if not, they are pushed further apart.The ultimate goal of this online algorithm is to produce a semantic model in which words that appear in similar contexts lie close to one another, while those from dissimilar contexts are far apart.Because the two dimensions of this refrigerator surface are insufficient to capture the full scope of meaning for each word, the algorithm is instead implemented within a much higher (100-1000) dimensional space.In such a way, the conceptual information of a target word is induced from words that surround it, plotting each word in the corpus as a coordinate or vector in n-dimensional space.And it is the relative location of words within this n-dimensional vector space that allows us to speak of word embedding models as representing the conceptual space of an area of human conceptual activity. 9In what follows, we first walk the reader through the process of training a representation of the conceptual space of innovation using the patents corpus.We then validate the output embedding model, showing that it provides an effective representation of this space before highlighting how scholars can use pre-trained embeddings in their research.We conclude this section on embedding models by noting some of their advantages relative to prior computational methods for the analysis of text and meaning.

Selecting a corpus.Scholars can use two kinds of word embedding models, depending on the application.On the one hand, researchers can train a representation using a corpus of text selected by them.This is useful when the empirical context of interest has a large text corpus available and the researcher is interested in understanding what the conceptual space of that domain of human action looks like and what conceptual relations reveal about that context of activity.In our example, patented innovation is well represented by the patents corpora, so we will show below how to train the conceptual space representation from scratch and what conceptual connections it reveals.Other examples of contexts where researchers might train a corpus from scratch include online communities (Aceves et al. 2022, Burtch et al. 2021, Chambers et al. 2022), academic disciplines (Hofstra et al. 2020, Lin et al. 2022), labor markets (Bana 2022), public records (Arseniev-Koehler et al. 2022), product and firm descriptions (Guzman and Li 2022), and earnings calls and public speeches (Kirgil and Voyer 2022).

Alternatively, if researchers want to trace conceptual dynamics within a smaller corpus that is not sufficient for training a unique, context specific embedding, researchers may use conceptual spaces from embeddings pre-trained on texts similar to those they seek to analyze.Widely used pre-trained embeddings have been trained on text from massive corpora such as the news (Google News Word Embedding 2013), Wikipedia (Devlin et al. 2018, Grave et al. 2018), or the web (Radford et al. 2019).To the degree that these pre-trained embeddings have been trained on a collection of concepts and claims present in the smaller sample of text we seek to understand, they encode information of these concepts in the contexts of others and can be used to approximate relevant distances.Research in the context of politics and historical semantics has found that pre-trained embeddings offer results comparable to context-specific embeddings (Kozlowski et al. 2019, Rodriguez andSpirling 2022).If there is reason to believe that the concepts and ideas contained within the research project are not well represented in these massive pre-trained embeddings, researchers can fine-tune them with the text from their smaller corpus (Burtch et al. 2021, Liu et al. 2019).Fine-tuning warps the pre-trained conceptual space into conformity with the sample (Liu et al. 2019), leading associations between concepts in the researchers' corpus to adjust word weights in the pre-trained model to better reflect those relationships.

In the end, which embedding model to use will depend on the purpose of the researcher and the kinds of conceptual dynamics that they seek to trace.In what follows, we focus our attention on describing the process of training and validating an embedding model from scratch.Throughout the following sections, we discuss tradeoffs between different parameter settings and strategies, and we encourage the reader to follow the article text alongside the methodological appendix.

Cleaning the corpus.The first step in training an embedding model is to read the corpus into a software program such as Python or R (c#3). 10We will here walk the reader through a Python implementation of the word2vec algorithm.We begin by taking the text in each patent abstract and breaking up the continuous string of text into a list of words (c#4).We then lowercase the text, remove punctuation marks and numeric strings (c#4) and convert each abstract into a list of words called tokens (c#5).We may optionally take this list of words for every patent and run it through a phrase detection algorithm, which will learn key phrases given the distribution of word co-locations to identify frequent bigrams or sets of abutting words (c#6-9).For example, when the words "electric" and "vehicle" appear together in certain contexts, they will be united to form the phrase "electric_vehicle."After the list of words or phrases has been established, the word embedding algorithm is executed to learn optimal distances between words or bigrams and their linguistic contexts to preserve the conceptual space of words and phrases in language (c#13).

Training the embedding model.The first step is to select the model.There are many available algorithms for training embeddings, ranging from word representations built from shallow neural networks (e.g., word2vec, fastText) (Mikolov, Sutskever, et al. 2013), low-rank approximations of co-occurrence matrices (GloVe) (Pennington et al. 2014), or deep contextual embeddings from transformers (e.g., BERT, GPT*) (Devlin et al. 2018, Radford et al.).The output of these different models, regardless of their implementation architecture, may be interpreted as an n-dimensional conceptual space in which words or phrases are represented by vector positions within the space.We focus the reader's intuition by illustrating the word2vec model, a widely used algorithm for training conceptual spaces, with the papers that introduced it (Mikolov, Chen, et al. 2013a, Mikolov, Sutskever, et al. 2013) garnering over 60,000 citations in the decade following its introduction. 11

A popular implementation of the word2vec algorithm is the continuous bag-of-words (CBOW) algorithm, easily accessible in the Gensim python library, which uses the linguistic context of a target word to predict that word when masked. We find it useful to think of the number of dimensions by thinking about the extremes.A naive model could treat each word as its own dimension, such that any word within a corpus with 100,000 unique words would require 100,000 dimensions for accurate representation.As words become identified as similar from context, however, that number of dimensions can be reduced within limits.12At the other extreme, 2 or 3-dimensions is what might be desirable for visual inspection, but every time one word is similar to a pair of others, and those others are not similar to one another, an additional dimension is required to render that intransitivity without distortion.Too many dimensions would translate into increased memory requirements and redundancy with reduced interpretability; too few dimensions distort distances and fail to account for linguistic intransitivities.In this way, accurate predictions are obtained by having at least enough dimensions to capture the complex semantic relationships in question.

In practice, 300-dimensions has become a standard largely from convention following the initial word2vec paper (Mikolov, Chen, et al. 2013b), which established it through cross-validation, identifying the optimal number of dimensions for reducing error on the task of predicting masked words.Most subsequent analyses have been built on smaller, less diverse text collections, requiring fewer dimensions and so 300 has often been used as an upper bound.More recent work has more explicitly demonstrated that dimension should be chosen based on corpus statistics-that the number of pairwise equidistant words of the corpus vocabulary provides a lower bound for the number of dimensions and going below this will typically result in a degradation of quality of learned word embeddings (Patel and Bhattacharyya 2017).Hofstra et al. (2020) find robust results using models with 100, 200, and 300 dimensions.Other work that has traced the effect of different dimension sizes on political corpora of different sizes (ranging from approximately ten thousand to one hundred thousand tokens per corpus), has found marginal improvements beyond 300 dimensions (Rodriguez and Spirling 2022).

Window size.Recall that window size refers to the number of words before and after a focal word the algorithm will use to predict that word (or its neighbors).The smallest this window can be is 1.

With smaller windows the algorithm will tend to encode syntactic relationships (e.g., nouns followed by verbs).As window size increases, more meaning and semantics becomes encoded into model output.

Consider the example from Rodriguez and Spirling (2022) with a corpus of two sentences: 1) "the lion eats meat", and 2) "the cow eats grass".With a window size of 1, we would know that both cows and lions eat, and in this sense cows and lions are syntactically equivalent as we do not have enough information to distinguish between the two.Nevertheless, as the window increases, the algorithm begins to encode more of what it means to be a cow versus a lion.As with the number of dimensions, there are decreasing returns here as well, with minor improvements to a model's performance with windows greater than 5 words (Rodriguez and Spirling 2022).13

Global conceptual structure at the document level.By extending the word embedding architecture, the position of sentences, paragraphs, and documents within the embedding space can also be established.Scholars can measure the similarity of documents by comparing the centroid vectors of the words contained within the documents (Lix et al. 2022).Alternatively, they can train a document embedding model that creates a unique vector for each document in the corpus using the recurrent neural network architecture (Guzman and Li 2022, Le and Mikolov 2014, Whalen et al. 2020).These models are called document embeddings, and rely on the same logic as word embeddings, wherein the words and their contexts are used to train the multidimensional space."Documents"-which is the formal terminology regardless of whether we are dealing with sentences, paragraphs, or stand-alone documents-are then embedded within this space as a function of the sequence of words they contain (Le and Mikolov 2014).

The validation procedures presented above are intended to give the analyst and reader confidence that the embedding model is accurately representing the conceptual space of interest.It is worth noting that these results required no explicit human coding besides the setting of our embedding model's parameters.Results presented here were based on the passive harvesting of all human conceptual activity within the patented innovation domain over the last 50 years.The embedding model harnessed knowledge from hundreds of thousands of inventors who worked hundreds of millions of hours to leave us the textual invention descriptions recovered as a singular representation of the conceptual space of innovation.

No need to formally specify relevant dimensions.Formal attempts at modeling concepts have sought to clearly enumerate the relevant dimensions of a concept through a logico-deductive approach (Gärdenfors 2004, Gardenfors 2014, Hannan et al. 2019).For example, physical objects can have the following relevant features, each of which can be considered a relevant dimension of that concept: color, brightness, weight, height, width, and depth.Within this multi-dimensional space, any given physical concept will have a particular position.For example, when considering the concept fruit, distinct fruits (e.g., apple, banana, orange) will occupy unique positions given their position within each dimension.

While this approach can be useful for understanding concepts within circumscribed domains, even then it can be impractical and hard to measure because it is difficult to state a priori what analysts should anticipate as relevant dimensions (Hofstadter and Sander 2013).Consider here Wittgenstein's example of the difficulty in arriving at a definition of the game concept (Wittgenstein 2010, sec. 66-75).There are simply too many relevant dimensions to have a satisfactory account of the game concept, given that the relevant dimensions of board-game, card-game, ball-game, Olympic game, and game theory are too many to be logically derived.The advantage of word embeddings is that the relationships between concepts, and therefore the relevant dimensions that matter for any given concept, can be induced from the way language is used and therefore do not need to be specified ex ante.Given that it is unnecessary to state the relevant dimensions prior to analysis, even the most complicated theaters of organizational behavior became analytically tractable.As others have noted, "word embeddings offer holistic and meaningful insights into numerous dimensions of meaning contained in language that prior methods have been unable to capture" (Lix et al. 2022).In part, this advantage derives from the fact that the dimensions of meaning are densely inscribed using the efficiently estimated neural network architecture.

Greater Effective Dimensionality.Embeddings are typically represented by something between 100 and 1000 densely encoded dimensions.The density of the encoding means that each word vector has a nonzero coordinate in all modeled dimensions.As stated above, topic models may have the same number of topics (e.g., 100 to 1000), but these are sparsely encoded to facilitate human interpretation, such that topics have only a few substantially nonzero word loadings, and documents have only a few substantially nonzero topic loadings.As a result, topic models are built for description, but at the cost of forcing the effective dimensionality of their representations from hundreds to just a few, distorting distances that might otherwise have been calculated within topic space.Because of its dense encoding, embeddings are naturally harder to describe, but the distances have many greater degrees of freedom to more precisely encode meanings.In this way, embeddings allow the analyst a "vast set of potential axes along which individuals and social groups may compete, cooperate, fracture, or coalesce" (Kozlowski et al. 2019, p. 27) relative to low-dimensional theories and measures.

Embeddings are also increasingly being used for precision topic modeling by drawing on approaches like k-Singular Value Decomposition (k-SVD) to provide sparse descriptions without compromising word distances (Arora et al. 2018, Arseniev-Koehler et al. 2022) and yielding topic with far greater semantic coherence.These are variously called discourse or semantic "atom" topic models and produce vectors associated with each topic or discourse atom.A word, phrase or document's meaning can be summarized with the collection of topic vectors most proximate to them.

Unsupervised training.Related to points above, a particular strength of the word embedding approach is that model training is performed in a seemingly unsupervised or self-supervised manner, such that the tedium of manually coding the semantic content of text is obviated, learned entirely by machine.This is advantageous, because as we noted above, individual human coders would be unable to specify all relevant dimensions or maintain dimensions in memory.Nevertheless, the model's training has been carried out by the collective community that generated the corpus.In our innovation example, the vector space was supervised by every inventor in our patent corpus, in proportion to the number and length of sentences they wrote.Each sliding window of words was constructed to convey a meaning to the patent examiner and future inventors, which the algorithm used to build a vector space and position words in conceptually appropriate ways.As a result, scholars can train a conceptual space of technical innovation using the patent corpus, a conceptual space of public company communications using earnings calls transcripts and press releases, a conceptual space of investment analysis using analyst reports, or a conceptual space of a specific firm's knowledge using internal communications such as slack and email messages.These conceptual spaces can be trained with minimal supervision and are therefore fast becoming valuable observatories for tracing the statics and dynamics of organizational life of central concern to organizational scientists (Aceves et al. 2022, Burtch et al. 2021, Carlson 2022, Chambers et al. 2022, Guzman and Li 2022, Hofstra et al. 2020, Lawson et al. 2022, Lix et al. 2022, Waller and Anderson 2021, Whalen et al. 2020).

Co-occurrence unnecessary.Another strength of these models is that it is not necessary for two concepts to co-occur with each other within any document in order for them to be encoded into a similar vector.All that is required is that they co-occur with similar concepts.For example, we could state a priori that doctors and lawyers are quite similar on certain dimensions (e.g., they require advanced degrees, have high income levels, live in similar neighborhoods, etc.), but they may nevertheless never occur together within the same document of a corpus.Despite this lack of co-occurrence with each other, they will likely both independently co-occur with concepts such as high-income, advanced degree, white-collar, etc., and in so doing will end up having close vectors that encode these similarities.In this way, the underlying computational architecture of embedding models better approximates social and cultural meanings without recourse to strict co-occurrence.

Context-relevant meaning structures.An advantage of using a custom-trained embedding model is that it will capture context relevant meaning structures.For example, the meaning of "sweet" would be different in the context of software teams compared to the context of cooking.As Lix et al. (2021) noted in their context of software teams, the closest terms to "sweet" were "intense", "dope", and "yay".Furthermore, to the degree that the same word encodes different concepts (polysemy), the conceptual information of each sense of the word lies in linear superposition within the word embedding (Arora et al. 2018).This means that the n-dimensional vector encoding a word such as "bank" contains within it the conceptual information for all concepts it represents, such as riverside or financial institution.

In this way, the context-relevant meaning of a word is encoded in the model even in the case of polysemous words.When these context-sensitive meanings are not only different but exclusive or opposing, then context-sensitive embeddings from transformers can render a distinct word vector for each word in context.

Measure.Distances between words within a document or associated set can be measured in order to calculate how broadly or narrowly they are distributed in conceptual space.A document could be anything from a patent to a person's email communications.We can measure how far each word is to every other word, on average.Taking the mean distance of elements within the set (or between each word and the set centroid) provides a measure of the conceptual breadth within that set.At the most basic level, we can measure the average distance between words within a document.For example, we can start with two simple sets, the first containing the words ["biochemistry", "chemistry", "analytical_chemistry"] and the second the words ["chemistry", "oceanography", and "computer"] (c#24).Using our patent embedding model, we get an average breadth of 29 for the first set and 47 for the second.This suggests that the second set is conceptually much broader than the first.We can carry out this procedure across entire documents as described in c#25, where we measure the conceptual breadth of each patent.

We might use conceptual breadth to assess the conceptual diversity of elements within an entrepreneurial pitch, an email, a product description, or a team brainstorming session.Of special relevance here is that this is not a measure of categorical diversity.Standard measures of diversity, as we noted in our example on figure 7, could capture equivalence between documents where little exists, given that two sets of unique symbol distributions would lead to identical diversity scores.As such, this measure of conceptual breadth opens up theoretical space that moves beyond categorical diversity, such that it is not about how diverse a set of concepts, ideas, or individuals is, but how far these concepts, ideas, or individuals lie with respect to each other within the relevant conceptual space.This is the mathematical equivalent of moving from set theory to geometry-and to far greater precision.

The same logic applies when we measure the conceptual breadth of a collection of documents rather than words.For example, we might be interested in understanding the breadth of inventor teams.In this case, we can treat each inventor on a team as a "document" embedded within conceptual space.We could ask if the team is drawing on individuals located conceptually far from each other, or if their conceptual position is proximate to other inventors on the team.Understood in this way, an inventor team with members who have published prior patents in areas of the conceptual space dealing with nanotechnology, biotechnology, and software would be considered conceptually broader than a team where all members have only published patents in nanotechnology.This measure would still provide significant variation even when all inventors have limited their published patents within a single class.

Because some patent classes can be conceptually broad, a standard measure of diversity could find none where it exists.As observed above, it is possible to have equally diverse teams that nevertheless vary significantly in conceptual breadth.

Application.This measure of conceptual breadth has been used in recent work to trace a variety of constructs.Lix et al. (2021) measure the discursive breadth of team members as they engage with distinct stages of software projects.They are able to trace the diversity of conceptual engagement during each unique project stage, finding that top performing teams have an ability to alter their cognition to the changing tasks at hand, exhibiting greater discursive breadth when coming up with new ideas and lower breadth when switching to tasks that rely on coordination.Such a fine-grained notion of knowledge engagement would have been difficult to trace with prior text analytic methodologies.

Separately, researchers have used conceptual breadth to trace how broadly or narrowly online community members allocate their attention to knowledge space in response to status changes, finding a U-shaped relationship between status and attention breadth (Aceves et al. 2022).These researchers trained the conceptual space of over 130 knowledge domains and were thereby able to trace similar attention dynamics across different knowledge domains, from computer programming and mathematics to parenting and gardening.Because they had the capacity to deploy the algorithm at scale across the hundreds of communities, they could compute how over 20 million members allocated their attention across the 23 million problems posted on these question-and-answer communities.

Other work has implemented this methodology across entire languages, tracing the degree to which languages have broader or narrower conceptual spaces across all of their conceptual domains (Aceves and Evans 2021).Using texts like the Bible, movie subtitles, and political documents written in multiple languages-containing the same information but encoded in a different language-they are able to trace significant variation in the degree to which concepts relate with each other across different languages.They find that while some languages densely interlink diverse conceptual subspaces and weave together diverse conceptual terrain, other languages are sparser and more fragmented, more strongly separating diverse areas of meaning.They then observe how this linguistic density of the conceptual space shapes the conceptual breadth of real conversations and Wikipedia articles across hundreds of languages.

All three of these papers have opened new theoretical avenues of investigation across diverse literatures.These new research avenues would have been difficult to operationalize without a notion of conceptual spaces and their representation in embedding models.

Measure.The conceptual breadth construct described above is pertinent when our analytical focus lies on elements within the set.The same underlying measure can be used when our analytical focus is on relationships between different sets.In this case, we will refer to conceptual distance or similarity rather than conceptual breadth. 18Formally, if we have at least two sets with at least one element in each, we can calculate the conceptual distance between those sets as the distance between the centroids or multidimensional means of each set (c#27-29).At its most basic, we can calculate the conceptual distance between two sets, each containing a single word.This is nothing more than measuring the conceptual distance between those words, which represent their own means.As the number of elements and number of sets increases, the underlying computation remains the same, but the scope of its theoretical possibilities grows.It is also possible to compute this distance/similarity measure by training a document embedding model (c#31), which assigns each document its own vector in the embedding space, with its weights being trained following the same logic of word co-occurrences (Le and Mikolov 2014).This essentially treats the document itself as one more word within the document, treating all the other words as co-occurrences with it.

Application.This measure of conceptual similarity has recently been used to characterize the innovation space within the patent data as we have done above (Whalen et al. 2020).Researchers used the doc2vec framework to calculate over 600 million patent pair similarities.In generating these knowledge similarity measures, the authors also put forward interesting auxiliary measures using these scores, including measures that operationalize a) prior art proximity-the degree to which a patent cites prior art that is similar or dissimilar to itself, b) prior art homogeneity-the degree to which a patent cites areas of knowledge space distant from one another (an equivalent notion to our conceptual breadth measure above, with homogeneous patents being equivalent to narrow coverage of the conceptual space), c) impact proximity-the degree to which a patent is cited by future patents that are similar or dissimilar to itself, and d) impact homogeneity-the degree to which a patent is related to a diverse set of future patents through its forward citations.

Scholars have also used this measure with a focus on conceptual distance.Burtch et al. (2021) used the doc2vec implementation of conceptual distance to investigate whether peer awards can influence the novelty of contributions within online communities.Novelty here is measured as the distance in a community member's contributions before and after receiving an award.The authors find that awards lead to less novelty and more exploitative behavior within the knowledge space in the aftermath of receiving an award.Similarly, Hofstra et al. (2020) use a word2vec measure of distance to capture the degree to which scientific dissertations introduce novelty into the scientific literature, finding that students from underrepresented groups are responsible for introducing the most novelty into the system.

Others have used this measure to operationalize firm differentiation.In the context of microenterprises in developing countries, Carlson (2022) uses the BERT architecture (Devlin et al. 2018, Reimers andGurevych 2020) to calculate the pairwise cosine distances for all microenterprises in their dataset.With these distances, they are then able to estimate that across 10,000 microenterprises within eight developing countries, differentiation is associated with increased revenues and profits.Similarly, Guzman and Li (2022) use the doc2vec implementation of distance to measure the founding strategic differentiation of startups using the Crunchbase data.The authors find results similar to Carlson (2022), namely that new firms that differentiate experience increases in early stage financing and equity outcomes.

An extension of this measure includes embedding two or more exemplar or archetypal documents into the relevant conceptual space.For example, if we were working with a corpus that included words uttered by different individuals such as executives or politicians, we might consider including a document for each of the major personality archetypes (Costa and McCrae 1985).By measuring the proximity of each individual's text to each personality type, we would be able to induce the personality composition of each individual and relate it to outcomes of interest.In aggregate, these conceptual similarity measures can be used to trace the composition of ideas, individuals, groups, organizations, or any other relevant agglomeration.

An interesting theoretical opportunity for these measures is the possibility to engage more deeply with theoretical traditions that have been influential in domains other than organization science, but which nevertheless remain outside of our sphere due to the lack of viable methodologies to quantify their theoretical constructs in a principled manner not reliant on literary interpretation.Measuring conceptual similarity as we have proposed makes it possible to scale up the measure of theoretical constructs related to ideal forms (Plato.and Bloom 1968), ideal types (Weber 2011), family resemblances (Wittgenstein 2010), and prototypes (Rosch 1973) in a consistent, principled, and replicable manner.In this respect, the conceptual similarity measure stands to open up large bodies of theoretical work for empirical examination in the context of organizations.

Application.This approach was recently used in a paper tracing the effect of hiring women into senior leadership roles on what it means to be a woman within those organizations (Lawson et al. 2022).

The authors first trained a word2vec embedding using SEC documents and earnings calls transcripts.

They then created and validated a set of 100 words to capture the meaning of the agency concept (e.g., capable, independent, dominant) and observed the distance between the agency concept and the woman concept before and after the appointment of a senior female leader within the organization, finding that the meaning of woman gets closer to the agency position in conceptual space for the time period after women are appointment to top tiers of management.The authors replicated their results using different embedding hyperparameters and dimensions sizes, speaking to the robustness of embedding models conditional on having the minimum necessary dimensions to capture semantic variation within conceptual space.

Measure.Conceptual spaces allow us to identify how meanings around terms differ across time and space.One way to explore conceptual meaning is by creating unique embedding models for different individuals, firms, industries, geographies, or time periods in order to understand how meanings differ across them.Once relevant cleavages of interest have been identified, we can take the relevant corpus (e.g., patents, earnings calls, newspapers) and train conceptual spaces for each within the data.In the context of our patents example, we might train two embedding models, one for the period before the invention of functional magnetic resonance imaging techniques in 1990 and one after.We could then explore how the meaning of concepts related to brain and neuro sciences shifted with this innovation.For instance, what were terms most associated with different brain regions before and after the fmri innovation (c#37).We could then compare how shifting meanings differed by company or state and how this shifting landscape might have influenced organizational and market outcomes for the firms and industries involved.

Application.The first major papers to use word embedding methodology in the context of the social sciences used this approach to studying meaning (Caliskan et al. 2017, Garg et al. 2018, Hamilton et al. 2016, Kozlowski et al. 2019).In the first of these papers, researchers used six historical corpora across four languages to trace the shifting meaning of words over time by seeing how a word's nearest neighbors in conceptual space shifted throughout the decades (Hamilton et al. 2016).Using the word2vec embedding they traced how the meaning of the gay concept shifted from a meaning around terms such as daft, sweet, and cheerful in the 1900's to a meaning revolving around the terms frolicsome, witty, and bright in the 1950's, and finally ending with a meaning around terms such as lesbian, bisexual, and homosexual in the 1990's.In another paper, researchers studied the relationship between stereotyped associations within word embeddings and their relationship to empirical data in contemporary society (Caliskan et al. 2017).For example, they traced the gender stereotypes of professions, finding that professions had female meanings to the degree that they were associated with female participation in the labor force.In another study, Garg et al. (2018) quantified gender and ethnic stereotypes over 100 years of United States history using the pre-trained Google News word2vec model (Google News Word Embedding 2013), illuminating how different adjectives and professions came to be more or less closely associated with different populations (e.g., men vs. women, white vs. asian vs. hispanic) over time.

Recent work tracing meaning through word embeddings has delved deeper into particular contexts using this methodology.One study used a corpus of 19th century first person narratives to trace how intersectional identities of black and white men and women mapped onto five social institutions, including the polity, the economy, culture, the domestic sphere, and authority relations (Nelson 2021).To

give one example from the paper, the author measured the distance to the dainty concept, finding it most closely associated with white women and least associated with black men.

In other work, researchers have mobilized this approach to measure the collective intentionality (people's ability to engage in collective reasoning and action) of political leaders and compare how republican and democratic leaders differently mobilize it (Kirgil and Voyer 2022).They measured collective intentionality by creating a composite list of plural pronouns (we, our), plural constants (state-names) and plural nouns (people).Then, using a word embedding model, they found the closest terms around the collective intentionality vector by state, allowing them to compare how collective intentionality was differently mobilized by different leaders.In toto, these studies of meaning illustrate that to the degree that language provides us with a window onto culture (Corritore et al. 2020, Goldberg et al. 2016, Srivastava et al. 2017), embedding models provide us with a unique representation of the picture seen through that window.

Measure.Another novel measure can be created by tracing the location of a concept relative to a conceptual dimension of interest.As described above, embedding models can be used to solve analogical reasoning tasks such as "king" -"man" + "woman" = "queen".This architecture can be used to define any dimensions of interest within a conceptual space.In the king-queen example, the gender dimension is operationalized through the "man" -"woman" and "king" -"queen" vectors.Kozlowski et al. (2019) detail how to construct such dimensions within a conceptual space.First, the researcher needs to identify dimension of interest.For our example here, we will project different concepts onto the masculine-feminine gender dimension.To do so, we begin by determining relevant terms that define the gender dimension.Here we use the sets ['man','him', 'he', 'male', 'men'] and ['woman', 'her', 'she', 'female', 'women'].We then calculate the orthogonal projection of different concepts onto this dimension (c#20).

The approach of projection to bipolar conceptual dimensions can be further extended to anchor low-dimensional subspaces with multiple meanings in which words and concepts can be plotted and understood as mixtures of those meanings.This can be performed by theoretically selecting a collection of "archetypes", extremal points with known and widely shared meanings and plotting all relevant words or concepts in the subspaces defined by these extremal anchors.For example, in categorizing a new, information technology-based entrepreneurial venture, one might ask where it fits in the space inscribed by Uber, Amazon, Google or Bitcoin, as a convex combination of the vectors inscribed by these archetypal commercial enterprises (Cutler and Breiman 1994, Damle and Sun 2017, Eugster 2012).

Alternatively, anchors can be discovered directly from data, as with topic modeling, but taking advantage of all contextual word information for which embedding models account.As described above, a successful singular value decomposition version of this approach has recently been designed by Arora et al. (Arora et al. 2018) and powerfully demonstrated in the social science literature (Arseniev-Koehler et al. 2022), identifying 'semantic atoms' or vectors that disentangle the thicket of conceptual meanings within a word embedding space and allow analysts to plot other concepts with respect to those atoms or within the subspace inscribed by them.

Application.This measure was developed and mobilized to study the evolution of social class across the 20th and 21st centuries (Kozlowski et al. 2019).Studying embeddings trained on the text from millions of books published during the 20th century, they operationalized dimensions of class following the procedure outlined above, seeking to understand how dimensions underlying social class had shifted over the 20th century.To do so, they generated the following theoretically informed dimensions: affluence (rich-poor), education (educated-uneducated), cultivation (cultivated-uncultivated), status (prestigious-unprestigious), morality (good-evil), employment (employer-employee), and gender (man-woman) for a separate embedding of each decade of the 20th century.Onto these dimensions they could then project distinct classes of concepts such as musical styles, sports, and professions to understand how these concepts evolved and developed during the course of the century.Researchers have applied this methodology to study other kinds of cultural associations in contexts such as health, morality (Arseniev-Koehler et al. 2022) and political ideology (Taylor and Stoltz 2021).

Researchers have pushed measurement possibilities forward by projecting not just concepts onto these dimensions, but entire documents (Taylor and Stoltz 2020).Further, while the above measures have relied on researchers to specify the relevant dimensions of the continua of interest, recent work has moved to automatically identify these continua (Milbauer et al. 2021).Using the content of Reddit communities, Milbauer et al. ( 2021) created an unsupervised procedure to identify multiple ideological poles across communities, allowing them to move beyond the static left-right ideological dimension to discover the many axes of polarization and ideological difference at play in modern discourse.One can imagine using this methodology in many organizational settings to, for example, identify the many potential sources of conflict that exist across teams, groups, units, or divisions.

Above, we discussed how researchers can investigate the position of key terms across relevant cultural dimensions, describing how the position of concepts differed across the gender dimension.But this is not the only use for dimensions, as conceptual spaces also allow us to measure and understand how relevant dimensions themselves relate to one another.An extension of this measure is to use the encoded dimensions within the space and compare them to each other.For example, Kozlowski et al. (2019) used their established class dimensions to trace how each dimension related to each of the others throughout the 20th century, showing, for example, that affluence came to be much more closely associated with education as the century progressed, independent of its relationship to cultivation.In such a way, organizational scholars can understand how the relationships between relevant dimensions might differ across relevant conceptual spaces.For example, scholars might investigate the degree to which different cultural dimensions are tightly or loosely connected within and across organizations.

Deep learning approaches to model language have added context sensitivity as another critical extension to word embeddings.These have been manifest in massive models like BERT (for Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) (Devlin et al. 2018) and the GPT (Generative Pre-trained Transformer) family of models (Radford et al. 2019), which use a neural network architecture called the transformer to link those context words, backwards and forwards in the case of BERT, that influence the meaning of a given word.Such models produce what can be described as contextual embeddings, such that each word is not represented by a single vector, but a cloud of vectors, each for that word in distinct context."Apple" in the context of "Google" has a different value than "apple" in the context of "orange."

These models improve prediction and further extend our ability to model conceptual space with precision.

They preserve strengths from prior models, but add greater precision by accounting for shifting contexts.

These contextual embeddings could enable the contingent classification of firms.For example, one can distinguish the position of Apple to Google in terms of earnings, and their relative positions in terms of products (e.g., iPhone vs. Android).

Because words have more conceptual degrees of freedom-more ambiguity or possible interpretations-than sentences, which have more conceptual flexibility than entire documents, the position of embedded words represents a broader cloud of points (or conceptual vectors) than the position of sentences than the position of documents.Even though these models are large and difficult to initialize, now comprising up to trillions of parameters, versions built from large corpora can be fine-tuned or "pre-trained" with small numbers of relevant text to warp their conceptual space into conformity with that text (Liu et al. 2019).

A recent and interesting use case takes advantage of the fact that these models are capable of predicting the next word in a sequence of words.This capacity has meant that a major use for these models is to create text automatically.Researchers have turned this ability on its head to identify visionary ideas by executives, politicians, and lawyers (Vicinanza et al. 2021).To measure a visionary idea, these scholars measure unusual or unexpected utterances relative to what the model would predict.In the context of organizations, they then can identify which firms are most likely to anticipate the future and achieve higher earnings (small companies).Used in similar ways, these models could naturally be used to understand how symbols are used to signal specific meanings, or use ambiguity to facilitate robust strategic action that speaks different messages to distinct audiences (Padgett and Ansell 1993).These models have also been used to predict salaries given the text in job postings (Bana 2022), to measure firm differentiation (Carlson 2022), as well as to develop a dataset of expressed sentiment indices across 100 countries to track national-and subnational-level affective states on a daily basis (Wang et al. 2022).This last use case could, for example, be easily extended by organizations wishing for a live barometer of employee satisfaction and wellbeing.

We end our discussion of the practical applications of word embeddings by noting that the methodology can be generalized and applied to any arbitrary set of symbols arrayed in a sequence or higher-dimensional context.Indeed, some exciting applications are beginning to emerge that do precisely this.In the context of images, sequences and two-dimensional convolutions or tiles of RGB pixel values have been used to train multidimensional image spaces (Jia et al. 2014, Krizhevsky et al. 2012), often described as image2vec models to stimulate comparison with word2vec models (Jo et al. 2018(Jo et al. , 2020)).

Researchers have used pre-trained image embedding models to measure the novelty and creativity of abstract art images (Banerjee andIngram 2022, Banerjee andKaplan 2022).Multidimensional spaces of music (Liang et al. 2020), audio clips (Hershey et al. 2017, Xie andVirtanen 2019), and video (Zellers et al. 2021) have also been constructed using tools including audio2vec (Tagliasacchi et al. 2020), signal2vec (Nalmpantis and Vrakas 2019) and video2vec (Habibian et al. 2017), opening the door for organizational scholars to engage with new media.Science of science scholars have created embedding spaces of academic journals using journal co-citations (Lin et al. 2022) and a growing field of scholarship enables the embedding of social networks in comparable spaces (e.g., Hamilton et al. 2017).

Along similar lines, scholars have traced the macroscale structure of over 10,000 Reddit communities, positioning communities in a high-dimensional space such that similar communities (like similar concepts) lie close together while dissimilar communities lie far apart (Waller and Anderson 2021).This embedding was trained based on the co-membership relations of community members across different communities.Embeddings can also be constructed for mixed-data spaces by simulating structure that links other forms of data.A recent analysis predicted future discoveries by simulating random walks over the hypergraph of research papers, where each paper is represented by a hyperedge containing authors, physical materials, and human-valued properties.The density of random walks constructed over this hypergraph correspond to the inferences about material properties that are cognitively available to scientists, and embeddings trained on the elements within these random walks double the degree to which new discoveries are predicted (Sourati and Evans 2021).

All of the studies in this section suggest that the scope of embedding methodology could in the future be applied to other contexts where sequence data is prevalent such as strategic business moves, sports plays, career trajectories, trading behavior, among others.

We have thus far limited our attention to discussing the structure of embedding models and how they relate to conceptual spaces, how they help to overcome limitations of prior text analysis tools, and some of their practical applications.We here switch to address their limitations, offering thoughts on their seriousness along with ways in which they can be, or have been, ameliorated.

The first two limitations are necessarily flip sides of their strengths.The fine-grained information produced by word embedding models comes with costs that a particular study may or may not be able to sustain.The first is size.Fine-grained information on each word for hundreds of dimensions or for contextual embeddings, requires much greater storage than simple dictionary counts or LDA topic models.Second, word embedding models require more text in order to estimate conceptual space with precision than prior models.This challenge may be compensated for with transfer learning when large corpora are similar enough to the subject of study and can be used as a proxy for theoretically relevant corpora or the initialization of a fine-tuning process.Nevertheless, sometimes relevant language is sufficiently different in content, purpose or form that it requires independent modeling, but small enough that it cannot sustain stable estimation of an embedding model.In such contexts, the use of dictionary counts or topic models may be superior because the data can only sustain coarse-grained associations, which those methods are designed to capture.

A third limitation is that static word embeddings do not deal natively with polysemy, instances where a word (e.g., "bank") encodes multiple concepts (e.g., financial institution, side of a river, tilting sideways).While the existence of polysemous words could impact some of the measures proposed above, there are countervailing issues.On the one hand, research has found that the meanings of polysemous words are encoded within the word's vector in linear superposition to each other (Arora et al. 2018).This means that the algorithm is encoding a word's position in conceptual space by taking all of the meanings into account at once, overcoming what could otherwise be serious drawbacks depending on the model's use case.On the other hand, the contextual embedding architecture explicitly tackles the issue of polysemous words by outputting different vectors depending on the context that surrounds a focal word.

Therefore, if an analyst suspects that polysemy could be a serious issue for a particular analysis, they could engage with contextual embeddings and circumvent this concern.

A fourth potential limitation is that bias from the corpus used to train the embedding will become encoded in the word embedding model itself.This can be especially pernicious when the model will be used in real-world, downstream applications such as classification and prediction.For example, race and gender stereotypes hardcoded into the embedding could lead to unfair and unethical predictions (e.g., likelihood of financial default) and therefore lead to incorrect decisions (e.g., denial of credit).Scholars should proactively consider whether such negative externalities are possible given the research questions and design.Nevertheless, to the degree that understanding the nature of conceptual associations within the communities and contexts of study is paramount, researchers will require these biases encoded into their models.If they were not included, the model, and therefore the research design, would miss important social and cultural regularities from the context under study.

A fifth potential limitation is that if the analyst does not have a clear understanding of the context that generated the corpus, they could end up making inferences that are not applicable or relevant.For example, research that has highlighted the changing nature of meaning through time (Caliskan et al. 2017, Garg et al. 2018, Kozlowski et al. 2019), suggests situations in which word meanings change suddenly as a consequence of exogenous shocks, reconfiguring the structure of conceptual associations across the space.A simple example here is how the meaning of "Katrina" changed after Hurricane Katrina in 2005.

A more far-reaching example could be how meanings surrounding financial terms were reconfigured in the aftermath of the 2009 financial crisis, including the addition of many terms such as "TARP."Ignoring such exogenous shocks to meaning could lead to misguided and problematic interpretations of the measures described above, leading to faulty inferences.

A final potential limitation is that, to the degree that the textual data in the corpus is a product of transcribed vocal utterances (e.g., European Central Bank or Federal Reserve chairperson speeches, political speeches, earnings calls, television or movie transcripts, conversational interactions), the intonation and tone of speech will not be taken into account.This may be more or less problematic depending on the social context in which the utterances were made and the language in which they were made, given that some languages such as Chinese rely more heavily on intonation to communicate meaning.Therefore, if there is a risk that intonation and tone play an important role in the context of the corpus, scholars should discuss the consequences of their embedding model choices and interpretive decisions made.

We conclude by briefly discussing what we see as worthwhile opportunities for theory, methodology, and organizations themselves that stem from understanding embedding models as fine-grained representations of conceptual space.This discussion is necessarily illustrative, yet we hope also suggestive of the broad possibilities now actionable.

At the theoretical level, understanding embedding models as principled, fine-grained representations of conceptual space has the potential to spur new theoretical developments and sharpen established theories.On the first of these points, we foreground classical statements in the study of meaning that have made an impact in other fields such as literary theory and cultural sociology but have nevertheless not gained a foothold within organization science.Since the structural turn in linguistics with the work of Saussure (de Saussure 1986) in the early 20th century, there have been a number of innovative attempts to theorize the role of meaning within organizational and social life.Levi-Strauss brought together diverse and far-ranging ethnographies from across the globe to suggest that there was a deep cultural order to the superficial chaos characterizing the world's cultures, arguing that complex meanings emerge from the conjunction of signifying elements (Levi-Strauss 2016).Foucault theorized how discourse and power were intimately interlinked, with power and knowledge being bound together in self-reinforcing alliance (Foucault 2012).Bourdieu articulated the concept of habitus as "systems of durable, transposable dispositions, structured structures predisposed to function as structuring structures, that is, as principles of the generation and structuring of practices" (Bourdieu 1977, p. 72).Yet, despite the appeal of these theories, until now they have been only loosely and indirectly testable.From this lack of analytical foothold, they never achieved prominence within managerial and organizational theory.We anticipate that empirical operationalization of conceptual spaces makes an engagement with these foundational works of cultural theory empirically tractable as many of their constructs are now defensibly measurable.Embedding models stand to make these theories relevant to managerial and organizational theory.

On the second point regarding the sharpening of existing theories, we hope that embedding models will open the door to deeper investigation in the context of existing theoretical frameworks.An interesting set of literatures that stand to benefit here are those that interface with knowledge.Given that much of the knowledge accessible to organization scholars is encoded in the symbolic conceptual system of language, knowledge is now accessible through ever more common sources of textual data and can be represented through the conceptual spaces of embedding models.Indeed, recent work within materials science has used such models to effectively predict future knowledge discoveries decades before they are put forward by scientists (Tshitoyan et al. 2019) while other work demonstrates that these findings can generalize to the biological and physical sciences (Shi and Evans 2019).At a minimum, explicit representation of conceptual spaces gives us a tractable hold for investigating the nature and structure of knowledge at scale across a social system, but also in fine-grained detail.On the one hand, such models can serve as telescopes that open up the skies of knowledge for theoretical development and refinement, making the large-scale structure of our knowledge systems visible for investigation.On the other hand, these models can serve as microscopes allowing us to peer deeply into the atomic structures of meaning from which larger knowledge systems are built.This advancement in measurement stands to enrich the testing of mechanisms in the large, multidimensional systems of knowledge that define the human experience.

At the empirical level, word embedding models stand to improve the fidelity of measurement across different domains of organizational science, leading to better mappings between empirical results and theoretical claims and frameworks.We illustrate this point with the example of research on diversity within teams and groups.Many of the benefits ascribed to diverse groups are said to follow from differences in how individuals within a group represent problems and solutions (Hong and Page 2004).

Groups composed of individuals with different approaches will better perform across a wide variety of tasks because they will have broader knowledge, perspectives, and informational resources from which to draw (Cox et al. 1991, Williams andO'Reilly 1998).Yet, due to measurement difficulties, research on team diversity does not measure distinct conceptualizations of problem and solution spaces.Rather, this research tradition necessarily assumes a link between the diversity of identities-demographic, cultural, ethnic, or experiential-among the problem-solving team members and its functional diversity-how team members actually represent and solve problems (van Dijk et al. 2017, Hong and Page 2004, Nisbett and Ross 1980).

The link between identity and functional diversity has generally been assumed given the lack of methods for accessing the location of team members with the conceptual spaces of problems and solutions.An important consequence of this disconnect between identity diversity used to operationalize research and functional diversity used to theorize it has been that while theory has actively used ideas and terminology from functional diversity, which is geometric and high-dimensional in nature, tests of theory have relied on set-theoretic notions associated with identity diversity.This might go part of the way toward explaining the equivocality of many results from the team diversity literature (van Dijk et al.

2017), as our research designs are blind to the homology between functional and identity diversity.

Measures like conceptual breadth, however, stand to elucidate open questions at this theoretical intersection, as we can now specify (1) the underlying conceptual breadth of teams, and (2) the degree to which results might be driven by this underlying breadth.Addressing these issues stands to inform research at many levels of analysis, from individual and team success (van Dijk et al. 2017, Srikanth et al. 2016) to firm and industry performance (Roberson et al. 2017).We hope that our examples have stimulated new possibilities for generating more fine-grained measures of meaning within a range of organizational research domains.

Finally, we suspect that word embedding methods will have an impact within the organizations we study.We illustrate the potential that embeddings have to shape organizational behavior in the context of labor markets.A central challenge for human resource management, from recruitment to job design, from training to promotions, is to effectively match individuals to well-aligned roles, jobs, situations, and tasks within organizations (Weller et al. 2019).As match quality improves, a variety of performance measures improve, including job satisfaction (Ashforth and Saks 1996), individual productivity (Paauwe 2009), and organizational performance (Dyer and Reeves 1995).One issue with effective matching is the degree to which different dimensions of match matter.For example, in one firm skills may matter above all, yet in other firms it may be the interplay of cultural fit, attitudes, skills, and specific expertise.As embedding models capture all of these dimensions, managers could embed distinct archetypal descriptions for each relevant dimension while also embedding the profiles or other relevant communication (e.g., emails, slack messages, etc.) from individuals to measure the match proximity between each individual and each relevant archetype.Doing so would allow managers to better understand the complexity and stability of matches and their consequences for workers within the firm.

Embedding models also stand to shed new light on the macro-level management of human resources, given that a difficulty of effective matching is identifying and accessing relevant information for making the initial match (Weller et al. 2019).For example, relevant information from large organizations is stored in human resource managers, frontline managers, employees, peers, and external recruiters.Yet, there is no centralized access to this information.Now, however, organizations could build conceptual spaces from the text of all their textual breadcrumbs, including emails, chats, job descriptions, formal reports, performance management records, etc. Doing so and using similarity analysis would allow firms to map and understand where relevant human capital is located across the firm.Managers could take advantage of these systems to know precisely how far away any employee's conceptual position is to any given firm requirement.This could inform processes of recruitment, hiring and fit, employee mobility, turnover, training, socialization, job crafting, and job design.Thus, in the context of labor markets embedding models stand to spur useful innovations.One can imagine many other organizational practices and structures that could benefit from these models and their measurement possibilities, including product design, market analysis, and strategy generation, among others.
