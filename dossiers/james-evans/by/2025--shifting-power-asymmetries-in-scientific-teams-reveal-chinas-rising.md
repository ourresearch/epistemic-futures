# Shifting power asymmetries in scientific teams reveal China’s rising leadership in global science

## Full text

## Abstract

AbstractChina’s emergence as one of the world’s top producers of high-quality science raises critical questions about its trajectory toward achieving scientific leadership. Traditional methods for evaluating the power of national scientific ecosystems, however, often overlook the nuances of a country’s global influence. In this perspective, we introduce a framework that highlights shifting power dynamics in international scientific collaborations, focusing on whether leadership positions in international scientific teams are moving from one country to another. Using rich sociological data from nearly 6 million scientific publications, we document a marked shift in team leadership from Western countries to China. In particular, the share of team leaders involved in U.S-China scientific collaborations that were affiliated with Chinese institutions grew from 30% of the total in 2010 to 45% in 2023. We further explore the implications of China’s rise by forecasting when Chinese scientists are projected to achieve parity in leadership vis-à-vis the United States, including in 11 critical technology areas that are focal points of technological development, and by analyzing how a potential decoupling of U.S.-Chinese science might affect Chinese scientific leadership. We conclude by considering the impacts of China’s growing investments in the training of young scientists in countries participating in the Belt and Road Initiative.Keywords: scientific leadership, development, collaboration, geopolitics

China’s scientific landscape has undergone a remarkable transformation over the past two decades, positioning the country as the world’s largest producer of high-quality research (1, 2). This ascent has not only reshaped global scientific output and accelerated progress (3–6), but has also raised important questions about the nature and extent of China’s influence within the international scientific community. Observers have begun to ask whether China is moving beyond being a significant contributor to science and toward a position of international scientific leadership, where it leads in setting the global research agenda rather than merely supporting research agendas developed by other countries (7).

While traditional metrics, such as publication counts and citation indices, provide a quantitative snapshot of China’s growing scientific output (1), they do not directly measure the ability of a country to organize global scientific labor and resources to meet its research objectives. At the same time, there is evidence that China is succeeding along this dimension. China has now awarded more doctoral degrees than the United States in every year since 2006 (2), and 10% of the doctoral degrees granted by U.S. institutions in recent years have been awarded to Chinese nationals (8). Following substantial efforts to advance its domestic universities and attract leading scientists working abroad, China may now be not only a producer of high-quality science but also an emerging organizer of the global collaboration network.

In this perspective, we seek to open a pathway to study China’s growing leadership in international science by focusing on the changing position of power of its scientists in their collaborations across international borders. Our approach begins with the division of labor in coordinated production (9). In scientific teams, heterogeneous tasks of varying complexity are partitioned across collaborators with different skills (10). The growing expanse of scientific knowledge has increased not only the size of teams (11, 12) but also the differentiation of their roles, with principal investigators and other team leaders appearing on bylines with dozens of supporting collaborators (10). Role differentiation produces power asymmetries, because collaborators are unequally involved in the topic selection, funding acquisition, study design, and manuscript writing stages of a research project (13). Because these tasks involve extensive decision-making, the individuals who perform them can exert disproportionate influence over the direction of a research project.

The rise of international scientific collaboration has extended the division of scientific labor globally (14). While more countries are integrated into the global scientific community, they do not stand on equal footing, with scientists from some countries more likely to occupy leadership positions than others. In both process and consequence, global science reflects the global distribution of economic production, where a “core-periphery” structure concentrates leadership functions in certain countries, shaping patterns of influence and access to the benefits of scientific research (15–19). For a country to achieve greater autonomy in global science, its scientists need to develop stronger independent research capabilities, or strengthen their leadership roles in international collaborations (20–22).

Recently, growing antiglobalist sentiment and policies have increased the salience of scientific autonomy. As existing norms of open international scientific collaboration have eroded, countries need to reformulate their abilities to organize and lead scientific research projects, either domestically or through international partnerships (18). Following the China Initiative, an antiespionage effort in the United States, scientists of Chinese descent at U.S. universities reported increased fear and anxiety, which impacted their collaboration and career choices (2). The number of international students from China in U.S. higher education institutions declined precipitously, from 373,000 students in 2019–2020 to 277,000 in 2023–2024 (23). The impact of decoupling US and Chinese science on China depends critically on whether Chinese scientists have developed the independent ability to lead projects that advance the scientific frontier.

To substantiate our perspective on Chinese scientific development, we empirically consider the dynamics of Chinese leadership in global science. Our analysis contains two parts. In the first, we identify the leaders of 5,991,656 scientific teams that collaborated bilaterally across international borders (while treating the European Union as a single entity), using a simple machine learning model. We identify the share of team leaders involved in such collaborations based at Chinese institutions, generate forecasts for when China is expected to reach parity in terms of its propensity to lead in collaborations with scientists from other countries, and project the potential impacts of a decoupling of U.S.-Chinese science on Chinese scientific leadership. In the second part, we use Chinese administrative data to uncover investments made by China to train young scientists from countries participating in the Belt and Road Initiative, and discuss how such investments may help China strengthen its role in research networks in parts of the world that are currently less integrated into the global research community.

## China’s Leadership in International Teams

To measure China’s leadership position in global science, we trained a supervised machine learning model to predict the leaders of the nearly 6 million papers indexed by OpenAlex that involved collaboration across international borders. Our model builds on the work of ref. 13 and is trained using explicit (albeit imperfect) records of team leadership provided by 83,877 publications sourced from four journals (Science, Nature, PNAS, and PLOS One) that consistently contain digitized contribution statements, and nine author-and-paper-specific features contained in the metadata for each paper. We use the trained model to predict the Lead Probability of each team member on each paper for our full dataset of nearly 6 million papers. Lead Probability values range from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating a greater likelihood of serving as an intellectual leader, such as being described in the contribution statement as having “designed” the research or “wrote” the paper (see SI Appendix for details). All data for the study is available in a public repository (24).

We aggregate the resulting data to the country level based on the country of authors’ affiliations, and study China’s leadership relative to its largest collaboration partner, the United States. We find that the number of China-affiliated scientists serving as leaders in collaborations with U.S.-based scientists increased substantially over our study period. Early in our study period (2010–2012), there were only 4,429 scientists from China with lead probability values greater than 0.65 engaged in collaborations with the United States, representing 30% of the total (Fig. 1A). However, by the end of the study period (2021–2023), the number of Chinese scientists with lead probabilities greater than 0.65 engaged in collaborations with the United States had risen to 12,714, representing 45% of the total (Fig. 1B).

To quantify China’s relative leadership with other countries, we developed a binary definition of lead versus support scientists: “lead scientists” are collaborators with predicted lead probabilities that exceed the threshold value of 0.65, and “support scientists” are those with lead probabilities less than or equal to 0.65. Using these definitions, we computed two measures: 1) China’s Lead Share with its partner countries, defined as the share of leaders involved in China–US, China–EU, and China–UK collaborations that worked at Chinese-based institutions in each year, and 2) China’s Lead Premium with each partner region, which expresses its leadership in per-collaborator terms. We calculate China’s Lead Premium by taking its Lead Share minus its Support Share. China’s Lead Premium thus expresses its leadership in per-collaborator terms, measuring the extent to which its international collaborators are biased toward leadership roles as opposed to supportive ones. Two examples of countries with high Lead Premiums but lower Lead Shares might be Switzerland and Singapore, because they have a relatively small number of scientists, but their scientists tend to be global leaders.

We plot China’s Lead Share vis-à-vis the United States, EU, and United Kingdom in Fig. 1C, and we plot China’s Lead Premium vis-à-vis these regions in Fig. 1D. In 2010, China’s Lead Share with the United States stood at 30%, but by 2023 its Lead Share had risen to 45%. The relationship is approximated by a linear regression (y = 0.012 × −24.25), which is overlaid with 95% CIs in Fig. 1C. The upper and lower bounds of the CI predict that China will reach parity with the United States in its Lead Share between 2027 and 2028 if the trends continue. For China and the EU, the model predicts parity between 2025 and 2027. For China and the United Kingdom, the model indicates that China already reached parity as of 2019.

While our Lead Share model predicts that Chinese parity with the United States is imminent in terms of its Lead Share, our Lead Premium model predicts that China’s catch-up is progressing more slowly in per-collaborator terms (Fig. 1D). China is not expected to reach parity in its Lead Premium with the United States until after 2087, with the EU between 2051 and 2108, and with the United Kingdom between 2032 and 2036. Thus, China is likely to continue facing challenges in elevating its large number of support scientists into leadership roles within the global collaboration network. This finding has important policy implications for Chinese policymakers concerned with distributional outcomes, and it also helps reconcile the widespread perception that much of China’s research is not yet at the forefront of the scientific frontier with its growing role in leading global collaborations. In SI Appendix, we show that China is not expected to reach parity with the United States in its Lead Premium across multiple cuts of the data.

To explore the robustness of our findings, we replicated our main analyses using two additional publication datasets: Microsoft Academic Graph (MAG), and a subset of publications that could be linked across the OpenAlex and Web of Science (WOS) datasets (SI Appendix). We found similar results using these alternative datasets: The MAG data predict China–US parity in Lead Share between 2028 and 2029 (SI Appendix, Fig S28), while the linked OpenAlex-WOS dataset predicts China–US parity in Lead Share between 2027 and 2029 (SI Appendix, Fig. S30). Furthermore, to account for the possibility of “honorary authorships,” where an individual may be listed on the byline of papers without contributing materially to the research, we developed a method to detect honorary authors and remove them from the dataset (SI Appendix). We found similar results after making this correction (SI Appendix, Fig. S12).

## Chinese Leadership Following Hypothetical China–US Decoupling

How might Chinese scientific leadership change if the United States and China were to decouple their science, reducing collaboration between the two countries (25)? Extending our framework allows us to explore this possibility numerically.

To estimate how US–China decoupling might affect Chinese global scientific leadership, we combined the Lead Share values predicted by our model for 2030 (Fig. 1C) with records on the frequency of collaboration between Chinese scientists and their main partner countries and regions. We use these data to compute China’s 2030 global scientific leadership under three distinct decoupling scenarios. In the first scenario, we assume no decoupling: China and the United States collaborate as intensively in 2030 as they did in the most recent year for which we have data, 2023. In the second, we assume partial U.S.-China decoupling: China and the United States are assumed to collaborate half as frequently in 2030 as they did in 2023, and China’s scientists that used to collaborate with the United States are reallocated to collaborations with other partner regions (including domestic collaborations within China), proportional to China’s 2023 collaboration intensities with those regions. In the third scenario, we assume complete decoupling, with proportional reallocation. For each scenario, we compute China’s weighted mean Lead Share as the weighted mean of its predicted 2030 Lead Share with each partner region, weighted by the share of its international collaborators that collaborate with each region. The calculations are shown in Table 1.

Table 1 shows that decoupling US-Chinese science is associated with an increase in China’s global scientific leadership. This outcome is driven by the reallocation of Chinese scientists from collaborations with the United States—with whom Chinese scientists are less likely to lead—to collaborations with partners in other regions, with whom Chinese scientists are more likely to lead. For example, our model predicts that China’s Lead Share will be 0.53 with the United States in 2030, while its Lead Share with the United Kingdom will be 0.75. If US–China decoupling causes Chinese scientists to collaborate more frequently with the U.K., its global weighted Lead Share will increase. This result highlights the multilateral nature of global science leadership and policy.

A second important point raised by Table 1 is that U.S.-China decoupling would increase the propensity for Chinese scientists to collaborate domestically. This change could generate unpredictable consequences. While an increasingly domestic orientation of Chinese science would limit opportunities for its scientists to access foreign knowledge, it would also restrict the flow of Chinese knowledge to the rest of the world. Given that China is expected to reach parity in its Lead Share with the United States in 2027–2028 (Fig. 1C), the net impacts of reduced knowledge flow between the two countries are hard to predict. Needless to say, the global rate of scientific advance would certainly decline following a U.S.-China decoupling.

A final point raised by Table 1 is that if China’s science were to become more domestically oriented, or increasingly shift toward the Chinese-language research community, the ability for other countries to observe and assess China’s scientific capabilities would decline, because these international collaborations provide information on the relative sophistication of the scientists in each country. A similar point applies to the ability for China to assess the status of U.S. science. With an impeded ability to cross-assess, diplomatic efforts between countries would rely on poorer information.

## Journal Quality and Leadership Threshold

One possible explanation for the trends we observe in China’s growing international leadership is that Chinese scientists may publish in lower-quality journals than do scientists from the United States, EU, and United Kingdom. To test whether China’s growing international leadership may be restricted to low-quality journals, we filter our data by journals based on their 2021 Web of Science Impact Factor. Fig. 2A shows predicted years of parity, with 95% CIs, for China’s Lead Share with the United States across five impact factor journal bins. The point estimates predict that China will reach parity with the United States no later than 2034, irrespective of journal impact factor.

A second potential driver of our results is that China’s growth may be restricted to “mid-level leaders.” Moreover, Western countries like the United States might be retaining top-level leaders. To explore this possibility, we ran our model separately with different thresholds to define leaders, which was set at 0.65 in the previous analyses. The point estimates predict that China will reach parity in Lead Share with the United States at even the highest thresholds within the next two decades (Fig. 2C).

## Chinese Leadership in Critical Technologies

Global interest in scientific leadership is linked to the centrality of science for economic production, the provision of IT infrastructure, and maintenance of security. In the United States, the NSF’s TIP Directorship defined 11 Critical Technology areas deemed essential for national objectives. These include Advanced Manufacturing, Advanced Materials, Artificial Intelligence, Biotechnology, Communications and Wireless, Cyberinfrastructure and Advanced Computing, Cybersecurity, Disaster Risk and Resilience, Energy Technology, Quantum Technology, and Semiconductors and Microelectronics. To assess the status of China–US leadership in these technologies, we ran our model separately for teams that work in each area, as well as a composite grouping, “All Critical Tech Areas,” which aggregates these 11 fields (see SI Appendix for methods details).

Fig. 3 shows China’s Lead Share in bilateral scientific cooperation with the United States over time in each of the 11 Critical Technology areas, plus the composite category (Lead Premium results shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S4). China is predicted to reach parity in Lead Share with the United States in the composite category between 2026 and 2030. Additionally, China is predicted to reach parity in Lead Share with the United States in 8 of the 11 Critical Technology Areas (AI, Disaster Prevention, Data Management and Security, Semiconductors, Advanced Communications, Energy, Materials Science, and High Performance Computing) before 2030. In Biotechnology, parity is not expected until 2030–2036, and in Quantum Information, not until after 2028. In one field (Robotics and Advanced Manufacturing), our data are sparse, leading to noisy predictions.

In addition to these Critical Technology Areas, we also analyzed our results by 6 broad scientific fields (SI Appendix, Fig. S22). We found that China is on track to reach parity in terms of its Lead Share with the United States earliest in Chemistry and Materials Science (parity between 2023 and 2027), Mathematics, Physics, and Engineering (parity between 2024 and 2031), and Computer Science (parity between 2025 and 2028), and latest in Medicine (parity between 2028 and 2038).

## China’s Investments in International Scientific Development

Beyond the United States, EU, and United Kingdom, we also find that China is engaged in supporting human capital development in countries that currently have underdeveloped university systems, particularly in low-income countries participating in the Belt and Road Initiative. In addition to advancing scientific capacity in these nations, these investments may also provide China with advantages associated with leadership in educational training and research, including the strengthening of shared research norms and cultures, the cultivation of trust, and the development of international resources to advance their shared objectives.

To arrive at these determinations, we first examined budgetary data from China’s Ministry of Education (26), which reveal that China budgeted approximately 33.3 billion RMB ($4.59 billion USD) between 2012 and 2025 to support the education of international students in China (Fig. 4A). This amount is only 40% less than the amount China spent on its own nationals studying abroad and is 68% of the size of the total budget requested by the U.S. NSF in 2018. Importantly, while spending for international students in China plummeted during the COVID-19 pandemic, it bounced back afterward.

Student-level data from the Ministry of Education of the People’s Republic of China (27) reveals the geographical origin of international students in China (Fig. 4B). In 2006, most of China’s foreign students were from East Asia, with a smaller share from South Asia. The share from South Asia and Africa grew over time and collectively accounted for nearly half of all of China’s foreign students by 2018, the most recent year for which the Ministry released data. Dotted lines in Fig. 4B shows the number of students from Belt and Road Initiative countries that studied in China each year, broken out by country income level (we classify BRI countries with 2010 nominal GDP per capita above China’s value for 2010 as high-income, and all other BRI countries as low-income). During the time series, China shifted from hosting more Belt and Road students from high-income BRI countries, to hosting more students from low-income BRI countries. In addition, in SI Appendix, Figs. S24 and S25 we show that the international graduate students in China are disproportionately from BRI countries and are associated with the training of advanced skills and sophisticated human capital.

While the impact of these investments extend beyond scientific research to industrial and managerial domains, data on international collaborations between scientists in China and in Belt and Road-signatory countries show how China has established a leadership position in scientific collaborations with those countries. Fig. 4C shows that China’s Lead Share in collaborations with low-income Belt and Road countries is high (and has been since the start of the data panel) and that China reached parity in its Lead Share with high-income Belt and Road countries in 2020. Thus, China’s investments are beginning to show measurable outcomes across Belt and Road participating countries and may further expand as it adopts a more open stance toward international academic engagement.

## Discussion

Our findings document the rapid growth of China’s scientific role and leadership in the global scientific community. Analyzing the full corpus of bilateral collaborations between China, the United States, the EU, and the United Kingdom, we find that China is on track to achieve a leadership position in scientific collaboration with Western countries in absolute terms within the next decade. These findings extend to leadership in high-impact journals, the most elite levels of leadership, and 8 of the 11 technological areas central to ongoing diplomatic discussions.

When considering policy action, these developments demand careful reflection. China and the United States benefit enormously from one-another’s excellence in science: Scientists from the two countries collaborate more frequently than any other country or global-region pair, with the exception of the United States and EU (SI Appendix, Fig. S1), and recent evidence from AI research reveals how collaborations between China and the United States can disproportionately benefit science as a collective endeavor (6). Policies to isolate scientific research in either country would entail large costs to both countries, as well as the rest of the world that benefits from the outputs of their research. Additionally, China has made considerable progress in assuming leadership roles in collaborations with countries with established research ecosystems, and it has actively supported the training of scientists in emerging economies participating in the Belt and Road Initiative. These developments limit the ability to isolate Chinese science, a point that is confirmed by our calculation of the impacts of a US–China decoupling on China’s global scientific leadership (Table 1).

For policymakers in the United States, China’s investment in scientific enterprises across the Belt and Road Initiative countries inspires a different course of action: The United States can deepen its investments in global human capital development. By expanding its scientific engagement in the developing world, through education, training, and collaboration, the United States can train a global workforce in the skills demanded by the US economy, facilitate cultural exchange, and strengthen its role as a leading hub of knowledge work, both in science and other knowledge-intensive domains.

For policymakers in China, the increase in China’s Lead Share with partner countries is notable and indicates that the policies and measures China enacted over the last 30 years to develop an advanced scientific workforce largely succeeded in making the country’s elite scientists global leaders. At the same time, the slower growth of China’s Lead Premium, documented in Fig. 1D and reaffirmed by analyses in SI Appendix (particularly SI Appendix, Figs. S3 and S4), indicates that China’s existing policies have yet to shift the mass of its large population of scientists into leadership roles. Given China’s potential demographic changes, the slow improvement in its Lead Premium is an important consideration for Chinese policymakers. Importantly, China’s recent investments in cultivating scientific ecosystems in Belt and Road countries may create opportunities to improve along this measure, because collaborations with these countries could allow more Chinese scientists to take on leadership roles in international teams.

Most importantly, our findings suggest that the dynamics of U.S.-China competition in scientific leadership can be shifted in a more productive and globally beneficial direction. The outcomes-positive or negative-depends on the nature of that competition. Efforts to restrain the scientific progress of either country are risky and unlikely to succeed without substantial costs to their own advancement, given the sophistication and interdependency of both countries’ scientific enterprises. On the other hand, competition that takes the form of integrating each country’s science more deeply into global scientific networks, through investments in human capital development, would generate positive externalities for the world, by expanding global educational opportunities and accelerating scientific advance.
