Abstract
Scientific research is often characterized by schools of thought. We investigate whether these
divisions arise in part from differences in researchers’ cognitive traits such as their tolerance for
ambiguity and the vividness of their visual imagery. These differences may guide researchers to
prefer different types of problems, to tackle the same problems in different ways, and perhaps
even reach different conclusions when studying the same problems in the same way. We surveyed
7973 researchers in psychology and allied disciplines and investigated links between what they
research (area, topic, methodology), their stances on open questions in psychological science,
and a set of validated scales assessing a variety of cognitive traits and dispositions. We also
linked responses to publication histories from which we constructed landscapes of similarity and
difference based on publication titles and abstracts, citations and co-authorship patterns. Our
results show that researchers’ stances on scientific questions are, as expected, predictable from
what kind of research they do. But they are also predictable from their cognitive traits, even
when controlling for their research areas, methods and topics. Further, all of these effects are
detectable in co-authorship and citation patterns. These findings support the idea that divisions
in scientific fields reflect not only differences in the kinds of data brought to bear on a topic, but
also differences in the researchers themselves, hinting that the divisions may be more difficult
to bridge than suggested by the traditional model of data-driven scientific consensus.

1

The view of science as the objective pursuit of knowledge suggests that when scientists are

2

faced with the same data, they should reach the same conclusions. On this view, disagreements

3

between scientists are driven by differences in what they know. As data mounts up and knowledge

4

gaps diminish, so should disagreements. In many cases, this normative (‘pro-epistemic’ 1 ) view is

5

justified. Certain ideas, clearly disconfirmed by data, have all but vanished. For example, the

6

Ptolemaic Earth-centric view was displaced by Copernican heliocentrism. Lamarck’s hypothesis of

7

inherited characteristics was largely discarded by the scientific community as evidence mounted in
1

8

favor of Darwin’s theory of natural selection 2 . Phlogiston, the luminiferious aether, and caloric fluid

9

are all consigned to the past.

10

While some disagreements are resolved by data that unambiguously support one side or another,

11

many controversies persist and become entrenched “schools of thought”. For example, social sciences

12

are divided by factions that favor social constructionism vs. biological essentialism 3–6 , dispositional

13

versus situational explanations for human behavior 7,8 , and biological reductionism versus theories

14

that emphasize social and cultural causal autonomy 9,10 .

15

The clustered nature of such differences suggests that fields like psychology are fractured into

16

distinct cultures 11 . But such divisions are not unique to psychology or the social sciences. Theoret-

17

ical physics is split over whether string theory or loop quantum gravity best unifies inconsistencies

18

in quantum theory and gravitational fields 12,13 . In biology, researchers can agree on the data while

19

still producing substantially different explanations of it 14 . Even Lamarck’s ideas are not completely

20

dormant, with some researchers suggesting a Lamarckian lens can inform recent discoveries in epi-

21

genetics 2,15,16 .

22

What factors determine whether researchers align themselves with one school of thought or

23

another? One possibility is that researchers across scientific divisions do not, in fact, know the same

24

things. A researcher who is most familiar with data supporting a particular school of thought will

25

naturally align themselves with it. A related factor—one that can help explain how such differential

26

knowledge can arise—is educational and professional networks 17,18 . A graduate student finding

27

themselves in a lab that uses a specific method or espouses a specific theory will become an expert

28

in that method or theory and its associated literature, while being less knowledgeable of alternative

29

approaches.

30

It is also possible that academic disagreements persist due to individual differences not directly

31

related to the research itself. For instance, a previous study observed sincere differences of opinion

32

about the inferential value of different research methods for controversial topics in psychology, and

33

sought to explain those differences by appeal to political ideology 6 . However, this approach produced

34

mostly null results.

35

Another possibility is that disagreements stem in part from differences in researchers’ cognitive

36

traits causing them to find certain types of approaches, findings and theories more or less compelling.

2

37

Indeed, scientists inhabiting various factions often differ substantially in their theoretical positions

38

despite using the same research methods and being aware of the same empirical data 19 . Differences

39

in cognitive dispositions may draw scientists to pursue certain approaches in the first place. After

40

all, most of the time a graduate student does not simply find themselves in a certain lab—they

41

actively choose it. It is possible that these choices are made in part because of a match between

42

what an approach or philosophy favored by the student and that practiced in the lab, unintentionally

43

reinforcing existing fractures. If some common cognitive dispositions contribute to the formation of

44

research clusters, it would be unsurprising that schools of thought are both ubiquitous and persistent.

45

The idea that researchers’ work is impacted by their psychological traits—in tandem with their

46

education and social contexts—has a long history in the psychology of science 20–22 . In particular,

47

science is a kind of problem solving 23,24 and cognitive traits predict differences in problem solving

48

from hypothesis generation to hypothesis testing 25–27 . Cognitive traits have been linked to what

49

field someone chooses: higher systematizing quotients have been associated with people who pursue

50

physical sciences while higher empathizing quotients has been associated with pursuing the humani-

51

ties 28 . Scientists report greater spatial visual imagery than humanities researchers; the latter report

52

higher object visual imagery 29,30 . Differences in some personality dimensions has also been found to

53

predict endorsement of mechanistic/objectivist vs. organismic/holistic worldviews across fields 31 .

54

Work examining these kinds of effects among researchers within a field is rarer. Within psy-

55

chology, researchers who use more qualitative approaches have been found to favor more holistic

56

explanations while those who use more quantitative approaches favor more analytic explanations 32 .

57

Differences along particular psychological dimensions have also been linked to specific disagreements

58

in specialist subfields. For example, cognitive scientists’ stance in the imagery debate (whether vi-

59

sual imagery plays a constitutive or largely epiphenomenal role 33,34 ) has been linked to the vividness

60

of the scientists’ own visual imagery 35,36 .

61

The studies just mentioned have either focused on broad differences across sciences and humani-

62

ties, without explaining persistent disagreements between people who study similar things in similar

63

ways, or concern smaller-scale disagreements within a highly specialized topic. Neither help us in

64

explaining how a broad field might fracture into schools of thought. Importantly, the existing work

65

also has not controlled for differences in research methods and topics of study, making it difficult to

3

66

infer the role of a researcher’s cognitive dispositions.

67

Study overview

68

To better understand the links between scientific divisions and differences in cognitive dispositions—

69

even among researchers studying the same topics—we conducted a large-scale survey of researchers

70

in psychology and allied disciplines such as cognitive neuroscience.

71

We recruited academic researchers to complete a survey that probed their stance on controver-

72

sial themes in psychology (Table S2). The survey also included several validated scales measuring

73

individual differences in cognitive traits. In order to understand academics’ specific research back-

74

grounds, we asked which areas of psychology they identified with, which topics they studied, and

75

which common research methods or tools they typically used. These classes of survey variable

76

(controversial themes, cognitive measures, research areas, topics and methods) are summarised in

77

Table 1, with further details provided in Methods, as well as Fig. S1 and Table S2. Respondents

78

also provided demographic information such as their gender, age, and academic position (graduate

79

student, junior faculty, etc).

80

To evaluate whether differences in researchers’ stances and cognitive traits are tied to actual

81

scientific output, we also asked respondents for their consent to anonymously link their survey

82

responses with their publication records in the Web of Science and Microsoft Academic Graph.

83

We applied machine learning techniques to these data to build a citation model (indicating what

84

literature the researchers draw on), a semantic model based on published abstracts and titles

85

(indicating what sort of work they publish), and a co-authorship model (indicating potential

86

paths of mutual influence).

87

Using these data, we sought to understand whether differences in cognitive traits/dispositions

88

among psychology researchers are linked to their stances on various controversial themes; and

89

whether such traits and stances predict clustering into research areas. If scientific schools of thought

90

are associated with interpersonal differences, they may be more deeply entrenched, and more difficult

91

to bridge, than the traditional view of science suggests.

Cognitive traits

185

That one’s choice of research area and research method is yoked to how one thinks about research is

186

not especially surprising and need not imply a causal relationship. A researcher who, for whatever

187

188

and research questions that focus on the individual (or specific component systems) as units of

189

analysis. It is more surprising if one’s stance on scientific questions is predicted by putatively

190

stable 38 cognitive/dispositional traits (Fig. 3c).

Underlying beliefs

238

There are striking patterns of similarity in how the controversial themes cluster across the various

Discussion

359

Our findings provide evidence for the idea that science represents an ecology of diverse perspectives

360

on contentious themes not straightforwardly resolved by the accumulation of more evidence.

361

Researchers often disagree. These disagreements can stem from differences in knowledge, train-

362

ing, and expertise. But access to the same data and use of common methodology do not guarantee

363

agreement. Instead, disciplines often fracture into schools of thought with diverging stances. We

364

focused on divisions among researchers in psychological science, asking them about their stances on

365

16 controversial themes (Fig. 1). These included far-reaching issues such as the importance of the

366

social environment on cognition, the connection between language and thought, and the utility of

367

computational metaphors and mathematical models for human cognition.

368

Where researchers stood on these topics was correlated in both expected and unexpected ways

369

with what the researchers study and how they study it. For example, use of neuroimaging was

370

associated with having a lower belief in the importance of social environments. This suggests that

371

it is not simply harder for those using neuroimaging techniques to study social groups—they are

372

also less impressed by theories that view social context as foundational for understanding human

373

cognition. More generally, the use of specific research methods comes bundled with foundational

374

beliefs about what is important and what signifies plausibility and ultimately, truth itself. Differences

375

in researchers’ stances on our controversial themes were also associated with individual differences in

376

various cognitive traits ranging from ‘tolerance of ambiguity’, ‘creativity’, and ‘breadth of interest’

377

to the ‘need for cognition’, ‘visual imagery’ and ‘deliberation’.

19

378

Some epistemic commitments reflect widely held stereotypes, such as women valuing social con-

379

text more than men. Others such as the association between spatial imagery and math are large-scale

380

replications of a pattern well-known to education researchers (for review, see ref 45 ). But like the

381

bulk of an iceberg invisible beneath the water, most associations are much less obvious. These

382

include the correlation of gender with realism, visual orientation with biological reductionism, and

383

many others (Fig. 3).

384

The results illustrate that incommensurability—difficulty mapping between one scientific paradigm

385

and another, whether due to differing theories, concepts, methods or measures 49 —is not purely an

386

issue of abstract logic, but is also rooted in individual cognition 50 . Divergence in stances on contro-

387

versial themes and underlying cognitive traits do not simply mean that researchers solve problems

388

in different ways. Rather, they reflect differences in how scientists approach, represent, and reason

389

about those problems. Moreover, our findings suggest that psychologists have different attitudes

390

regarding what solutions are epistemically satisfying and worthy of contributing to the canon of

391

psychological fact. These divisions run deep.

392

Insofar as many such differences in foundational beliefs are often hidden, one of the goals of this

393

project was to make these divisions between visible and available for public evaluation and critique.

394

We do not imply that certain cognitive traits are associated with worse science, but demonstrate

395

how they influence the approaches and answers that psychologists obtain in their research and could

396

slow, side-track, or indefinitely stall the resolution of truth claims within psychological science. In

397

the worst-case scenario, cognitive differences could be exploited to prop up or even canonize an

398

evidentially weak position that is intuitively attractive to researchers in positions of power.

399

An alternative, cross-cutting division of cognitive labor, where cognitively diverse researchers

400

study more topics with more approaches might make for better psychological science 51,52 . Such cog-

401

nitive diversity is particularly crucial at the stage of identifying questions and framing problems 53,54 .

402

However, this would require that researchers not predisposed to frame and investigate psychological

403

phenomena in a particular way to undertake what they might perceive as unsuitable or illogical

404

research for the good of science. This poses the supreme challenge of allocating researchers and

405

resources over questions and methods in order to resolve contentious issues.

20

406

Limitations

407

Choice of field

408

This is the first large-scale study of an entire field’s epistemic or theoretic commitments and their

409

associations with cognitive traits. But a limitation is that we focus on only one field. We chose

410

psychology, in part because it is (and aspires to be) pluralistic 24,55,56 . We do not think that the

411

main claim here—that cognitive traits predict what kinds of ideas scientists find appealing, even

412

holding their research areas and methods constant—is limited to psychology. We would expect to

413

find similar patterns (though naturally involving different theoretic content) if we had chosen other

414

social or behavioral sciences, and potentially even if we had chosen a harder science for this first

415

look. Indeed, an assay of scientific status found psychology to be more like biology than sociology 57 ,

416

though Fig. 5a hints at some heterogeneity in this regard. However, it is important to demonstrate

417

this empirically, and we look forward to future research that tests these ideas in other disciplines.

418

Effect sizes

419

Most effects reported here are small in size. For instance, the correlations in Fig. 3c that passed the

420

Bonferroni-corrected threshold for significance ranged from |r| = 0.04 to |r| = 0.18. Some potential

421

worries regarding these effect sizes are (1) that they could be random noise, only rendered significant

422

due to our large sample size (even after applying the Bonferroni corrections); and (2) if real, these

423

effect sizes are too small to be consequential.

424

Regarding the first worry, we see our sample size (and the corresponding breadth of research

425

areas, topics and methods illustrated in Fig. S1) as a major strength of our approach—and crucial for

426

investigating divisions of thought across a field as broad as psychology—and crucial for investigating

427

divisions of thought across a field as broad as psychology. We also address this concern in Appendix

428

4, assessing the robustness of results by repeatedly simulating a range of sample sizes from 300 to

429

3000, rerunning benchmark analyses for each simulated sample size. Fig. S11 illustrates how our

430

reported effects would still be detectable at much smaller sample sizes.

431

More importantly, the consistency of the patterns in our data speaks against the idea that

432

they are random noise. For instance, across panels of Fig. 3, the themes ‘Social environment’,

21

433

‘Context matters’ and ‘Holistic view’ consistently cluster together, regardless whether those clusters

434

are defined according to associations with research methods, research topics, cognitive measures or

435

gender. It is hard to see how such consistent patterns would emerge if each of those associations

436

was just noise amplified by a large sample size.

437

Regarding the second worry, we think it is important to calibrate how large of an effect would

438

be plausible for this phenomenon. Previously reported 31 correlations between personality traits and

439

the sorts of theoretic commitments considered here ranged from r = 0.28 to r = 0.38. We consider

440

this an upper estimate of plausibility, as that study examined effects across fields from physics and

441

mathematics to sociology and anthropology. Two average psychologists would likely be more similar

442

in terms of theoretic commitments than an average physicist and an average sociologist, so effects

443

within a field should be smaller than the aforementioned effects across such a broad range of fields.

444

Moreover, looking beyond academia for plausible effect sizes linking worldviews with attitudes to

445

science, our recent estimate 58 for the correlation between conservative political ideology and distrust

446

in science in a large international sample is r = 0.23. If traits as easily observable as personality

447

and political ideology have an effect on science beliefs just greater than r = 0.2, we would surely

448

expect the effects of information processing dispositions to be harder to detect. After all, it is easier

449

to judge whether one’s closest friends are extroverted or liberal than it is to judge whether they rely

450

more on object vs spatial imagery.

451

Overall, if scientists’ thinking were strongly constrained by obvious traits, we all would surely

452

have noticed this by now 54 , and it would offer a pessimistic view of science as being irredeemably

453

under the thumb of human cognitive bias. If, instead, the effects of cognitive traits are detectable

454

(even in the conservative way we have attempted here, for instance by including all null effects from

455

Fig. 3 in the vectors feeding into correlations in Fig. 7) as small-scale influences on the large-scale

456

dynamics of publishing papers, then this seems more in keeping with the complex nature of science

457

as an open-ended undertaking: one that is impacted by human cognition, but not wholly subject to

458

it.

459

It remains for future research to understand the practical significance of how small cognitive

460

effects might play out during scientists’ development, training and career choices, social networks,

461

and complex research projects—topics at the intersection of psychology of science 20–22 and science

22

462

of science 59,60 .

Response rate and recruitment

464

A contribution of our study is that our findings are based on a large number of academics (7,973).

465

However, this sample size nevertheless reflects a low response rate (3%). The survey link was sent

466

out to 278,692 email addresses comprising authors who had published in psychology journals as

467

recorded in WoS. This includes people who may have retired, changed careers, as well as emails that

468

are no longer active and filtered by their mail programs. A review of surveys with similar methods

469

reveals comparable response rates: Deryugina et al. 61 and Houtkoop et al. 62 also used emails from

470

WoS authors resulting in a 3% and 4% response rate, respectively, and Mohammadi et al. 63 obtained

471

email addresses through web links on Twitter accounts, resulting in a 2% response rate.

472

A related concern is whether our respondents are representative of academic psychologists. Fig.

473

S1 that shows our respondents represent a range of demographic characteristics, academic ranks,

474

research topics, and methods. Second, we compared respondents with non-respondents, using data

475

available in WoS, which shows the median first year of publication for respondents is 2009 versus 2008

476

for non-respondents, and the median most recent publication year is 2017 versus 2013, respectively.

477

Fig. S7 shows the distribution of the number of publications for respondents versus non-respondents,

478

and Fig. S8 shows the countries they are from. Overall, these comparisons suggest that our survey

479

respondents are largely similar to non-respondents, but they publish more (especially in psychology),

480

have published more recently, and are slightly more likely to be located in the United States (the

481

distribution of respondents over other countries is similar to the distribution among non-respondents,

482

as Fig. S8 illustrates). These differences should be taken into consideration when interpreting our

483

results.

Selection and measurement of cognitive traits

485

To keep the survey to a manageable size, our assessment of cognitive traits/dispositions was nec-

486

essarily brief and selective. The measures we used have high reliability (e.g., the study validating

487

Tolerance of Ambiguity 39 reported a split-half reliability of .86, and a 6-month retest reliability of

488

.63). However, high reliability in either sense can coexist with lifespan malleability and context-

23

489

dependence 38,64 . As with all retrospective question-based assessments, one must retain a degree of

490

skepticism concerning people’s ability to accurately self-report their traits. That we find consistent

491

and meaningful relationships despite these limitations of self-reported traits suggests the existence

492

of an underlying measurable signal.

Conclusions

494

Our findings support the idea that ‘science is a human enterprise, and understanding the develop-

495

ment of scientific knowledge depends on an account of the thought processes of humans’ 65 . Our

496

research provides evidence for a pluralistic view of what constitutes an explanation and what makes

497

explanations satisfying, persuasive, and ultimately motivating for the community of scientists 66 .

498

Variation in cognitive traits predicts variation in perspectives on controversial topics, and positions

499

on controversies influence the likelihood that people cite similar literatures and ultimately write

500

similar articles. This also means that divisions in perspective run deep and pose a fundamental

501

challenge for interdisciplinary research between communities with distinct epistemic commitments,

502

and composed of individuals with different cognitive profiles.

505

Materials

517

Themes

518

We generated 16 themes that we thought would provoke disagreement among psychologists. We

519

piloted these on psychology graduate students mostly from the psychology department at UW-

520

Madison, to ensure that the descriptors were comprehensible and that both extremes were endorsed

521

by at least some respondents. As this was the case, we consider these themes controversial. The

522

themes and anchor labels are shown in Table S2.

523

Cognitive traits

524

The full questionnaires for the scales described here are provided at https://osf.io/zyec9/. Phrases

525

in italics below map onto labels in Fig. 3c.

526

Two scales probed differences in what are sometimes called “cognitive styles”. The Verbalizer-

527

Visualizer Quotient (VVQ) 44 comprised two subscales measuring people’s verbal orientation (e.g.,

528

‘I prefer to read instructions about how to do something rather than have someone show me.’) or

529

a visual orientation (e.g., ‘I find illustrations or diagrams help me when I’m reading.’). Of note,

530

the correlation between these two orientations is slightly positive (r =.11). We also included two

531

subscales of the Object-Spatial Imagery and Verbal Questionnaire (OSIVQ) 29 designed to probe

532

vividnes of object imagery (‘I can close my eyes and easily picture a scene that I have experienced.’)

533

and spatial imagery (‘I can easily imagine and mentally rotate three-dimensional geometric figures.’).

534

The visual orientation from the VVIQ is moderately correlated with both object imagery (r =.36)

535

and spatial imagery (r =.40).

536

The next two scales focused on differences in preferred information processing. Need for Cogni-

537

tion 67 measures one’s disposition to engage in effortful cognition (‘I like to have the responsibility of

538

handling a situation that requires a lot of thinking’). Tolerance of ambiguity 39 taps into a person’s

539

tendency to seek out and enjoy tasks that are poorly structured or lacking in clear cues (‘A problem

540

has little attraction for me if I don’t think it has a solution. [reversed]’).

25

541

We also took advantage of the finding that abbreviated scales can be used to simultaneously

542

measure multiple constructed with sufficiently high construct validity 68 . Cognitive structure and

543

Deliberation reflect methodicalness (‘I plan my life logically’, ‘I like to act on a whim [reversed]’).

544

Aesthetics and Breadth of interest relate to intellectual curiosity (‘I love to hear about other countries

545

and cultures’, ‘I read a large variety of books’). Dominance is a facet of extroversion about leading

546

or influencing others (‘I like being the authority who has everyone’s attention’, ‘I find it easy to

547

manipulate others’).

548

Finally, we included three single-item scales probed three aspects of self-rated skills 69 : partic-

549

ipants were asked to rate their creative, analytic and practical abilities from very weak to very

550

strong.

Procedure

552

Participants were first presented with an information screen explaining the general scope of the

553

project followed by a consent form. After providing informed consent to begin the study, participants

554

indicated their stances on the 16 themes, presented in a random order. Participants responded

555

using a slider with 0% indicating complete agreement with the label presented on the left and 100%

556

complete agreement with the label on the right. The labels anchoring the left and right sides of the

557

scale were randomized and re-aligned during analysis so that the 0% label corresponds to the left

558

label in Table S2.

559

Participants then completed the cognitive trait surveys. These were grouped into blocks as

560

reflected by the separate paragraphs above. The order of blocks was randomized. All responses

561

were on 5-point Likert scales (from ‘Strongly Disagree’ to ‘Strongly Agree’) except for the three

562

self-reported ability scales which were given as percentages from low to high ability.

563

Participants then provided biographic information (age, gender, rank) and free-text responses

564

indicating up to 5 topics that they researched (e.g., episodic memory, categorization, language,

565

sleep).

566

They were then given a list of broad areas of psychology (e.g., social psychology, cognitive

567

psychology), and clicked on check boxes to select whichever they worked in. An ‘other’ check box

568

allowed further open-text responses if their research areas were not given. Similarly, they clicked on

26

569

check boxes to select whichever methods they used in research (as opposed to practice). Again, an

570

‘other’ check box allowed for further free-text responses if their preferred methods were not given.

571

In the final part of the survey, requiring an extra stage of consent, we explained that we wanted to

572

understand how responses to the survey mapped onto people’s published work. If participants con-

573

sented, we would use their provided email addresses to create a link between their survey responses

574

and the bibliometric models. We explained that once this link was made, the email addresses would

575

be discarded, so any mapping between survey responses and vectors in the bibliometric models would

576

be anonymous.

577

Bibliometric models

578

To assess the distance of authors in terms of their linguistic patterns (i.e., what words they use;

579

the semantic model), reference patterns (i.e., what articles they cite in their articles; the citation

580

model), and co-authorship patterns (i.e., what group of people they write their articles with; the

581

co-authorship model), we embedded individual authors into three geometric vector spaces with

582

128 dimensions and calculated their distance in those spaces. To do this, we first collected the

583

bibliographic data from the Web of Science (WoS) database. We only used articles classified as

584

being written in English and published in journals that were classified as psychology journals in the

585

WoS database (i.e., the journal’s subject had the word “Psychology” in the WoS database). For the

586

semantic model, we also linked the articles in the WoS database to the Microsoft Academic Graph

587

(MAG) database via Digital Object Identifier to retrieve additional titles and abstracts missing from

588

the WoS database (note that the MAG database was not used for the other two bibliometric models).

589

Using the bibliographic data, we embedded the articles into three different vector spaces. For the

590

semantic model, we embedded 733,133 articles that had valid titles and abstracts in the database

591

by running the Doc2vec 47 algorithm implemented in the Python library Gensim 70 on the found

592

titles and abstracts. Doc2vec and related unsupervised machine learning models have transformed

593

modern natural language processing. These models “discover” semantics from linguistic context

594

and validate the distribution hypothesis that words occurring in the same contexts tend to have

595

similar meanings by performing at human-level on analogy tests 47,71 , question answering 72,73 and

596

a wide range of language understanding tasks. It has also been demonstrated that embedding texts

27

597

produced by persons in given times and places can replicate surveyed associations among people

598

from those same times and places 74–77 . Here we operationalized the “intellectual” distance between

599

papers as the cosine similarity between paper titles+abstracts. This approach produces estimates of

600

greater semantic similarity than bibliometric approaches for assessing the co-citation of articles or

601

journals 78 while not assuming that the compared works frame themselves with respect to the same

602

prior work.

603

For the citation model and the co-authorship models, we first constructed a citation network and

604

a co-authorship network using the data in WoS database, respectively. For the citation network, the

605

citation between the articles was treated as directed, unweighted edges (total number of citations:

606

16,495,908), and the articles were treated as nodes (total number of articles: 1,190,495). Only articles

607

that had valid citation data in the database were used to construct the citation network. For the

608

co-authorship network, we constructed a network where the articles were treated as nodes (total

609

number of articles: 511,508), and the nodes were connected with an undirected edge if they shared

610

at least one author (total number of co-author connections: 9,228,646). The edges were weighted

611

by the number of authors the articles shared. Additionally, to account for the possibility that

612

first or last authors contribute more towards a paper’s framing, we up-weighted edges connected

613

by first or last authors by a factor of 4. The articles were included in the network if the first

614

name, the last name, and the organization of the author(s) were in the database. The articles

615

were considered to be written by the same author if and only if the first name, the last name, and

616

the organization of the authors were matched between the articles. After building the networks,

617

we ran the Node2vec algorithm 48 (algorithm code available at https://github.com/eliorc/node2vec)

618

on the network to embed the articles into each vector space. Network embedding models have

619

revolutionized network prediction and description, just as text embedding models have transformed

620

natural language processing 79 . Additional details on the model building are provided i Appendix 2.

621

After embedding the articles into the vector spaces, we calculated the average of vectors cor-

622

responding to the articles that each survey participant published, yielding vectors for each author.

623

To acquire the articles that each survey participant wrote, we first queried the email address to the

624

WoS database (note that this step was done in the entire WoS database we had access to, not just

625

the psychology journals) and collected all first name, last name, and organization tuples, e.g., Jane,

28

626

Doe, XXX University, that matched the email address. Then, we collected all articles that matched

627

the set in the WoS database and considered these articles to be authored by the survey partici-

628

pant. This process was only done for participants that agreed to have their data cross-referenced

629

to the bibliographic database and provided their email addresses. In the semantic model, we were

630

able to link 6,637 survey participants to at least one valid article (mean=11.91 articles per par-

631

ticipant, SD=18.42). In the citation model and co-authorship model, we found 6,779 participants

632

(mean=13.67 articles per participant, SD=22.60) and 5,708 participants (mean=11.42 articles per

633

participant, SD=15.61) that had at least one article represented in the model, respectively. Finally,

634

we used the cosine similarity between the author vector pairs to calculate the similarity between the

635

authors in each model, which was used in the statistical analysis (see below).

Regression with multi-member random effects

637

As authors appeared multiple times in the analysis of similarities across high-dimensional spaces,

638

we required a random effect for each author in the relevant regression models. Each row in the

639

data represents the similarity between a pair of authors (Author A and Author B), but as their

640

ordering does not matter, it would be inappropriate to include one random intercept for Author A

641

and another for Author B (a given participant could appear in the Author A column on one occasion

642

and in the Author B column on another occasion). Multimembership random effects (with R package

643

Bayesian models of responses to controversial themes

848

To model the distributions of responses shown in Fig. 1, we developed custom Bayesian models to

849

account for various idiosyncratic features of the data:

850

• The response format was a percentage scale, so responses map onto the range [0, 1]. Beta

851

regressions are apt for modeling data in (0, 1) so they are an obvious choice here, but as they

852

cannot model 0s or 1s, this necessitates additional model components (‘inflation’ components)

853

for responses at either extreme end of the scale.

854

• Additionally, a number of themes showed a spike at exactly 50%, which represents a distinctive

855

response strategy: Participants had to move the slider to progress to the next item in the

856

survey, so if they wanted to answer exactly 50% rather than some number near 50% (perhaps

857

to avoid making a choice, no matter how trivial, for either left- or right-wards movement),

858

this necessitated moving the slider off the mid-point where it was initially, then moving it

859

back to 50%. We thus included an additional inflation component for responses exactly at the

860

mid-point.

861

• Many of the themes showed a bimodal response pattern, so all responses that were not one of

862

the unique strategies above (i.e., everything not at 0, 0.5 or 1) were handled with a mixture

863

model. This estimated parameters for two Beta distributions, each representing one of two

864

latent groups in the data.

865

Thus, each theme is modeled as a combination of inflation components (0, 1 or 0.5, highlighted

866

with lighter blue in Fig. 1 and mixture components (everything else, shown in darker blue in Fig.

867

1). The Stan code for these custom models is available at https://osf.io/zyec9/.

Additional details on the bibliometric models

894

To create the input for the semantics model, we first concatenated the title and the abstract of each

895

article. Then, we used spaCy (https://spacy.io/) library to tokenize the words in each article. Words
52

896

that were classified as punctuation or number by the tokenizer were thrown out. We also removed

897

the stop words included in the spaCy model. Finally, a bigram detector implemented in Gensim 70

898

package (the phrases module) was trained on the whole corpus and was applied to the corpus. We

899

used the distributed memory training algorithm for the Doc2vec 47 algorithm. All hyperparameters

900

were identical to the default value in the Gensim package, except that we used 128 dimensions for the

901

vector space (this was to match the citation and the co-authorship models). For the citation and co-

902

authorship networks, we removed the self-loops in the network. We used the Node2vec 48 algorithm

903

implemented in https://github.com/eliorc/node2vec with all hyperparameters set as default in the

904

implementation.
