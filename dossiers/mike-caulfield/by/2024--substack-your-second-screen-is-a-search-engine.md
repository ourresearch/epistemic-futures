# Your Second Screen Is a Search Engine

*An example from two phases of The Office shows how mobile phones changed TV consumption -- and brought the overlay to television*

## Full text

People often talk about second screen experiences with television in terms of content production — commenting, interacting with communities. But one of the biggest uses of the second screen is searching for information about a show while one is watching it. 

I came across a good example recently showing how smartphones had changed the way we consume TV. As you likely know, the show The Office had a run in the mid-to-late aughts, ending in May of 2013. It then got a second life on Netflix, and for reasons I don’t fully understand, experienced an explosion of popularity among a much younger generation starting sometime in 2017.1

You can see this trajectory in the Google Trend for the topic here, the cyclical seasonal searches when it was broadcast television followed by the steady hum of searches during the streaming period.

[](<https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!4zYp!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fffc61a89-aefb-496e-ad1f-725f229e12cb_742x450.png>)Relative search volume from Google Trends for topic The Office. The pattern is double peaked, with one peak in the mid-aughts and one in the late 2010s.

We can then take one of the most popular TV searches out there — searching for the cast of the show to answer that nagging question of “where do I know this guy from exactly?” And we can see it's a pretty significant search in the revival but not in the original. Here is the topic against searches on _office cast_ :

[](<https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!tmo5!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fd813f632-7bbd-4809-8553-befec7af2107_743x451.png>)Relative search volume for The Office and office cast. The search volume for office cast is proportionally significant in the revival period but not the original broadcasting period.

I know you may not think that is a significant amount — but the dynamics of both search and linguistic usage are such that a targeted query that forms a tenth of usage of a general trend is gigantic. And that’s what we see in the second phase, though not the first:

[](<https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!G1gl!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F25175537-4b33-495f-942e-688328d6678e_359x188.png>)Broadcast period stats, with cast searches forming 1% to 2% of the larger topical figure.

[](<https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!td7A!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F31399b2f-ac1a-4108-aff4-1cea4e7a6cfe_360x188.png>)Streaming revival stats, with cast searches forming about 8% of the larger topical figure, and four to five times as many searches as in the original period.

Why did this shift? My thought is cell phones (as well as watching shows on laptops) changed things. Suddenly it was easy to throw a quick search into one’s phone and figure out if why you were sure the last time you saw that actor she had a British accent. Maybe that’s right, maybe it’s wrong. But it’s [another instance of the overlay](<https://mikecaulfield.substack.com/p/google-search-is-an-overlay>), where we are searching _just-in-time_ and _right-in-place_.

1

To be clear, I think The Office is a good show — I just don’t know what suddenly made it popular on Netflix years after the last season was posted there.
