# Verified: How to Think Straight, Get Duped Less, and Make Better Decisions about What to Believe Online

## Publisher summary

ABOUT THIS BOOK
An indispensable guide for telling fact from fiction on the internet—often in less than 30 seconds.
The internet brings information to our fingertips almost instantly. The result is that we often jump to thinking too fast, without taking a few moments to verify the source
before
engaging with a claim or viral piece of media. Information literacy expert Mike Caulfield and educational researcher Sam Wineburg are here to enable us to take a moment for due diligence with this informative, approachable guide to the internet. With this illustrated tool kit, you will learn to identify red flags, get quick context, and make better use of common websites like Google and Wikipedia that can help and hinder in equal measure.
This how-to guide will teach you how to use the web to verify the web, quickly and efficiently, including how to
•     Verify news stories and other events in as little as thirty seconds (
seriously
)
•     Determine if the article you’re citing is by a reputable scholar or a quack
•     Detect the slippery tactics scammers use to make their sites look credible
•     Decide in a minute if that shocking video is truly shocking
•     Deduce who’s behind a site—even when its ownership is cleverly disguised
•     Uncover if that feature story is actually a piece planted by a foreign government
•     Use Wikipedia wisely to gain a foothold on new topics and leads for digging deeper
And so much more. Building on techniques like SIFT and lateral reading,
Verified
will help students and anyone else looking to get a handle on the internet’s endless flood of information through quick, practical, and accessible steps.
AUTHOR BIOGRAPHY
Mike Caulfield
is a research scientist at the University of Washington’s Center for an Informed Public, where he studies the spread of online rumors and misinformation. Creator of the SIFT methodology, he has taught thousands of teachers and students how to verify claims and sources through his workshops.
Sam Wineburg
is the Margaret Jacks Professor of Education, Emeritus, at Stanford University, and the founder of the Stanford History Education Group, whose state-of-the-art curriculum on digital literacy has been distributed freely to schools all over the world. He is the author of
Why Learn History (When It’s Already on Your Phone)
, also published by the University of Chicago Press.

TABLE OF CONTENTS
Introduction
DOI: 10.7208/chicago/9780226829845.003.0001
[
SIFT;verify;lateral reading;research-based
]
In a digital world, traditional ideas about critical thinking are badly in need of an update. Digital texts play by different rules from the printed texts we’ve been given since grade school. This Introduction sets the stage for what readers will glean from this book. They will learn how to quickly verify news stories and other events, often in as little as 30 seconds; how to determine if an article is written by a reputable scholar or a quack, how to detect the slippery tactics scammers use to make their sites look credible; how to deduce who’s behind a site, even when its ownership is cleverly disguised; how to uncover if a feature story is actually a puff piece planted by a foreign government,and so much more. Building on research-based techniques like SIFT and lateral reading, this book will help students and practically anyone else looking to get a handle on the internet's endless flood of information.
1. Get Quick Context: It Can Take as Little as Thirty Seconds—Seriously!
DOI: 10.7208/chicago/9780226829845.003.0002
[
SIFT;Investigate the Source;Find Better Coverage;Trace Claims
]
Chapter one introduces readers to the SIFT methodology, a simple acronym that stands for Stop, Investigate the Source, Find better coverage, and Trace claims, quotes and media to their original context. Vivid examples from social media demonstrate how these four steps cut through hype and allow searchers to locate reliable information–often in less than a minute.
2. Cheap Signals: Or, How Not to Get Duped
DOI: 10.7208/chicago/9780226829845.003.0003
[
cheap signals;dot.org;dot.com
]
Chapter two exposes the “cheap signals” used to give websites a patina of credibility. It debunks common beliefs about what makes a website credible. For example, the dot.org domain does not mean an organization is a nonprofit or has passed some special vetting process. Like dot.com, dot.org is an open domain; anyone with $15 and 15 minutes can register adot.org. Other features commonly listed on web credibility checklists—the recency of updates, the absence of ads, dispassionate language, lists of scientific references, a physical address, etc.—are featuresludicrously easy to game. Dwelling on a website without first asking who’s behind it plays into the hands of scammers, lobbyists and anyone else who works to hijack our attention and deceive us.
3. Google: The Bestie You Thought You Knew
DOI: 10.7208/chicago/9780226829845.003.0004
[
Google;snippets;SERP;neutral language;key words;search;tell
]
Google is ubiquitousas the home page on many computers. But how well do you know how to use this tool? This chapter explains how to quickly read Google's snippets (the short lines of text that accompanyeach result) by "mining" results on the Search Engine Results Page (SERP). It recommends ways to "think like your search engine" by crafting unique key words and phrasing queries in a neutral manner to avoid givingthe search engine a"tell." Google detects the slightest tell and produces what it thinks you want to hear. Phrasing a search in a neutral way provides a balanced picture on issues with multiple sides.
4. Lateral Reading: Using the Web to Read the Web
DOI: 10.7208/chicago/9780226829845.003.0005
[
lateral reading;click restraint;vertical reading
]
This chapter focuses on using the internet to read the internet by employing the technique of lateral reading. Unlikevertical reading, in which the reader examines a text from topto bottom, lateral readers openup new tabs before dwelling on an unfamiliar website. "Is this even what I think it is," is the first questionsavvy searchers ask. "It looks like it comes from a major medical association, but am I sure?" The savvy searcher puts thename of the organization in a new browser tab, and usesthe power of the internet to vet the original source. This chapter introduces the term "click restraint," the alternative to "promiscuous clicking," which enjoins the searcher to survey the full set of results, not just the first three, before making a first click.This first click is often destiny. It should be a wise one.
5. Reading the Room: Benefiting from Expertise When You Have Only a Bit Yourself
DOI: 10.7208/chicago/9780226829845.003.0006
[
reading the room;consensus;fringe position;gullibility;knee-jerk skepticism
]
We often search topics for which we lack expertise.But if we lack requisite background knowledge, how do we decide who and what to trust? This chapter explores how to search for and determine if there is consensus on an issue.The chapter provides tools for "reading the room"on topics for which multiple positions abound. Does a certainopinion represent a broadly held view by a majority of scholars, or is it a fringe position that refuses to engage with the fullbody of scholarly evidence? The chapter introduces the term "trust compression," in which knee-jerk skepticism makes it hard to distinguish between an established medical site like the Mayo Clinic and a dubious one like Dakota Bob's All Natural Health Supplements. Gullibility is often defined as believing everything. On the internet, gullibility takes the form of believing nothing.
6. Show Me the Evidence: Why Scholarly Sources Are Better than Promotional Materials, Newsletters, and Random Tweets
DOI: 10.7208/chicago/9780226829845.003.0007
[
peer review;Google Scholar;junk scholarship
]
Peer reviewis seen as the gold standard for assessing research claims. This chapter explains in plain language how peer review operates and why it is the first step in the line of defense against junk scholarship. Albeit an important safeguard, peer review is not perfect. With over 5 million academic articles published annually, weak studies slip through the cracks. This chapter addresses the importance of "reading the room" on a scholarly issue by starting with reviews of the literature, not single studies, that provide a broad overview of a given field or topic. The chapter includestips for using Google Scholar as a quick check for judging the reputation of a particular scholar or piece of research.
7. Wikipedia: Not What Your Middle School Teacher Told You
DOI: 10.7208/chicago/9780226829845.003.0008
[
Wikipedia;checking sources;investigating claims
]
For years educators have cast a suspicious eye toward Wikipedia.During the online encyclopedia's early years the suspicion was justified. But Wikipedia of 2023 is not your grade school teacher'sWikipedia. This chapter examines widely-held--but incorrect--beliefs about the site, such as "anyone can change it" (many articles are "locked" and editable only by high-level Wikipedians) or "it's filled with mistakes" (a study by the prestigious journalNatureshowed that Wikipedia compared favorably to the for-profitBritannica). Wikipedia, for those who know how to use it effectively, is an indispensabletool for validating unfamiliar organizations and websites, investigatingcontentious claims, and checking the credibility of many kinds of sources.
8. Video Games: The Dirty Tricks of Deceptive Video
DOI: 10.7208/chicago/9780226829845.003.0009
[
video;false context;false framing;cropped video;viral video;false dating
]
Bad actors and propagandists play games with video on the internet. They crop and edit clips, creatingfalse contexts that spin a video in directions often opposite to their meaning in a broader context. False framing and false dating are used to warp interpretations of real events, creating scandals out of relatively ordinary occurrences. The tighter a video is cropped, the more one should ask questions about what happened before and what happened later.
9. Stealth Advertising: When Ads Masquerade as News
DOI: 10.7208/chicago/9780226829845.003.0010
[
stealth advertising;sponsored content;weasel words
]
This chapter covers the move to stealth advertising, otherwise known as "sponsored content." Stealth advertising has become a growth industry for major news outlets. Publications like the New York Times, the BBC, the Atlantic and many othersnow have in-house ad agencies that produce ads that masquerade as news stories. A set of weasel words enhances the ruse; in addition to 'sponsored content', these stories carry ambiguouslabelssuch as "in cooperation with," "presented with," "brought to you by," and sometimes just "with." Research shows that people fall for these deceptions. In one study, where a Bank of America icon was overshadowed by the New York Times masthead, a prominent headline, and an attractive color picture, only 1 in ten adults identified the item as an ad. With Russia and China leading the way, foreign governments spend millions to place their stories in news publications using the similar tactics. The chapter ends with tips for identifying stealth ads, whether byfossil fuel companiesor foreign governments.
10. Once More with Feeling: Using Your Emotions to Find the Truth
DOI: 10.7208/chicago/9780226829845.003.0011
[
emotions;overwhelmed;surprise
]
This chapter argues that emotions, rather than being a hindrance to fact checking, can be harnessed as tools to evaluateonline information.Paying attention to what surprises you--what you find compelling--can help you realize the assumptions you made once you attempt to gain a broader context for the claim you're investigating. Feeling overwhelmed during the process of context-building may be an indication that you need to rethink your strategy and start anew.
11. Conclusion: Critical Ignoring
DOI: 10.7208/chicago/9780226829845.003.0012
[
critical ignoring;preserving attention;critical thinking
]
The conclusion emphasizesthe concept of critical ignoring, a necessary counterpart to critical thinking. The first act of critical thinking is to decide what's worth critically thinking about.Critical thinking consumes attention, and in an attention economy, that is our most precious resource. Learning to make strategic decisions about what to ignore allows us to preserve attention so that we can focus it onsources that truly merit our time and energy.
Postscript: Large Language Models, ChatGPT, and the Future of Verification
DOI: 10.7208/chicago/9780226829845.003.0013
[
Large Language Models;ChatGPT;cheap signals;Artificial Intelligence
]
Large language models have now made it possible to create deceptive content and post it on the internet in an instant for next to nothing. Fraudulent "news" sources, generated by AI, can produce content with impeccable grammar, perfect spelling, and finely crafted sentences that exceed the writing ability of many college graduates. How do we protect ourselves in a world when we don't know who orwhatproduced the prose we're reading? We need to employ the skills we spell out in the previous chapters, and use the full internet to check the internet--especiallywhen the prose on the other side is crafted by a convincing machine.
