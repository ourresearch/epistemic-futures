# Predictive Efficiency

## Full text

From Farrington & Tarling’s _[Prediction in Criminology](<http://books.google.com/books?id=JBC-_UgQ7E4C&pg=PA22&lpg=PA22&dq=%22are+percentages+predictions?%22&source=bl&ots=2hmFmga0aS&sig=9rU3n-DcJtopXDgjiq7_f30n734&hl=en&sa=X&ei=eyEgT5zsMejj0QGVyNkG&ved=0CB4Q6AEwAA#v=onepage&q=%22are%20percentages%20predictions%3F%22&f=false>)_ , a new term: _predictive efficiency_. The way to think about it is this — suppose I say that a college education predicts low incidence of being convicted of a violent crime, and at the end of the day I’m right — over the course of a year, 97.5% of our college grads are not convicted.

In the absence of a base rate, that doesn’t really tell us anything. It can be a good predictor in that it does predict at high rates of certainty, but it’s _inefficient_ compared to alternative predictors.
