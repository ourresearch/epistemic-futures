# A bit more on Wikity 2, the Obsidian-based Claude-powered LLM I built

*There's a lesson for educators here too: We're going to get beyond the chatbot very soon.*

## Full text

The following video is probably most interesting to people who want to set something like this up themselves, but I do hope that if you’re an educator you watch it, because there’s an important lesson here for educators too.

Our AI discourse is currently stuck in the realm of chatbots. And I don’t think that back and forth element of what we do with AI will ever _fully_ go away. I think, for example, that it will continue to be an element of how people search, partially because that back and forth request and response element has been part of computer search since before I was an undergrad playing with DIALOG and other information systems in the library. 

But it’s not the only model. One user mode I explore above, using a system I built over the course of a weekend while putting off doing my taxes, is one where our interaction is as an editor leaving notes on the work of an LLM. I know there are products out there that do stuff like this in a contained way, but this is a system that plugged into Claude Code on the backend has near infinite extensibility that far exceeds all the junky little “talk to your PDF” stuff you’ve seen around.

Note the changes here — you don’t engage in search directly. You comment on the document that you need that evidence and the document becomes the context; you see the search “result” as a document edit. Again I know there are wiki1 out there that do things like this. That’s part of the point as well, I suppose. But look at how easy it is to build functions here as just wiki pages. Style guides to conform to. New functions. And the stuff I’m showing you here is the bread-and-butter of the system, not even the flashier bits.

I think this is also important when we think about educational research on things like “cognitive offloading”. I’m sitting here right now typing this after having done the above screencast a half hour ago. If you asked me right now what the revenue of _Body Double_ was, I would tell you, off the top of my head, that it earned 8 million and change on an estimated 10 million dollar budget, which was a 0.9 return. As I type that, I realize it must have been over 8.5 million to get that 0.9 return figure. That’s exceptional recall, I think.

It’s also the sort of recall that is _not supposed to happen_ with LLMs. Facts are supposed to go in the eye and out of the head. But all that research that supposedly tells us what AI does to the brain is telling us what a certain _type_ of AI paired with a certain _use pattern_ in a certain _educational environment_ does. And what they are testing in most cases is the most unimaginative version of all of that. 

What has always interested me is the question _what are the better models_? That was true in my work with the concept of digital gardens, that was true with SIFT, and that is true with this. So putting students in front of the sweet salty fat of the chatbot-as-friend with no prompt modifications or instruction at all didn’t produce educational nirvana? Shocking. In other words, it didn’t work any more than giving them a book to read and saying “problem solved” did. Trivially true and fundamentally uninteresting. 

What are the other models? Ways to control the back and forth, absolutely, and make it more productive. Ways to interact with the chatbot, sure. But also ways like this, where the back and forth is not a chat. Where you play the role of an editor, interacting through comments. What possibilities does that open? How much of our current discourse becomes irrelevant if we make that switch?

Do you think an editor interacting through comments learns nothing? Some frustrated reporters might think so occasionally, but in general the answer is that editors deepen their topical knowledge over time, edit by edit. In a world where we teach students the language, process, and values of editing we could perhaps also be teaching them productive ways to interact with the technologies of tomorrow. Not by focusing on the technology, but by focusing on the craft. 

Worth exploring, right? I hope you think so too, and I’ll continue to build this out.
