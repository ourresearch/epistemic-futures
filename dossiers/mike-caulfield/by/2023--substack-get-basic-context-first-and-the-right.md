# Get basic context first and the right questions will come to you

*The reason why SIFT puts the "doing up front" and the questions later.*

## Full text

_Summary: We’re good at recognizing what’s important when we see it, so get to the “seeing it” part quickly._

I gave a keynote to the ID:EALS symposium at Purdue recently and it made me realize how much material has built up in my sojourn from blogging. A sojourn so long I am now using words like “sojourn”. It’s bad, folks. 

Gearing back up is going to be slow, so let’s start with something easy that I don’t know I ever pulled into a blog post: a lot of our success with SIFT and lateral reading as an information strategy comes down to this — do stuff before you think. By _do stuff_ I mean get some context, but I really want to put the emphasis on _do_.

So what do I mean by that? When I came into this field the dominant approach to information literacy was to plunge a student who has encountered something new immediately into thinking. Specifically, _looking at the item in front of them_ and asking themselves questions about it. Here, for example, was CRAAP:

[](<https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!NQd0!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F674d4050-52e4-4cb0-a80b-48674caffbd4_652x867.png>)

There’s a couple ideas embedded in this approach, but one of the biggest is that _students don’t know what questions to ask a document_. Or that they don’t know all the questions to ask, or that they aren’t thorough about the questions they ask. 

In practice, however, the problem with evaluating a resource isn’t whether you ask all the questions of it. The main problem is knowing which question will be important. Something has a typo on it, but it’s also the newspaper of record in Illinois, founded in the 1800s (apologies if that’s not when the Tribune was founded I’m trying to get my blogging pace back, can’t stop won’t stop). And the weird thing is to find out which question you should be asking you need to know the answer to the question, which kind of renders the list of questions a bit meaningless.

If this doesn’t make sense, consider this. You’re looking at a website that suggests baking soda injections are a good replacement for chemotherapy. That’s obviously bad, but let’s assume you just want to double-check and see if the source is better than you’d expect.

You can go through all the questions of CRAAP: was it published recently. Does the author have books out? Do the links work. Is the author associated with a company or a university? Are there biases in the language. How’s the spelling?

In the olden days we honestly used to make students do this, I’m not joking. But the thing is your mind doesn’t work well this way. Want to make the worst decision of your life? Collect a hundred datapoints and try to weight them.

So here’s the SIFT approach (and lateral reading approach more generally) to sourcing Do the doing. Look the guy up on Wikipedia. When you do that you find something interesting:

[](<https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!eSLY!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe9ac6159-3666-456f-9c7d-1a45115ebfc9_1246x406.png>)

Anything jumping out here? For me it’s the two manslaughter convictions. 

You’re thinking right now, ah, but it’s not that simple. And I’m going to tell you — for a large class of things people get fooled by online it’s pretty close to that simple. Maybe the person wasn’t convicted of killing people. But you’ll find their main claim to fame is as a conspiracy theorist, or that they have questioned whether six million Jews died in the Holocaust. More boringly, it may turn out that great defense of up-zoning you were about to send your colleague is from the state builder’s association, or that compelling case for IBM software you were about to send your boss as an “article” is actually a sponsored post — from IBM. This class of things may be fine, of course, but must be read with that context in mind.

The same thing is true for claims. Presented with a claim that “suitcases of ballots” were wheeled into a counting location you find an article that says they weren’t wheeled in, and they were security bins, not suitcases. Seems relevant, and notably you don’t need a question on a sheet asking you to check “Are the containers in the claim as described?” to tell you that. You get the context, and the relevant questions come to you.

I could go on. But here’s my point. In general, _you know what’s important when you see it_. And students do too. There’s skills to teach here, and some students do struggle. But when they read additional context that notes the “suitcases” were standard security bins, or that the promoter of a treatment was convicted of killing a patient — they can get that. But they have to _see_ it to get it. 

As humans we’re actually pretty good at picking out what’s important to us when we see it. Not perfect, but far better at that than correctly weighting the relevance 32 answers from 26 questions. So SIFT puts the doing first, asking the student or citizen to gather basic information while using some basic techniques to notice what’s important. More on the noticing part tomorrow. In the meantime — I’m back folks.
