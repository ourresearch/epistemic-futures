# The problem with Grammarly's "experts" is it doesn't use their expertise at all

*I'm in this photo and I don't like it*

## Full text

If you write something on digital information literacy in Grammarly and click the icon for the new AI-based “experts” feature, you get a list of “experts” ready to help you improve that essay. For that topic, you’ll find me in there:

[](<https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!LLKo!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F49c7ec15-ef9c-4ac5-8fff-ce3e10e06c52_378x663.jpeg>)caption...

Needless to say, I have nothing to do with this. I just heard they were doing this yesterday and typed in some stuff that used some SIFT moves and there I was. So let’s look at what robot me says about the writing!

[](<https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!rv1c!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F56674b28-9224-4e2b-9960-aacd0c4deff4_798x420.jpeg>)

So my advice is apparently “Add a punchy hook!” 

What?

Look, a lot of people are offended they would do this thing, that they are stealing names and trying to make money off of other people’s reputations. Maybe I’m abnormal, but I don’t care about that. I invented SIFT and it is so widely used and known that most people don’t even know it as “Mike Caulfield’s SIFT” anymore. It’s just “online information literacy” now. There are absolutely people out there using variations of SIFT to fuel projects and initiatives that make more in a week than I do in a year. Meanwhile I am working a fairly mundane job that right now is half running an academic technology team (currently doing cutting edge things with AI) and half running the team of people that install and support campus audiovisual equipment.1 I’m fine with that and prefer it actually. I’ve been famous before and it was mostly a pain in the ass. 

The thing that actually peeves me is not that they tried to make a Mike Caulfield expert, or that I don’t get a cut of that. The thing that peeves me is clearly this isn’t about my expertise at all. It’s pulling me up because of the topic, then handing out the standard Grammarly pablum about punching up sentences, then slapping my name on it to give it a false credibility.

If I actually _was_ looking at an early draft of your essay I would not be telling you to punch up your intro. I would be asking you some basic questions related to SIFT. What was your initial reaction to this claim you’re discussing? How did you first come across it, and what do you know about that initial source? What did other people say about it? Are there any interesting patterns of disagreement? For instance, do the disagreements on this issue among experts differ from the disagreements among politicians? 

I’d continue — let’s look at your citations. Are you citing intermediate sources? That can be good; summary sources are often better suited to our needs when we’re not an expert. But you still want to make sure the citations in the summary sources check out. And so on.

If someone built a real Mike Caulfield bot, I’d welcome it, honestly, as long as they were clear it was a simulation. I got into online information literacy not for the money, but because it seemed to me people were struggling with these skills and I wanted to help. Anything that advances people’s skills in this area is good and I don’t particularly care who gets the money or credit.

But this ridiculousness where they take my name and slap it on advice that is the modern equivalent of “You should begin your speech with a joke people love jokes” is horrendously wrong. It is not in fact “inspired by Mike Caulfield”; it is laundering mundane junk through deceptive citation. There is no earth on which Mike Caulfield or any of his simulacra will ever tell you to punch up your sentences before encouraging you to read more broadly and check your citations. Any person who truly practiced SIFT could tell you that.
