# Your Peak Never Looks Like a Peak

*LLMs are a half-decent hit single on the way down from stardom. Just don't call it a comeback.*

## Full text

Back in the 2010s, I read an autobiography of The Streets (aka Mike Skinner), a musical act out of the U.K. While not that big in the U.S., his music was pretty influential in Britain in the early 2000s, creating a distinctly British form of — well, it’s hard to explain exactly. It was hip-hop influenced but wasn’t hip hop, it was UK Garage but also wasn’t. By the end of it all he’d drawn on everything from ska to punk, and he resembled none of it. But I digress.

The first track he released, the 2001 “Has It Come to This?” just made me anxious, with its glitchy half-rap and off-beat loop. But in a hip-hop world driven by a U.S. version of urban, lower class life, Skinner’s vision of an approach and lyricism more grounded in a U.K. experience won him a fan base there — even if U.S. critics mercilessly panned it. His next single, off the album _Original Pirate Material_ (2002), did slightly _worse_ than his first hit, but introduced a retweaked style, less glitchy, less tight, more informal, even more grounded in a deeply British context. It hit 30 on the UK singles charts. His single “Weak Become Heroes”, hit 27, and showed how he could weave the commonplace into stories of belonging, alienation, and memory and loss. Again, the _critics_ hated it, especially the U.S. press, but he had fans and was locked into a vision.

By the time his album _A Grand Don’t Come for Free_ was released in 2004, he’d locked in his style and the world had caught up a bit with him. The first single off it smashed into the UK top 10, landing at number 4 — the sloppy, half bored, unapologetically British “Fit But You Know It”. It rotated into U.S. airplay too, ending up on alternative music stations and college radio. 

Things were on the upswing for Skinner, and the next single confirmed that. “Dry Your Eyes”, an awkward, spoken-word ballad showcased his vulnerability. It was sappy, sure, but in its sappiness it felt profoundly relatable, almost daring in its desire not adorn the song with any cleverness. Skinner’s delivery is so sincere as to be mesmerizing. The orchestration and the beat come together in a profound way, one soaring, the other pulling you down to earth. The cadence and flow of his voice fits the song, and the mood, and the moment like a glove. It washes over you like successive waves then is gone, leaving you — like the song’s narrator — to wonder what just happened.

It’s one of those pure gems of a song that comes around a few times every year, a song where all the stars align and make it much, much bigger than the sum of its parts. The British public shot it to number one on the charts, and gave his album a Mercury Prize nomination. Even the U.S. critics came along to praise it. 

And after that? 

After that there were other singles. Some were good, some less so. Some did well, others not so much. But none of them would ever be on the scale of “Dry Your Eyes”. Not even close.

This has been a long intro (and surprisingly, a lot of data) detailing the path of a star of significant but ultimately middling importance to the history of British music as a whole. But what I wanted to get to was this. I read his autobiography over a decade ago and one thing he said about that time stuck with me. He said no artist he has ever talked to thought their biggest hit single was going to be the peak of their career. In the moment going to the next level of success feels like momentum, not your ultimate high-water mark. And he was no different.

When “Dry Your Eyes” hit, he felt like this was the _beginning of something bigger_. This wasn’t going to be _the_ number one hit he had, but rather part of a _string_ of number ones. It’s just the way we are wired. After all, hadn’t he had a bit of a dip before? And what happened then? He went even higher. He persevered, he went to the top. 

The next single flops horribly, but the one after that at least lands in the top 40. In 2006 the initial single of his new album cracks the top 10 — he’s back on the upswing! Except that upswing will actually be the _last_ time he cracks the top 10, just as two years before was the last time he hit number one. He’ll fall to an embarrassing 157 on “The Escapist” in 2008 before swinging back up to 37 with his following single. Again, on the upswing! 

It would be his last Top 40 hit.

I wish I had the book in front of me right now, but I lent it long ago to someone. I may be completely mangling this, remembering it through my own lens at this point. But what I took away from it was that it takes a very very long time for people to realize their greatest achievement in a field is behind them. For a person that has had a big hit, the little reprieves and small surges of success on the way down feel like acceleration up. They’ve felt this climb before, and they know what comes next. Something bigger and better right?

We can’t help it, we’re human.

I think about this a lot in my own life. There’s no peak of my _life_ , of course. But for every run of achievements in a given domain or on a given project, the day I was at my peak is the day I was thinking this is the first of many achievements of this scale. For me, it’s always been a reminder to ask myself if I still love what I do or if I am chasing after some unattainable past glory. Sometimes the next level of the thing you are doing is right over the hill; other times it’s behind you. That can be fine, of course, but it can also be a sign that you need to move onto your next chapter.

Lately I’ve been thinking about this a lot when it comes to Silicon Valley. There was the invention of the internet and web — a pair of things on an amazing scale. Then there was the social web which isn’t an internet scale invention but has been massively influential. After that? There’s MOOCs (bust), Blockchain (mostly a bust), NFTs (a complete bust). 

Now there’s LLMs, which I think are in fact interesting, and have potential as a technology applied to a somewhat _narrow_ class of _specific_ problems — but will almost certainly have less impact than the social web. In fact, when people look back on the history of 2022-2024 forty years from now they are more likely to talk about it as the time of the emergence of cancer vaccines and anti-obesity drugs than ChatGPT. 

So why can’t we live in a world where people talk about LLMs in a reasonable way — “this is not as big as the internet, and not even as big as the advent of social media, but it’s going to significantly advance efforts in a number of limited domains.”

I know some people say capitalism and others say grift, and I’m not naive enough to think there isn’t a lot of that. But I increasingly think much of it is just human nature. Before you realize your last number one hit is behind you, your last Top 10 hit is behind you as well. And you’ll realize _that_ just as you touch the Top 100 for the last time.

There’s plenty of life to be lived, and plenty of cool things to accomplish. There’s a ton of ways to make people’s lives better. But we might be more successful at finding them if we didn’t think of everything as our way back to an internet-scale hit that happened once, and is not coming back in our lifetimes. There is not an internet-level innovation left to find on the internet. And honestly, that’s OK.
