# We could be building a better world with our students. Why are we disempowering them instead?

*And why did our constructionism impulse die when it was most needed?*

## Full text

There’s a mystery at the heart of our educational moment for me. At the moment when the progressive dream of constructionism in education was poised for victory… many educators decided to walk away.

If you don’t know what constructionism in education is, it’s an approach that engages students in exploring their world by making things, whether those things are physical or digital. Your students learn history by cataloging audio tape archives of a local historical figure. They learn chemistry by researching how to clean up a local lake, then doing it. They learn the dynamics of microeconomics by creating a board game mimicking them.

Constructionism always had rough sledding with educational experts, who rightly pointed out building a trebuchet was an extremely inefficient way to learn trig. You had to really fight for it in the academy, being stuck between many long time professors who viewed any form of active learning with suspicion and an administration looking for assessable skills. 

When I was working at a state college as the instructional designer, one of the best projects I worked on was with a sociology professor who engaged her class in community resource mapping. Her students went out into the community, compiled information methodically on the strengths of a community often thought of as one of the poorer sections of the county. The students were electric, motivated, vibrant. We in educational technology helped her come up with a way of mapping the resources that had physical locations on top of the newly released at that time Google Maps. 

The doubters will say, as with the trig trebuchet, this is a lousy way to learn a subject. And they are right. It does not have peak efficiency for students who need to be able to explain the details of social reproduction theory. But it turned out to be an excellent education in learning to have impact and in working with others. When we went begging the state for money under the claim that we were preparing all of our majors for the world of work, that was the project we demo’d to the trustees. And despite some grumbling from her peers that this was not replicable and not academically rigorous, more than a few of her colleagues benefitted from the good will this built with our community stakeholders, as it quite literally helped us secure the funds to keep them employed.

It’s true that constructionism didn’t always prepare students perfectly in the disciplines. But those students went out to job interviews with real projects they had worked on, concrete experience they could discuss, and I can tell you just from knowing some of the students she worked with after they graduated that for those students that experience was life-changing and that mattered a lot.

## The constructionism challenge

The hardest thing with constructionism was discipline-project fit. You needed to find a project that 

  * Allowed students to do something meaningful

  * With novice or near novice-level knowledge to start 

  * That exercised the correct disciplinary understandings

Part of the attraction of the [edupunk trend](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edupunk>) in education in the 2000s was that digital constructionism when combined with publishing via Wordpress presented opportunities to engage students in building real things for audiences in ways that did not require the superheroics of running a student community mapping project. You could translate poetry from Latin, run simulations of de-gerrymandered maps and publish them along with commentary, itemize and research all of a county’s historical markers on a public site. 

Suddenly, with a real path to impact, there were many relatively straightforward ways to make learning real, impactful, and demonstrable to a future employer. Outside of the job prospects of our students (which I believe should always be front and center, since they place their trust in us we are preparing them for a world of work) the students coming through these programs were learning an important lesson — see a problem and try to find your little piece of the solution. Not because you will be the one to solve it forever and a day, but because trying to engage with the broader world is profoundly human.

The movement fell apart, in my opinion, for two reasons. The first was the internet became a more dangerous place on which to show your whole self on. That was partly the trolls, partly right-wing activists looking for any student projects on which to set the dogs. There was also a more general (and probably related) shift, where the early millennials had been relatively open about being online, even excited. Many of the later millennials wanted nothing to do with that. Gen Z was similarly reticent. The death blow in my opinion, was COVID, where tech was permanently corralled into a delivery mechanism, the province of classes observed on video, not students creating videos on the hidden history of your town. It was necessary at the time but came to form everyone’s perspective of technology as _classroom capture_ and not something larger. 

People are still trying, but it’s rough out there. Edtech is now the Zoom of the class session. People are doing the lord’s work in places, but for most students publishing their thoughts on the internet is less the utopian vision of 2008 that the horror film of 2026. All of which is a shame, because more than ever college is fighting for the sort of relevancy and legitimacy that students doing community engaged work provides.

What we really needed was a new approach to this that didn’t depend on self-publication as the sole model.

## Enter agents

My AI and education work is mostly in AI and information literacy, and I do think I’m good at that. I enjoy it. But as a former proponent for digital constructionism in higher education I’m not certain that “AI literacy” of any sort is our biggest issue. 

The bigger issue is I think the opportunity we are missing. 

Students are anxious and resent AI which they believe is setting them up for a poor future in the workplace. A worthwhile education would help them see how they might fit into that future workplace and give them skills and understandings that would help them gain employment. Like it or not, that probably involves working with AI, at least a bit. 

The good thing is that AI, especially agentive AI, is incredibly well-suited to constructionism projects. One of the things I’ve been building recently is [a film recommender](<https://plot.fyi/>). And one of the reasons it’s a compelling project to me is the heart of the project is building a little classifier that tags film records according to their plot and character elements. 

[](<https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!wJGd!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F906fb857-f6bd-4f6a-be05-90b08d61dfcc_1176x463.png>)

You write a classifier, you see what it catches, you come up with an evaluation scheme. Each round helps you crystallize what distinctions matter to you. The process of building is also a process of clarifying your questions. 

You do need to know a bit about markdown formats and have an idea of what JSON is, but not that much. You are generally checking the top level of the project for errors and the effects of poor definitions or poor processing. That said, even at the bottom of the project in the data formats that the LLM maintains the meaning is completely legible:

Using LLMs to find and classify data, and building processes that check that data will absolutely be part of our students’ future work environment. It’s part of mine in my job as a director of academic and collaborative software, which is perhaps not surprising. But it’s also part of my boss’s work life, as she had to write a prompt that went through a project full of data and deduplicate and apply classifications to over 600 pieces of software we need to process. It’s part of a variety of other people’s work as well. 

Now, there’s lot’s of ways to work a classifier project into a college class on a subject, whether in the humanities or the social sciences. I’ll leave that to the reader’s imagination. Put a visualizer on top of it and you’ve got a pretty clean constructionism project. Scope it out as a multi-week task. Have your students write classifiers for genre, have them classify the modifiers used for women vs. men in a 1919 newspaper archive. Have them pull and classify the location and nature of extreme weather events over time. Build the classifier, audit the output, rethink and rewrite the classifier. Make sure the students do this as a group and debate/talk through each iteration, always using the project goal as the touchpoint for decisions. At the end have them reflect how they might explain what they learned on this about AI, classification, and your discipline to a future employer.

They don’t need to have Codex or Claude or whatever. You can even run this as a front of room exercise, putting in their definitions and then soliciting their feedback after each run.

This is just one example, but my point is this — when we work backwards to better understand how students will use this tech in the jobs they go to, it’s not hard to come up with classroom activities that truly prepare them.

## The wrong ethics

There’s a couple things I think we are getting wrong at this moment. The first is the idea that the main impact of education of agents will be in the _delivery_ of education. It’s possible, of course, and I’ve seen some decent applications of this tech in that direction. But I don’t believe this will be the transformational impact of this tech.

For higher education, I think the most interesting story is not the world inside the classroom, but the world we are graduating them into. 

It’s very evident that students are quite stressed about graduating into a world where AI is in the workplace and where it is not clear how they fit into that world. 

For all the talk about AI ethics in education I am unsure of the ethics of looking at students nervous about their future employment and celebrating their anger rather than modeling for them how they might find a place in this new world of work.

It seems to me that given the debt we put these students in, debt they take on to have a better chance in the workforce, that we might talk less about AI ethics, and more about the ethics of our educational responsibilities. 

We have educational models for this, and tools that make a good fit. Why not do something extraordinary?
