# Why patriotic England can’t see outside itself

## Full text

2.   [Commentary](https://www.politico.eu/section/commentary/)

Edward Bernays**,**the man behind the term “PR,” always said: The ideal way to bring an audience around to your side is to set up a landscape gently conducive to the result you’re after. The Remain campaign appears to have taken — knowingly or not — the mantra to heart. The U.K.’s upcoming referendum on its EU membership coincides with the Euro 2016. If people you like repeat the word “euro” enough times, so the PR theory goes, you might subliminally lean toward voting for Remain.

The football championship provides a warm, pro-European sheen to English screens and conversations.It is full of European players the English have come to see as their own: Fans watch West Ham’s Dimitri Payet play for France, or Arsenal’s Mezut Özil fight for Germany.Frenchman and ex-Arsenal favorite Thierry Henry and Italian former Chelsea player-manager Gianluca Vialli act as hosts and pundits on British TV. Behind them, through the studio windows, the Eiffel Tower lights up in the colors of every European nation’s flag.

> The football championship provides a warm, pro-European sheen to British people’s screens and conversations.

But the Euro 2016 has also dug up England’s deep-seated dislike and distrust of anything European. In the run-up to match that pitted England against Russia, British TV ran a short documentary about English striker Harry Kane. It opened with a quote from Shakespeare’s “Henry V,” a play whose patriotic England-versus-Europe violence kids are taught in school. The [English hooligans](https://www.politico.eu/blogs/the-linesman/2016/06/why-are-the-english-being-hooligans-again-uefa-euro-2016-violence-russia-clashes-marseille/) in France are following a century-old tradition of Euro-bashing.

The English grow up on stories about fighting off Europe in its various forms:the Pope, the Spanish Armada, Napoleon, Hitler. The European Union is just its latest incarnation. “Europe” and“the Continent” are often used as negative terms, ideas for the English define themselves against rather than aspire to, and that historically have threatened their independence. The Leave campaign have used this to their advantage, and framed their message as “taking back” independence from Brussels.

To counter England’s anti-European instinct the Remain camp has tried to play up the country’s internationalist traditions. Historically, Britain has been united with (at least someone in) Europe, and often tried to find solutions to European problems. “[Lead not Leave](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gPX9MLALjAE)” campaign ads, fronted by former Prime Minister Gordon Brown, argue the U.K. has always been an outward looking country, the most “internationally-minded of all,”and that its rightful role is at the forefront of the EU.

Sadly I’m not entirely sure today’s England — and the crucial English, rather than British, swing vote especially — is all that “outward looking” any more. For many years I worked in British television, making the sort of mass-market documentaries and reality shows that are a good indication of what the nation wants. When I began, in the early 2000s, there was an entire sub-industry devoted to making in-depth films about foreign countries. The “foreigners” were the main heroes, and the films were often shot in a foreign language and subtitled.

***

**Today, that sub-industry has close to disappeared.**People in the English TV business know that if you want to make shows about foreign countries they have to be narrated through the prism of an English person abroad, either a celebrity or, more commonly,loutish English drinking and shagging at a holiday resort. Many of these new shows are interested in the world only as it relates to the English, typically as something to define English-ness against.

> The great paradox is that as England has become utterly globalized … it has only grown more insulated.

In 2003, for example, the TV production company where I first worked as an assistant, October Films, made immersive Emmy and Bafta Award winning films such as “[The Last Peasants](https://www.theguardian.com/film/2003/jan/12/hayfilmfestival2005.hayfestival),” a three-year project about village life in Romania and illegal immigrants coming to London.A decade later they were best known for “Sun, Sea and A&E,” a show about Brits who end up in hospital while on summer holiday.

The great paradox is that as England has become utterly globalized, as it has become home to 4 million people from all over the world, it has only grown more insulated.Maybe this is not so surprising. In retrospect the old documentaries were products of a self-confident, at times slightly patronizing, afterglow-of-Empire-culture where the English thought it was their “civilizational” duty to observe the “natives” in faraway places. Today, as the English feel more helpless in the face of globalization, they don’t have the confidence to look outwards — or even around themselves,at the lives of immigrants in their own country.

This new insularity means the Remain campaign’s appeal to England’s “outward looking” heritage may have misread the public mood. Perhaps Remain needs to find a way to feed the English desire for insularity and independence, and show that the EU actually strengthens nationhood. As historian [Alan Milward](https://www.amazon.co.uk/European-Rescue-Nation-State/dp/0415216281?ie=UTF8&*Version*=1&*entries*=0) and other have long argued, the message is far from unjustified. For all the federalist yearnings of some EU founders, the union’s time has been spent strengthening national projects.

> Perhaps Remain needs to find a way to feed the English desire for insularity and independence, and show that the EU actually strengthens nationhood.

The French pursued the EU as a way to maintain their global status and domestic economy after World War II; the Germans as a way to rejoin the community of nations. For the countries emerging from the Soviet block post-1989, the EU was a way to guarantee their sovereignty and security. For today’s England, EU membership is a way to keep Britain united, and thereby strengthen England’s status and historical legacy. It ensures there’s no friction around our security Achille’s Heel in Northern Ireland and and overseas territory Gibraltar, and acts as a safety net in case the economy runs aground.

Most of all it’s a vehicle through which England and the rest of Britain can strengthen their nationhood(s), buffeted from the crueler gales of globalization. There is no old boy network of former colonies ready to embrace them. Outside the EU Britain will be dependent on the money and largesse of China, the U.S., Russia and the Gulf states. Leaving the EU won’t make Britain independent, Remain could argue, it will make it more dependent than ever.

The EU is a vehicle to channel and express nationalism not undermine it: just like the European championships.
