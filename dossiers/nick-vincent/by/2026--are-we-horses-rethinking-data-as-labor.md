# Are We Horses? Rethinking Data as Labor

## Full text

This paper asks a simple question: when we use digital platforms, are we more like horses that leave manure behind or like workers whose efforts help create value?Building on the idea of "data as labor," we suggest that everyday activities, such as scrolling, clicking, and solving reCAPTCHAs, can function as a thin form of labor for data-driven firms.We argue that the key issue is not who owns the data, but whether the terms on which platforms use it are fair.To make this case, we connect debates on household production, unconscionable contracts, and surveillance capitalism to current data practices.We then explore how tools such as portability and erasure rights, data unions, and data strikes might give users modest forms of bargaining power.Our aim is not to offer a final theory, but to invite a more careful discussion of recognition and fairness in the data economy.

## Setting the Stage: Rojas-Lozano v. Google

The questions, "Is data manure?"and "Are we horses?",moved from metaphor to legal claim in 2015, when Gabriela Rojas-Lozano brought a lawsuit against Google. 3n doing so, she challenged one of the most powerful assumptions of the digital economy: that ordinary users are passive consumers, not productive contributors.Her case asserted that users, through their interaction with reCAPTCHA, had effectively been transformed into unpaid workers.What seemed like a harmless security feature, verifying that one is not a robot by interpreting blurred text or identifying traffic lights, was in fact a data extraction system that used the unpaid labor of users to enhance corporate capabilities.

Originally developed at Carnegie Mellon University, CAPTCHA was presented as a clever way to improve digitization.It recycled difficult-to-read text from old books and archival fonts, using millions of unsuspecting users to decipher and thus digitize these texts.When Google acquired the technology, the task changed.Users are no longer helping preserve literary archives; they are training Google's algorithms.Each time a user identifies cross-walks, busses, or storefronts in fragmented images, they train commercial algorithms used for everything from Google Street View to selfdriving car navigation.

The lawsuit argued that this transformation was not benign.Rojas-Lozano contended that users were conscripted into digital labor without their knowledge or consent, all while being told they were simply accessing a free service.What they were actually doing, she argued, was contributing to an automated workforce, one whose economic value was measurable, yet whose contributors were invisible.

The court ruled in favor of Google.Their reasoning relied heavily on contractual for-malism.The judge stated, "Here, Plaintiff signed up for a Gmail account and agreed to Google's Terms of Service and Privacy Policy...This 'clickwrap' agreement reasonably communicated the terms." 4 The judge also noted, "Google's profit is not the only benefit the court considers in this balancing test, completing the prompt also entitles users to a free Gmail account.Additionally, users' transcriptions increase the utility of other free Google services such as Google Maps or Google Books." 5 The court's decision is instructive not only for what it affirms but also for what it fails to confront.First, it sidesteps a normative question: whether a person's productive activity, even when channeled through an interface labeled "free," might constitute labor that merits recognition.Second, it leans heavily on the fiction of free services as compensation, over-looking the deeper structure of the exchange.Yes, users get Gmail.But the true currency of the transaction is not goodwill: it is data which powers high-revenue platforms through advertising, profiling, and behavioral prediction.

## 3 4 Who Owns the Data Does Not Matter

A common objection to the phrase "data as labor" is likely a legal-economic one, the issue of "ownership."If platform terms of service purportedly make the company the owner of the data, the thought goes, then it is misleading or incoherent to characterize users' activity as labor.

However, the premise is overstated.Most mainstream terms of service for usergenerated content leave the title to user contributions to the user, granting the platform a broad license to collect, process, and commercialize behavioral or usage signals (Lemley, 2006).Even if companies own users' information, that does not necessarily mean that users cannot do labor for the firms.

Consider the case of a software engineer at a tech company who develops patentable algorithms.Intellectual property rights typically belong to the employer.The engineer has no claim to the ownership of the resulting code base.However, no one would dispute that the engineer is a laborer and deserves recognition, protection, and compensation.The company owns the work, but the worker gets paid.The legal structure assigns ownership to the firm, but this does not undermine the idea that the effort of the engineer constitutes labor, nor does it justify offering no compensation.

This logic applies equally to digital users.Even if the platforms legally own the data produced by user activity, as per most click wrap agreements, it remains that these data depend on and result from the user's time, attention, decision-making, and behavior.The activity is labor-like not because it fits traditional definitions, but because it contributes economically to the value of a company, is structurally requested by the design of the platform, and occurs without alternative pathways to negotiation or exit.

The New York Times's 2023 suit against Microsoft and OpenAI, alleging unlicensed training on Times articles and that some model outputs reproduced protected text, illustrates the point rather than providing an exception.If a newspaper's articles can ground claims when copied or used without authorization, so may a blog post or social-media thread that meets the same standards of originality and fixation. 11 Similarly, commercial scientific publishing is based on a pronounced structural asymmetry.Professors provide the essential labor of writing and conducting peer review, but publishers capture most of the resulting economic value.Authors or their institutions often pay Article Processing Charges that can reach several thousand dollars despite the comparatively low marginal costs of digital distribution, and libraries must then repurchase the published content at subscription prices that have outpaced inflation for decades. 12Open access initiatives aim to counter this imbalance by aggregating the bargaining power of universities, libraries, and funders.Much like collective bargaining, these coordinated efforts allow institutions to set baseline conditions for price, access, and reuse, and to resist exploitative "big deal" contracts that no individual scholar could effectively oppose on their own.

The central normative issue, we suggest, is not who legally owns which category of data, but whether the activities through which users generate that data should be regarded as economically productive labor.If such activities can reasonably be classified as labor, then users may have a justified claim to recognition, protection, and compensation because they systematically create value that platforms appropriate, regardless of whether they hold formal title to the data.

In this view, terms of service are better understood as exchange contracts that specify what users receive in return for their data (Kolt, 2019), rather than as categorical denials that user activity could count as labor.The debate over "data as labor" is therefore less a dispute about property in the strict legal sense and more a dispute about justice and fairness in economic transactions.Given that user activity may generate surplus value, should that value be acknowledged, negotiated, and compensated, for example, through wages, credits, data dividends, or collective bargaining rights?


## Definitions of Labor or Work Are Secondary

A familiar concern is probably that the phrase "data as labor" is imprecise or misleading.In a straightforward reading, the data is a product of human activity, not the activity itself.However, our ordinary language shows that people sometimes treat certain outputs as labor products even when they do not legally own them.

Consider a professor who says "This lecture is my labor."She may lack formal ownership.Perhaps her university claims broad IP rights.But she is still entitled to describe the lecture as the product of her effort.This illustrates what we might call a "constitutive view": some output remains closely tied to the human activity that produced it, retaining its moral identity as labor products regardless of legal ownership.

The constitutive view is illuminating but not strictly necessary to defend "data as labor" in our context.One can set it aside and still claim that the contributions users make through their activities are labor-like in the relevant sense.It is this contribution view that we develop primarily in the following.

Assessing whether data-generating activities count as labor often begins with definitional intuitions.Folk conceptions of labor emphasize intensive effort, physical or cognitive stress, necessity, or contributions to society through tangible production or care work.Measured against this background, the idea that scrolling, clicking, or liking constitutes labor can sound implausible.Most users would not say that they "work for" Google or OpenAI while casually browsing, and few would describe an evening spent streaming videos or searching online as time spent performing labor.The challenge, therefore, is to determine whether these activities involve forms of effort, attention, or participation that are systematically harnessed by platforms in ways that resemble and, in some cases, substitute for more traditional forms of labor (Jonker, 2025).

Definitions of labor are not timeless standards.They are shaped by and responsive to evolving economic realities.Labor is not simply what a dictionary says it is.

It is what institutions, policies, and value-attribution systems come to recognize as 1 3

For much of the twentieth century, unpaid domestic and care work was excluded from measured national income, not because it lacked economic value, but because the System of National Accounts drew the production boundary tightly around market transactions and a narrow set of own-account activities, that is, categories that largely aligned with what was socially constructed as "private" family life.Economists have long shown that this boundary is a matter of accounting convention, rather than an inherent feature of the economy.Nordhaus and Tobin (1972) offered an early extension with the Measure of Economic Welfare, incorporating non-market production and environmental degradation, and antici-pating what are now called satellite accounts.Eisner (1988) has similarly developed extended income and product accounts that bring household production, human and other intangible capital, environmental assets, revaluations, and government services into a broader account-ing framework.Folbre (1994) demonstrated that treating care labor as a free or residual input distorts both distributive assessments and efficiency analyzes.And Goldin (2024) placed the unpaid and paid work of women in the center of modern economies, showing how continued responsibility for home and care work contributes to the persistent undervaluation of their labor.

The broader lesson is straightforward: whether an activity is recognized as labor in official statistics reflects institutional and methodological choices about what we count, not the underlying economic importance of the work itself.

Philosophical and sociological research has long supported the view that what counts as work or labor is historically contingent and subject to social negotiation.

For example, the tripartite distinction Arendt (1958) draws between labor, work, and action in The Human Condition helps clarify that the social meaning of labor is historically shaped rather than fixed.In her reconstruction of the classical polis, labor refers to the repetitive and necessary activities tied to biological survival and household maintenance, regarded as the lowest and most private form of human activity.Work denotes the fabrication of durable objects that build a shared world and is held in higher esteem because it leaves behind lasting artifacts.Action, the highest form of activity, consists in speaking and acting with others in public, a mode of life through which individuals reveal who they are and participate in collective self-governance.

Arendt's argument is not that these categories change over time.Their basic structure, life-sustaining labor, world-building work, and public-facing action remain constant.What is historically variable is the status societies assign to them.Modernity, she argues, has elevated the values of the animal laborers -productivity, consumption, and necessity -at the expense of work's durability and action's public freedom.With the rise of "the social," economic activity spills into public life and crowds out spaces for genuine political action.This is precisely why Arendt's framework supports our claim that the boundary of what a society recognizes as "labor" is historically and politically constructed.What counts as a higher or lower activity, and, by extension, what a society treats as socially valuable labor, depends on which human capacities it chooses to institutionalize, reward, and make visible.The labor/non-labor line is therefore malleable, even 1 3 if the underlying human activities of sustaining life, fabricating a world, and acting in concert remain constant. 13 Arendt's framework also helps illuminate the data economy.Data generation does not involve the bodily exertion of traditional labor, the durable artifacts of craftwork, or the visible public engagement of action.It is dispersed, has low-friction, and is largely invisible.However, this does not make it insignificant.In contrast, contemporary digital capitalism depends precisely on this invisibility: platforms continuously extract value from users who do not see themselves as workers, and therefore lack clear avenues for recognition, bargaining, or redress.What remains hidden is not the importance of the work, but the social choice to treat it as something other than labor.

Tasks such as clicking, liking, or typing are often considered trivial.But triviality is not a barrier to economic value.Each digital interaction contributes to the refinement of targeted advertising, recommendation algorithms, and behavioral analytics.The power of platforms lies not in any single click, but in the scale and aggregation of billions of such interactions.Similarly, as individual keystrokes by a typist may seem inconsequential but cumulatively produce a novel, each user's actions feed into vast data-processing systems that generate profits at scale.Some users are explicitly paid to perform exactly these actions.Platforms like JumpTask, Amazon Mechanical Turk, or various gig-based "click farms" offer monetary rewards for liking posts, following accounts, or filling out surveys.These users are understood to be performing labor, even if the task is low-effort and unskilled.If the same activity, performed under a contract and with direct compensation, is acknowledged as labor, then there is no principled reason why the same activity, performed without compensation but under structurally similar conditions, should be excluded from the category.

## Beyond Traditional Labor Law Definitions

Ask a veteran labor lawyer about "data as labor," and the immediate response is jurisdictional: "Who is the employer?"The doctrinal framework has long assumed a dyadic employer-employee structure as the basic site of regulation and remedy.

Data labor is currently unrecognized as labor under U.S. law.Foundational labor statutes such as the Fair Labor Standards Act, 29 U.S.C. S 203(g) and the National Labor Relations Act, 29 U.S.C. S 152(3), define labor in ways tightly bound to the mid-twentieth century model of employment: a formal contractual relationship with a single employer, a defined scope of work, direct oversight, and monetary compensation.These legal definitions assume that a traditional employer-employee structure in which workers are clearly subordinate to and directed by identifiable firms.Under this framework, labor is legally acknowledged only when it fits within the contours of a regulated hierarchical organization of productive activity. 14ourts and regulators have repeatedly struggled to fit gig economy workers, such as Uber drivers or delivery-app couriers, into either category (Kondo & Singer, 2020).As Judge Vince Chhabria put it, the legal task of classifying such workers is like "hand[ing] a square peg and ask[ing] [the jury] to choose between two round holes." 15That observation applies equally to data labor.Users' economic contributions do not map neatly onto existing categories, but the absence of a clear legal label does not change the fact that something of value is being produced and captured.

Legal systems have begun to recognize analogous dynamics in adjacent domains (Posner, 2024).Consider unpaid internships that extract skilled labor under the guise of "training," often requiring extensive hours of real work while offering no wages.These are cases in which legal and economic systems benefit from productive activity without formally acknowledging it as labor.Similarly, recent federal appellate decisions have re-examined the status of NCAA athletes and unpaid interns, holding in one instance that student athletes may plausibly be considered employees given the time, training, and profit generation they facilitate, 16 and in another that unpaid interns performing core business tasks cannot be excluded from labor protections merely because they receive no monetary compensation. 17

In recent years, lawmakers have begun to experiment with treating gig work as a form of labor without traditional employment.California's Assembly Bill 5 (AB5) tightened the test for classifying workers as independent contractors, explicitly aiming to curb misclassifications and extend basic labor protections to app-based drivers and other gig workers.Industry push-back produced a counter-model in Proposition 22, which preserves drivers' status as independent contractors while layering on a bespoke set of wage floors, insurance, and benefit guarantees, effectively creating a hybrid regime in which drivers remain non-employees but are nonetheless recognized as a group whose labor warrants tailored protections.

Outside the U.S., the European Union's Platform Work Directive introduces a rebuttable presumption that many platform workers are in an employment relationship, and Canadian labor law has long recognized "dependent contractors" as an intermediate category between employees and independent contractors, bringing certain platform drivers and couriers within the ambit of collective bargaining law even when they are not classic employees. 18 Data labor occupies a structurally similar position.Like gig drivers, users who generate data for platforms often lack a conventional employer, schedule, or workplace, yet their activity is a core input into highly profitable business models.The emerging treatment of gig workers demonstrates that law can detach recognition and protection from the narrow category of "employee" and design intermediate or bespoke regimes for contributors whose work falls outside twentieth-century templates.By analogy, data as labor need not be forced into the orthodox employment box to become visible to law and policy: it can be treated, like platform driving, as a form of productive activity that justifies its own set of rights, safeguards, and institutional mechanisms, even in the absence of a standard employment relationship. 15Johnson v. The deeper issue is structural.Data labor exposes a fault line in the foundations of labor law: the assumption that productive contribution, legal protection, and economic recognition must follow formal employment status.That assumption no longer reflects the architecture of the modern economy.Data production is now a central input to value creation in many of the world's most profitable firms.That it is currently unpaid and legally unacknowledged does not mean that it is not labor.This pattern is well documented in platform studies.In particular, Zuboff (2019) shows how platforms structure environments to extract "behavioral surplus" without overt coercion.


## What Matters: Fairness

The central issue in evaluating whether data generation should count as labor is not semantics or formal taxonomy.It is a question of fairness: Are contractual and institutional arrangements between users and platforms justifiable given how value is created and appropriated?Platforms rely on continuous streams of user activity; users, by contrast, are bound by non-negotiable terms that offer no compensation, representation, or voice.The resulting asymmetry allows one party to extract disproportionate value while the other receives neither recognition nor recourse.In our view, this is the normative core of the "data as a labor" claim.

Contract theorists have long been concerned about structurally similar arrangements.In U.S. law, the doctrine of unconscionability permits courts to refuse enforcement when an agreement combines procedural unfairness (pressure, confusion, or informational gaps) with substantive unfairness (terms so one-sided that they offend reasonable standards of equity) (McCullough, 2016).Classic cases involve poor consumers who sign written installment contracts to ensure that they almost never build equity in the goods they "buy," and who lose everything with a single missed payment.Formally, they consent; substantively, they are locked into a scheme that manufactures vulnerability.

Philosophers have shown how unconscionability can be understood as a principled response to this kind of exploitation, rather than as a paternalistic override of individual choice.Shiffrin (2000) argues that even when competent parties voluntarily assent, the state may refuse to enforce agreements that are seriously one-sided because it has reason not to lend its coercive power to exploitative projects.The point is not to protect imprudent agents from themselves, but to protect the community's own integrity.Strudler (2009) makes a parallel move in his analysis of insider trading contracts.Even if shareholders and managers could, in principle, agree to license insider trading as a form of compensation, any such contract would be substantively unconscionable; it would require shareholders to pay an unreasonably high price in heightened and opaque vulnerability to manipulation for whatever benefit they received.Because those contracts are exploitative in that specific way, they cannot legitimately transfer informational rights; insider trading remains wrongful even under a permissive formal regime.

If we translate these ideas into the platform setting, an analogy may help.Consider a gym that offers free memberships under the condition that members wear a tracker whose data the gym can resell.The contract is non-negotiable, the cancellation process is deliberately obstructive, and the gym reserves the right to change terms unilaterally.Members might still rationally sign if they lack better options for exercise, but they are required to pay an unreasonably high hidden price: pervasive, non-revocable exposure to profiling and exclusion in other markets they cannot foresee.

In a Shiffrin-Strudler view, the state may reasonably refuse to enforce the most one-sided aspects of such an arrangement, not because members are irrational, but because the gym's business model depends on manufacturing and exploiting vulnerability.In Cornell's view, the gym's choice to structure its revenue model around that vulnerability undermines its standing to complain if members later resist or circumvent the terms (Cornell, 2016).

Platform data relations look much the same, scaled up.Users provide continuous, structured, monetizable activity in terms they cannot meaningfully negotiate and often cannot even read.Platforms extract, aggregate, and license that activity to third parties, while invoking boilerplate consent, mandatory arbitration, and class-action waivers to insulate themselves from challenge.

In light of the unconscionability literature, the fairness question can be put simply: "Is the price users pay in surveillance, profiling, and exclusion unreasonably high relative to what they receive, given the background conditions that shape their choices?"If so, then the problem with current data-for-service contracts is not simply that they misdescribe users as consumers rather than laborers.It is that they instantiate a pattern of structural exploitation in which one side has both the power to dictate the terms and the institutional backing to enforce them.In this sense, fairness provides the most promising normative framework for making that injustice visible and contestable.

A common objection is probably that unconscionability is rarely applied, and therefore toothless.However, recent empirical work suggests that courts use it more often and with greater nuance than is typically assumed, particularly where systemic power disparities dis-tort contract formation and outcomes (McCall, 2020).That record offers a template for adapting the doctrine to the digital context. 19 In the end, the moral heart of the data as labor argument lies here: in the asymmetry between what is given and what is received.When users provide continuous, structured, monetizable activity, and platforms extract that activity without meaningful reciprocity, we are no longer in the domain of free exchange.We are in the domain of structural injustice.The concept of fairness may provide the most compelling language and the most robust normative framework to make that injustice visible and actionable.

Up to this point, we have argued that users' ongoing online activities may be understood as a form of labor that produces and maintains the datasets and behavioral signals on which contemporary AI systems depend, and that this re-conceptualization matters for how we think about power, entitlement, and institutional design in the platform economy.This section turns from diagnosis to strategy by examining "data strikes": forms of collective action that operationalize the "data as labor" view.

It is a simple but powerful thought: if data generation is a form of labor, then we can also ask what platforms owe to the people who perform it.One natural response is to turn to markets.In a standard sense, the market exchange is the central mechanism for discovering prices and allocating value.If data labor is treated as something that can be contracted for, then the question becomes not whether users should be compensated, but how the terms of that exchange are set.

However, in ordinary labor markets, prices are not determined by abstract supply and demand alone; they are shaped by institutions such as unions and collective bargaining (Card et al., 2017).Balanced and fair contracts typically require workers to organize and negotiate with firms on an equal footing.The same logic could apply to data as labor.Users can, in principle, form collective bodies, such as data unions, cooperatives, or other representative organizations, to bargain with platforms over the terms on which their data are used.Crucially, such bargaining need not focus exclusively on monetary compensation or individualized payments.It may concern privacy protections, governance rights, access to benefits, or shared control over data infrastructure.What matters is that the parties are free to structure the deal in ways that reflect the actual interests at stake, rather than treating data labor as if it were costless or invisible.

"Data strikes" name a family of tactics, including selective withholding, deletion, rerout-ing, or deliberate obfuscation of personal data, through which users interrupt the pipelines that fuel contemporary AI technologies.The practice overturns the fiction that users are merely consumers.Once users are understood as data laborers, it becomes clear why withdrawal has teeth.When participation decreases or becomes noisy, predictive accuracy de-clines, feedback loops weaken, and commercial viability erodes (Vincent et al., 2019a;Vincent & Hecht, 2021).

As with industrial strikes, the aim is not refusal for its own sake, but a public assertion of indispensableness.Withdrawing labor imposes measurable costs on those who depend on it (Arrieta-Ibarra et al., 2018).Data strikes make the same claim in a digital key: collective abstention, tracker blocking, and signal pollution declare that participation is not a gift, but a contribution subject to negotiation.In this sense, data strikes instantiate the shift, developed in the previous sections, from a consumer sovereignty picture to one in which users appear as co-producers with standing to bargain.

The technical reason that these actions have bite is structural.Many data-dependent systems are tuned to high-speed behavioral flows and therefore sensitive to recency, coverage, and context.Leaving or distorting these inputs impairs calibration, increases exploration costs, and blunts real-time adaptation; even moderate coordinated disruptions can reduce ad relevance and recommendation quality enough 1 3

to force costly engineering reallocations (see e.g., experiments meant to specifically explore "data strike"-style scenarios (Vincent et al., 2019b;Vincent et al., 2021), as well higher level papers from industry labs that describe data-dependence (Covington et al., 2016)).Models can coast for a time on stored parameters, but dynamic services-search, social networks, and e-commerce recommendations-depend on continual refresh with timely context-specific data.Data as labor helps to interpret these technical facts as a form of structural bargaining power rather than as mere background conditions.

In response to the growing potential impact of data-related collective action, machine learning scholars have begun to study more aspects of such this action (Ben-Dov et al., 2024;Gauthier et al., 2025;Hardt et al., 2023;Sigg and Hardt, 2024).This work formalizes the idea of treating users not as inert training points but as strategic collectives capable of steering model behavior.

In this view, coordinated non-contribution or controlled randomness functions as algorithmic protest: a way of bargaining over design, defaults, and oversight rather than an attempt at malice.These models give analytical backing to the normative claim advanced earlier that users occupy something like a workplace relation to platforms, even in the absence of formal employment.

The public readiness to engage in such tactics is increasing.Surveys report growing aware-ness of data extraction and increasing adoption of ad blockers, privacy-preserving browsers, opt out tools, reduced engagement, privacy-focused migration, and encryption or obfuscation-individually modest moves that, in aggregate, degrade data quality and reveal latent capacity for coordination (Li et al., 2019;McClain et al., 2023;Story et al., 2021).What looks, from the perspective of individual choice, like scattered privacy-protective behavior can, from the data-labor perspective, be reinterpreted as incipient collective action.

Scale and organization remain the central obstacles.Dispersed, asymmetrically informed users need intermediaries.Data unions and related organizations can pool bargaining power, establish norms for fair use, and condition access on concrete terms, such as dividends, transparency reports, purpose limitation, revocability, and opt-out clauses.This is the in-stitutional counterpart to the normative arguments for recognition and fair terms developed above.

Existing law already supplies procedural levers: GDPR's portability and erasure rights and the CCPA's opt-out and deletion rights make collective removal and licensing practically feasible. 20Proposals for dedicated data intermediaries envision entities that aggregate and manage member data, audit compliance, and negotiate limited, purpose-bound licenses with firms, thereby lowering individual cognitive burdens and translating diffuse data labor into organized bargaining power (Lanier & Weyl, 2018).

Full-exit is often costly given the infrastructural role of search, social platforms, cloud storage, and navigation.But a data strike need not be total.Partial tactics targeting tracker disablement or the introduction of calibrated noise can allow users to 20 An alternative to full-scale data withdrawal is conscious data contribution, in which users direct their data to ethical firms or public interest research, thereby maintaining engagement while reshaping incentives and encouraging more accountable AI development (Vincent & Hecht, 2021).exert pressure while maintaining the necessary functionality.These forms of "voice without exit" complement the legal and contractual strategies discussed earlier by providing a credible threat of disruption when formal channels stall.

Platforms do have countermeasures: synthetic data generation, scraping of public sources, or simulated behavior can partially substitute for lost inputs.However, these substitutes typically add noise, amplify bias, and degrade evaluation fidelity; they also impose substantial engineering overhead and face tightening regulatory constraints.As a result, substitution often proves more expensive than bargaining, nudging firms toward negotiated terms with organized users (Vincent et al., 2021).

From the perspective of this paper's broader argument, data strikes thus help explain why treating users as mere licensees of their data, without corresponding bargaining institutions, misdescribes the strategic situation.

The trajectory of data strikes will turn on three mutually reinforcing fronts.Legal inno-vation can engender portability, transparency, default opt-in, and purpose limitation, giving de jure support to the de facto leverage of data labor.Institutional design, user-owned cooperatives, data unions, and other intermediaries can provide durable governance, compliance auditing, and collective bargaining capacity.Corporate reform can prevent confrontation by offering data dividends, participatory governance, simplified terms, and meaningful appeal and oversight mechanisms (Vincent et al., 2019b).

Historically, strikes are both instruments of economic disruption and claims of democratic agency.Properly understood, a data strike makes the same claim: users are co-creators of value entitled to recognition, bargaining rights, and a share of returns. 21 This conclusion returns us to the central theme of the paper.Our argument does not rest on inevitability: data can count as labor even when individuals choose among venues of production.The normative questions are about recognition and fair terms, not metaphysical compulsion, and data strikes make those questions visible in practice.


## Concluding Remarks

This paper has argued that user-generated data should be recognized not as a passive byproduct of digital participation, but as a form of labor.The activities through which users inter-act with platforms, including scrolling, clicking, liking, searching, and sharing, are not mere expressions of leisure or consumption.They are the productive inputs that power contemporary digital economies.They train machine learning models, optimize content delivery, and generate behavioral insights that fuel corporate profitability.Platforms do not simply offer services; they extract structured, patterned human behavior and convert it into capital.In doing so, they depend on a form of labor that remains unrecognized, unregulated, and uncompensated.Recognizing user-generated data as a form of labor has important implications for legal, regulatory, and corporate practice.If data production is understood not as an incidental by-product of digital life but as a core economic activity, then principles of justice may call for governing it accordingly.At the legislative level, policy makers can move toward treating data generation as a labor-like activity.

This could involve creating new forms of legal protection inspired by traditional labor rights but adapted for digital contexts.One promising direction is the development of collective bargaining mechanisms for data producers.Just as industrial workers organized to negotiate the terms of their labor, users may, in some contexts, benefit from the ability to collectively negotiate with platforms over data use, privacy, compensation, and algorithmic governance.

Policymakers might also explore universal or progressive data dividends funded by corporate profits derived from user-generated data.These payments do not need to capture the precise economic value of individual data; instead, they can serve to recognize the collective contribution of user activity and redistribute a portion of the resulting gains in a manner that affirms digital dignity.To promote accountability and equity, any such redistributive mechanism could be paired with transparent reporting standards, third-party audits, and public oversight bodies empowered to monitor compliance and evaluate distributional impacts.

In parallel with legal and regulatory reform, platforms themselves have an opportunity to demonstrate ethical leadership by voluntarily adopting fairness-oriented data governance practices.Firms may publicly acknowledge that user data is not merely a byproduct of engagement but a central input into their business models, and that users, as contributors, warrant recognition.

Beyond compliance and profit redistribution, both companies and governments have a broader responsibility to support democratic dialogue about the future of the data economy.Public understanding of data labor, platform governance, and algorithmic accountability remains limited.Through investments in civic education, transparency initiatives, and par-ticipatory design processes, institutions can help ensure that decisions about digital labor are made openly and with democratic input.Public participation is not incidental to justice; it is one of its conditions.A data economy that is equitable, accountable, and sustainable will depend not only on laws and systems, but also on shared understandings of fairness, public norms of recognition, and institutional commitments to ethical innovation.

History offers a useful analogy.When horse-drawn transport filled cities with waste, some owners individually strapped buckets to their animals, collected manure, and sold it.That intermediary fix monetized a by-product, which is one way to contribute to the problem.A path we propose is collective action.
