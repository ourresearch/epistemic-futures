# An inventory of big data books

## Full text

##### A *quick scan *of tomes related to data, AI, algorithms, etc

I’ve been asked to recommend a good book on big data that’s not [Big Data](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/0544227751/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=cukier-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=0544227751&linkId=a5e3015f98588359655960e376a99122) (which I coauthored with Viktor Mayer-Schönberger, images below).

It’s a hard question for several reasons. First, though I’m usually brutally critical of my work, it’s not a bad book.

Yet it’s also a difficult question because there are so many good books to chose from. The question becomes: which book is right *for whom*? This is an overview of what’s been published in recent years. I’ll continually update it.

[***The Signal and the Noise***](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/0143125087/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&camp=1789&creative=9325&creativeASIN=0143125087&linkCode=as2&tag=cukier-20&linkId=b8b7323449210576071b1cc19b2739c1)***: Why So Many Predictions Fail but Some Don’t, *by Nate Silver**

This is an excellent book but it’s not for everyone. As a thoughtful look at how new statistical approaches have made inroads to new domains, it’s great. Policy wonks and sports geeks should love it. But it’s not right for business people trying to understand how data creates new sources of value and areas of competition. Indeed Silver rejects the idea of “big data” (since it can lead to spurious correlations). Yet that’s shortsighted: more data is inherently better because we can learn *new things* with it.

[***Naked Statistics***](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/039334777X/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&camp=1789&creative=9325&creativeASIN=039334777X&linkCode=as2&tag=cukier-20&linkId=a5d33cb7f0a28c34360887acaf2b79df)***: Stripping the Dread from the Data*, by Charles Wheelan: **If getting a basic grounding in statistics is what you’re after, then this book is pure gold. It is the very best introduction to statistics that exists — and far better than the pack of “cartoon guides” to stats (there are several), which are mostly abstruse but with silly pictures. (My review in *The Economist* is [here](http://www.economist.com/news/books-and-arts/21573958-accessible-primer-number-crunching-joy-stats).)

> [**The Second Machine Age**](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/0393350649/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=cukier-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=0393350649&linkId=853ae0013f71d19ae0ba189e84f15031)**: Work, Progress, and Prosperity in a Time of Brilliant Technologies,* by Erik Brynjolfsson and Andrew Mcafee***

This book is great. The authors are deep thinkers and the book teems with fresh ideas. Being academics, they do a great job of reviewing the literature on the themes they take on. So there’s depth, not just insight. One caveat: it is very much a book about economics, not technology and society per se. The authors have unleashed a smart, urgent debate on the role of technological change and its effect on employment.

[***The Wealth of Humans***](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/1250075807/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=cukier-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=1250075807&linkId=6b540b53224ea5775054ca12f040070f)***: Work and its Absence in the Twenty-first Century,* by Ryan Avent**

A book that promises to be a smart, shrewd read from *The Economist’s* senior economics writer. I plan to read it very soon — it’s on my night table staring at me, after all. Avent is a careful reporter, clever observer and big, bold thinker. So this should be a terrific work (even if I resist the dyspeptic view of an AI-induced “jobpocalypse.” I’ll review it soon here and on Amazon.

[***The Rise of the Robots***](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/1780747497/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=cukier-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=1780747497&linkId=b03467f18f16a91c73fce6d0dfb415fb)***: Technology and the Threat of Mass Unemployment* by Martin Ford: **A good book on a similar theme as “2MA” (above): how technology is stealing jobs. Ford is a software developer, not an economist, so he really knows the state of the technology, though it lacks the historical sweep of 2MA. He also has a fresh set of eyes with which to examine the issue. Like 2MA, Ford is deeply pessimistic about the effect of AI on jobs.

[***The Future of the Professions***](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/0198799071/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=cukier-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=0198799071&linkId=317e3630ea2d62428f6466e665cdf945)***: How Technology Will Transform the Work of Human Experts*, by Richard Susskind and Daniel Susskind: **This is the book I’ve been meaning to read for over a year and haven’t yet — but will soon. So watch this space. The father-son pair have a reputation for being very smart. And they’re taking on a critical issue. So I look forward to reading the book very soon.

[***Humans Need Not Apply***](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/0300213557/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=cukier-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=0300213557&linkId=06500e758fff71a9feb27b5aa3eb2562)***: A Guide to Wealth and Work in the Age of Artificial Intelligence,* by Jerry Kaplan**

I loved this book. I judge books by their covers, and I confess I hated the title and cover image. But when I reviewed three AI books for *The Economist* ([here](http://www.economist.com/news/books-and-arts/21669597-computers-will-get-smarter-humans-charge-machines-thinking)) this was the best of the bunch. It is technically accurate (the author is an AI expert, not a generalist), well written, funny and most importantly, it’s loaded with original, ambitious ideas. In one section, Kaplan considers robot liability by looking at how US law treated another area of “property”: slaves. It’s odious to consider morally, but an example of the bold conceptual thinking throughout the book. And his description of wealth inequality at the middle of the book is jaw-dropping and brilliant — with an “O’Henry twist” at the end.

[***Machines of Loving Grace***](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/0062266691/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=cukier-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=0062266691&linkId=294bcda080f0d8169b52c453989e50a3)***: The Quest for Common Ground Between Humans and Robots,* by John Markoff: **Arguably the most important technology journalist of our day (and one of the nicest), Markoff’s books are always great and this is no exception. The book delves into a crucial dichotomy of AI systems: augment human capabilities or replace humans? A feature of the book is that it looks at the personalities behind the code. But some readers may be bothered that this narrative technique means it doesn’t delve into deeper questions that the technology poses. Chapter 4 on the history of AI, at 65 pages, is the best, single summary that exists.

[***The Master Algorithm***](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/0465065708/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=cukier-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=0465065708&linkId=25e0b39ca5e6938fd5115f76fa3c73a4)***: How the Quest for the Ultimate Learning Machine Will Remake Our World,* by Pedro Domingos: **This is a book that I liked but didn’t love. It aims to explain the different techniques of machine-learning. But in an attempt to simplify the subject, it was rendered both complicated and simplistic. Domingos uses analogies to describe the technology rather than explain how it works. And he uses invented examples rather than dissect the actual, amazing breakthroughs in recent years. That was a missed opportunity. Some readers may feel let down.

[***The Glass Cage***](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/0393350770/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=cukier-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=0393350770&linkId=a10c9b0f854c40e2ff28d624105f9499)*** — Automation and Us*, by Nicholas Carr**

Everything Carr touches turns to gold for three reasons: he see critical issues early, he does strong research (uncovering obscure but telling facts) and he’s a great writer. Here, he offers a thoughtful critique of the age of AI, algos and bots. As they help us by handling hard tasks for us, we become less able to do things for ourselves — with potentially severe consequences. These are tradeoffs that few talk about because few appreciate it. Carr put it on the agenda.

[***The Black Box Society***](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/0674970845/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=cukier-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=0674970845&linkId=c4e37c59c7a13398d43c58eea1ae5480)***: The Secret Algorithms That Control Money*, by Frank Pasquale: **The downsides of big data, mostly from a legal viewpoint. I only skimmed this and need to spend more time with it.

[***The Economic Singularity***](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/099321164X/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=cukier-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=099321164X&linkId=83c52f2e44786636eb1f65ebce59769c)***: Artificial intelligence and the death of capitalism*, by Calum Chace**

An overview of the concerns that AI will lead to severe unemployment and whether a 
universal basic income might work as a balm to joblessness. The book takes a rather pessimistic view. From the author of [*Pandora’s Brain*](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/0993211607/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=cukier-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=0993211607&linkId=d10dadf45e951ccdcf176934b84d6767) (fiction, 2014) and [*Surviving AI*](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/0993211623/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=cukier-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=0993211623&linkId=700080247b6138062efa14f4edc8afbd)*: The promise and peril of artificial intelligence* (2015).

[***Superintelligence***](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/0198739834/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=cukier-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=0198739834&linkId=fab14f024b738d19753cbcaec23ee45a)***: Paths, Dangers, Strategies*, by Nick Bostrom: **Fine as far as a book on philosophy and technology but ridiculous in other contexts. Basically Bostrom believes that AI may become so smart that it can destroy mankind — but not so smart that it might suss out that this would be a bad thing. The fact that so many people such as Elon Musk cite this book, by an academic philosopher not AI expert, is proof that that we need artificial intelligence since the authentic stuff is so wanting. That said, read as a book of philosophy, it’s fine.

[***Our Final Invention***](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/1250058783/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=cukier-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=1250058783&linkId=d34234611d64dcf1b8bbbfac0f5c1bbf)***: Artificial Intelligence and the End of the Human Era, *by James Barrat: **A good introduction to the AI-dystopian arguments. The history of early AI is well researched and well written. But the pessimistic view seems over done and without a look at the counter evidence.

[***Big Data***](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/1472920058/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=cukier-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=1472920058&linkId=eb5dea6f581d2e6a1ce3d41cd0cc045c)***: Does Size Matter? *by Timandra Harkness**

The author is a great, witty journalist — and she even hinted I should be sure to give this a shout-out.

But I’ve not yet given it a proper read.

I’ll do that…

##### *Other books of note (that I’ll finish writing-up soon):*

[***Automate This***](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/1591846528/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=cukier-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=1591846528&linkId=53d6375797380a059d92bff000bc1b04)***: How Algorithms Took Over Our Markets, Our Jobs, and the World*, by Christopher Steiner: **This book was a fun, interesting read. It has some great vignettes, such as the advent of algo-based stock trading and how NASA uses data to match astronauts together by personality — to avoid disastrous tensions while sitting in a tin can far above the world. It may lean too heavily on the financial-markets dimension rather than the bigger, societal stakes. And it doesn’t explain the technology. But it’s a solid book.

[***Predictive Analytics***](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/1119145678/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=cukier-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=1119145678&linkId=453cf3322e9ce55afcdffc7259ec4dc2)***: The Power to Predict Who Will Click, Buy, Lie, or Die,* by Eric Siegel: **A book intended for practitioners of predictive analytics, and loaded with real-life examples. Those “in the biz” will love it; for others, it’s not the introduction they need.

[***Big Data at Work***](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/1422168166/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=cukier-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=1422168166&linkId=c024da694db971f6b28a3b5310e51de0)***: Dispelling the Myths, Uncovering the Opportunities*, by Thomas H. Davenport** : The author is a business professor who spotted analytics (now sometimes rebranded as “big data”) a decade ago and has produced several books on it. They’re for corporate managers who need to understand and implement data technologies, not for general readers.

[***The Inevitable***](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/0525428089/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=cukier-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=0525428089&linkId=f6d938b753a4b8098427659bdd07d513)***: Understanding the 12 Technological Forces That Will Shape Our Future,* by Kevin Kelly**

[***Rise of the Machines***](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/0393286002/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=cukier-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=0393286002&linkId=134ffc99e471550ca9d58f3fb6ab6fc0)***: The lost history of cybernetics*, by Thomas Rid**

[***The Industries of the Future***](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/1476753652/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=cukier-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=1476753652&linkId=fa05c4c739ca34a4afbd6fd90be19d2a)**, by Alec Ross**

[***Data Smart***](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/111866146X/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=cukier-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=111866146X&linkId=c44b790aa780fe538633e88e0fce6ec7)***: Using Data Science to Transform Information into Insight, *by John W. Foreman**

[***Smart Cities***](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/0393349780/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=cukier-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=0393349780&linkId=bd0de719a38f8982a9b7f007a5b061bc)***: Big Data, Civic Hackers, and the Quest for a New Utopia*, by Anthony M. Townsend**

[***What to Think About Machines That Think***](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/006242565X/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=cukier-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=006242565X&linkId=7650cda180cd46f85b7ef1eca49b3912)***: Today’s Leading Thinkers on the Age of Machine Intelligence, *by John Brockman**

[***The Technological Singularity***](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/0262527804/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=cukier-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=0262527804&linkId=06418faa29bfa328d6b60434f4710881)**, by Murray Shanahan**

[***Machine Learning***](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/0262529513/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=cukier-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=0262529513&linkId=af4e69781fe197f8e38e36983f39c374)***: The New AI*, by Ethem Alpaydin**

[***Humans Are Underrated***](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/0143108379/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=cukier-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=0143108379&linkId=216d93eb9b26ef471b00dc96c917ea0d)***: What High Achievers Know that Brilliant Machines Never Will*, by Geoff Colvin: **The book is good: it’s fun, simple, “life-and-business lessons” carried forward by interesting anecdotes written well. However despite the hint of AI in the subtitle, it has nothing to do with AI. And what makes humans special is what you’d expect: our personable qualities; our humanity. Ergo: great read, not deep, not AI.

[***Dataclysm***](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/0385347391/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=cukier-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=0385347391&linkId=133a71b4c29f6eef8c72f9bacd4d88a7)***: Love, Sex, Race, and Identity — What Our Online Lives Tell Us about Our Offline Selves*, by Christian Rudder**: From the co-founder of OKCupid (and a data/math guy), a look at how data is brought to bear to help people find mates online — and what that tells us about human relationships. A fun, fascinating book that opens the mind to how data can be applied to other questions in society that beg data-driven answers.

[***Super Crunchers***](https://www.amazon.com/gp/product/0553384732/ref=as_li_tl?ie=UTF8&tag=cukier-20&camp=1789&creative=9325&linkCode=as2&creativeASIN=0553384732&linkId=d4a6e8d536eef7b53d01e063198273b7)***: Why Thinking-By-Numbers is the New Way To Be Smart,* Ian Ayres**: An older book (2007) that now feels its age, it delves into how the web allows us to measure, quantify and run stats against things we never could before. The book mostly looks at A/B testing rather than nod to big data or machine-learning. But that’s the trail he spotted early and explored. (Note: Ayres is one of the most creative, ambitious and formidable intellects alive today. So everything he writes is worthwhile.)
