# ICTs to Reduce Climate Change: The coming age of ubiquitous networking (slides)

## Full text (slide deck)

ICTs to Reduce Climate Change:
The coming age of ubiquitous networking
Kenneth Neil Cukier
GIIC - April 25, 2008 - Tokyo, Japan

Good morning.
Yesterday and today we heard a lot about how the IT and telecoms industries
are responsible for around 2% of global carbon emissions. And we have
looked at how the industry can reduce its carbon footprint in two ways. First,
in its own operations (as Matt Bross of BT did an excellent job of describing).
Second, in the products that the industry provides to users, which can be made
more energy efficient (as many speakers have discussed).

1

The Network of Tomorrow

Moreover, some speakers have noted how technology can be applied to
reducing the more pressing 98% of carbon emissions. It represents far greater
stakes, and frankly, it affords more scope for improvement. Many people have
mentioned the role that a new age of sensors can play, but their remarks have
been very general since such products scarcely exist and few people have any
real experience with them.

2

Outline
- Tech Trends: “Marconi Meets Moores Law”
- Current and future uses:
* cars
* buildings
* appliances
- Public Policy & Regulation
- Conclusion

So this morning I would like to drill down into this topic, to note: 1. What is
happening and where these things come from in context of technology trends;
2. How sensors can be used to reduce carbon emissions; and 3. Some of the
future issues we can expect in an age of ubiquitous sensor networks.

3

Trends in I.T.
* 900 million PCs
* +1 billion Internet users
* 3.2 billion mobile phone subscribers
(1.6 million new mobile subscribers per day)
* 10 billion microprocessors in 2007

We are all familiar with the success of the IT and telecoms industry. The
numbers speak for themselves. But what is important to see is that the real
momentum is hidden from view: around 10 billion processors were shipped
last year, in everything from cars and televisions, to toasters and children’s
toys, not to mention computers and cell phones.
Most of these processors can “think” but not “talk” -- that is, they process data
but do not communicate over a network. However, this is changing. A simple
look at technology trends explains why and how. It is these chips that will
power a new world of sensors that can be used to promote energy efficiency
and alleviate climate change.

4

Trends in Processors: Moore’s Law

Gordon Moore was of course an Intel engineer who in the 1960s noted that the
number of transistors on a chip doubled roughly every 18 months. More
generally, every two years the power of a chip doubles and its cost and size
halves.

5

Trends in Wireless Efficiency

This trend actually mirrors the improvements in wireless efficiency. Since
Marconi’s first wireless transmission around 1900, the engineering community
has been able to increase the amount of data that can be sent through a given
amount of the airwaves by around 1 trillion times.

6

Trends in Wireless Cost

Likewise, the price has fallen at a similar pace. Since Marconi’s first wireless
transmission, the engineering community has been able to reduce the cost of
transmitting data by around 1 trillion times. Note, these charts are not “linear”
but “logarithmic” scales (they progress in factors of ten, not one by one), with
the corresponding technology noted.

7

Trends in Power Consumption

The success even appears in the area of power consumption. It is less well
known, but the amount of energy needed to power wireless devices has also
decreased in a logarithmic fashion for the past 25 years. This is important,
because the need to provide energy is a huge obstacle for wireless
communications. We are already at the stage where the battery for wireless
sensors are between five and ten times the size of the chip that it powers.

8

Machines vs. Humans
- M2M to surpass people circa 2009-2011
- Cell phones communicate with base-stations
800 times per second (power management)
- 400 billion DNS queries per day in 2010 (peak
load of 4 trillion)

What these trends suggest is that the basic infrastructure now exists for
wireless sensors to be applied to new areas, and thus they can unlock new uses.
One of the most promising places they are cropping up is in the area of
monitoring the environment and reducing energy consumption.

9

Taxonomy of Wireless Technologies

There are many different types of wireless technologies that will coexist for
different uses. The important thing to note is that in all cases, their data-rate is
fast and getting faster, and their cost is low and getting lower.

10

Examples
* GPS & Bluetooth chip: $1; size of match-head
* Zigbee chip: $4, size of pinky-nail (1/4 cost &
size in 4 yrs
* RFID tag: ¥5
* RFID prototype: 0.05mm (groove in fingerprint)
* RFID 2007 sales: 1.7 million

The degree to which these technologies are not only powerful, but small and
inexpensive, is why they are being so readily added to so many things. I would
also note that it is an area where Japanese electronics company lead the world.

11

Market Size by Volume

The volume of devices is expected to increase tremendously over the next five
years, as data from two research organizations suggest.

12

Market Breakdown by Application

Analysts expect the uses to be varied, with remote monitoring of devices -- the
sort of activity that can reduce carbon emissions -- representing the most
common type of devices.

13

Uses
* Cars: GPS; traffic avoidance
* Sensors: buildings; environment
* New infrastructure: lighting; building controls
* Applainces: power management
* Cellphones: the gateway & controller

The uses of sensors in regards to energy efficiency are manifold. The sensors
enable us to measure and manage things in a ways that were not possible
before. So cars will be more efficient by taking more direct routes to their
destination. Buildings can adjust lighting and heating based on where the
people are at any moment. Appliances can interact with the electrical power
grid to adjust their consumption to prevent blackouts due to overuse.

14

Future Uses: Cars
* Accident notification
* Predictive maintenance
* Toll charges
* Green uses: GPS & traffic monitoring

Cars are being equipped with radio-devices not just for GPS but accident
notification, tolls and predictive maintenance (eg, knowing before a part fails
that something is amiss). But once the infrastructure is in place, it can be used
for other purposes, such as identifying if there is traffic congestion so that cars
can take alternative routes that ultimately save fuel.

15

Future Uses: Buildings
* Security alarm
* Operations monitoring
* Predictive maintenance
* Greeen uses: lighting & heating controls

Factories and office buildings are among the biggest consumers of energy.
Sensors can easily be applied for environmental compliance, as well as to
improve the operational processes. For example, lights can be turned on and
off depending on if a person is in the area. Hotels are starting to use them, so
they can cut the heat or air-conditioning once a guest checks out, which lets the
firm save money as well as reduce its energy needs.

16

Future Uses: Appliances

* Monitor energy use
* Remote control
* Predictive maintenance
* ‘Green-Goods’: “smart grid” systems

Companies are developing appliances like air-conditioners, refrigerators and
other household goods that can interact with the electrical power-grid and scale
back consumption at times of peak demand.

17

Obstacles

* Who would do it?
* Who will pay for it?
* Do we need it?
* Will it actually work??!!

Obstacles are plentiful. The least discussed -- because it is rather humbling for
the system’s designers -- is whether the stuff will actually work.

18

Public Policy
* Privacy & data-security
* Intellectual property
* Radio-waves and health
* Spectrum availability and allocation
* Access to ICANN’s IPv6 addresses

The systems pose a plethora of concerns. First and foremost is privacy and
data-security. IP will be a big issue, keeping GIIC members who are IP
lawyers employed for many years to come. Likewise, access to IPv6 addresses
from ICANN will be important for those who want globally-routable
addresses.

19

Regulation

* Who, what, where, when, how?
* Bilateral vs. multilateral relationships
* Public- vs. private-sector governance
* The risk of regulating too soon

However, regulating this world leaves us with more questions than answers. In
addition to the uncertainty of the way regulation might be enforced, there are
other concerns. For example, regulatory policy tends to treat issues in a
bilateral way: between individual and the state, and company and customer.
But in the wireless world that is emerging, there will be multiple parties
involved all the time, as different networks link up on the fly to do new things
(akin to a Web 2.0 “mashup”). There are questions over whether the
governance is best done by the public or private sector, and the dangers of
imposing rules too soon.

20

Comparison of Cellphone & M2M units

Ultimately, the technical, economic and regulatory obstacles will probably be
overcome. Today, the mobile phone gets all the attention, and rightly so, since
the number of devices and volume of traffic swamps machine-to-machine
communications and sensors. But things will change.
The point about these new technologies is that, unlike phones, they will not be
seen. Instead, connections will be invisibly added to all kinds of things.
(Indeed, already more than 1 billion RFID tags are in operation. It is primitive
technology, for sure, but they are a start.)

21

Conclusion
“People often overestimate what will
happen in the next two years and
underestimate what will happen in ten.”
-- Bill Gates, “The Road Ahead,” 1995

That should give us the right perspective with which to consider the changes
taking place -- and how they can be brought to bear in the area of energy
efficiency to reduce carbon emissions and alleviate climate change.
We are deploying a new infrastructure. The era of ubiquitous networking will
bring new services, connections and ways for things to interact that we can
scarcely predict. As with all technologies, we are limited only by our
imagination.
