# Should Americans have more kids?

*baby bonuses, political divides, and unexpected stats*

## Full text

Hi Friends,

You’re not supposed to play favorites, but this week’s topic is one that has long fascinated me: Why are Americans having fewer children?

And - to be fair - this is far from an American phenomenon:

In South Korea, women now have less than one child each, on average. In Japan, it’s about 1.1 children per woman.

Stop for a minute and think about what this means. Absent immigration, these populations could be cut in half in a few decades.

That would be a stunning development.

But in this week’s show, we talk - mostly - about America, where the fertility rate has fallen from 2.1 children per woman in 2006 to 1.6 children per woman in 2024.

That’s a collapse of more than 20% - in less than two decades. But, as Notre Dame economist Kasey Buckles pointed out to me - it’s not as much of a collapse as it might seem.

[Listen on YouTube, Apple Podcasts, or Spotify]

If you go back 50 years to a time when you might expect that couples were having lots more kids, the birth rate was… drum roll… 1.74 children per woman.

Wait, what?

Maybe 50 years isn’t long enough? How about if we go back to the 1930s, when lots more women stayed at home, gender roles were far more entrenched, and no one had access to modern contraception? How many kids were people having? 5? 6?

Nope. In 1935, people were having 2.1 children, on average.

This week, we’ve got a totally fascinating conversation (at least, to me!) about what we believe about fertility, versus what is true.

[Listen on YouTube, Apple Podcasts, or Spotify]

Buckles - who studies birthrates - talks about the Republican/Democratic divide on this topic, and why today’s ideological divisions between men and women may actually prove to be a barrier to family formation.

Here’s another mind-bending tidbit: Though the percentage of women who are moms has stayed constant, the percentage of men who are dads has fallen considerably. And Buckles talks about the men being left out of parenthood. Why is this happening?

I hope you take a listen, and give me your thoughts. Like I said, this topic is endlessly fascinating to me - and Buckles offered some unexpected insights.

If you want to dive deeper:

Too boring to look at the recent birthrate? How about the birthrate from 1850-1930? I’ve got you covered.

The Trump administration is trying to find ways to boost the birthrate. Pronatalism gets a lot of press. But - according to polling from the Associated Press - most Americans don’t think boosting the birthrate is a high priority.

Gallup finds that Americans want 2.7 children on average. They don’t end up having them.
