# Where did the kids go?

*Why public schools have lost out to private schools and homeschooling*

## Full text

During the pandemic, there was an enormous amount of controversy about when schools would re-open.

Some re-opened in the fall of 2020. Others weren’t fully back in-person until the winter or spring of 2021.

But Thomas Dee assumed that by the following school year (2021-2022), kids would mostly be back at their local public schools.

Dee, a professor at Stanford’s Graduate School of Education, quickly discovered his assumption was wrong. In 2023, he calculated that public school enrollment had cratered “by more than 1.2 million students.”

He told me he struggles to think of any similar drop in American history.

Where did these kids go?

Mostly to private schools and homeschooling, which have seen notable upticks in enrollment. (And keep in mind that this is at a time when the population of children in America is naturally declining, due to a falling fertility rate.)

In this week’s show, Dee explains why more Americans have turned to private and home schools over the last few years. And why this might be fueling a cycle of “school choice” in states, which could further boost private school enrollment.

We also look at the work of two Boston University scholars who examined Massachusetts data to find out who is leaving public schools, and why. Interestingly, Massachusetts also saw a notable uptick in private school enrollment, even though - as a decidedly blue state - it has no school choice.

The BU scholars - Abigail Francis and Joshua Goodman - find that “High-income, White, and Asian students were more likely to leave public schools early in the pandemic and have not returned.” I talk with Dee about whether he sees those patterns throughout the country.

I found this a fascinating conversation - and we look at what could turn things around. Higher teacher pay? A more rigorous curriculum?

If you want to dive deeper:

Thomas Dee wrote about the decline in public school enrollment for The Conversation.

Massachusetts prides itself on its public schools, but enrollment is falling. Particularly in middle school.

The Cato Institute tracks private school enrollment. By 2025, it was still growing, but the years-long “boom,” they noted, “might be ending.”

PBS reports that “Soon, half of all American schoolkids will be able to apply for state money to finance a private education.” But, for many, the path to private school is going to be tougher than they might hope.
