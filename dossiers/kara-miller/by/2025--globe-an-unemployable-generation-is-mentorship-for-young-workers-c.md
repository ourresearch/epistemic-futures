# ‘An unemployable generation’: Is mentorship for young workers collapsing?

## Full text

During his internship last summer at a large financial services company, MBA student Sam Henken felt adrift.

It was a prestigious firm and an exciting opportunity. But when he tried to learn about the core functions of being a product manager — like troubleshooting client issues — “I never felt like I could learn from people,” he said. Everyone was ”so slammed all summer long” that his questions often went unanswered.

“I was a little bit marooned, you know? A little bit alone on an island,” he said.

Henken, 31, said friends who interned at large companies — all in roles focused on knowledge work — tended to feel similarly.

Unemployment among young workers has surged over the last couple of years, rising from just over 6 percent in June 2023 to 9.2 percent in September 2025. While AI is often cited as a cause, experts say a decline in mentorship may be undermining young people’s ability to get and keep jobs.

And Gen Z and young millennial workers must navigate a slate of massive challenges: companies struggling to integrate on-site and off-site employees, an unpredictable political and regulatory environment, and corporate stress over AI cost and strategy.

“If these problems persist,” said Andy Ellis, former chief security officer at Akamai Technologies, “I worry that we’re going to end up with an unemployable generation that never learned the work skills. Then, at the same time, AI is showing up. And it’s going to be easy for people to convince themselves that you can replace these humans — who you never taught how to work — with AI that you don’t have to teach how to work.”

Ellis, the author of the book “1% Leadership,” said “what we’re seeing is, sadly, a return to the chaotic meritocracy of 15 years ago.” He said we’ve rewound to a time before diversity, equity, and inclusion policies. Now, companies “throw everybody into chaos. There is zero support. There’s no organization. And the people who succeed, we promote and advance.”

He worries that misplaced notions about how to measure workers’ performance — like mistakenly valuing “activity rather than output” — has created an extremely challenging landscape for those who have recently entered the workforce: “If you can dedicate all of your time to the employer, you can succeed. And if you can’t, screw you.”

In June, a report from The Harris Poll and Big Brothers Big Sisters of America found two-thirds of adults between 18 and 25 agreed that they were “missing opportunities because [they] don’t have the right guidance,” and more than three-quarters agreed they “have hesitated to ask for mentorship due to discomfort.”

Libby Rodney, chief strategy officer of The Harris Poll, argues that the generation gap has grown more pronounced, postpandemic. “We’ve always had a generational story about [young people]. They’re either self-serving or entitled.” But, she said, older workers have become more “insular” and must shoulder some of the blame. “We’re more comfortable not talking to people or not making that bridge and that connection. This data to me just showcases the societal reasons to strike up conversations.”

If those conversations don’t get started, Rodney worries about “this lost generation of young people who academically have done all the things that they’re supposed to do. They’ve switched their majors a million times to try to get the right job in the job market. . . . And they don’t find that career or that financial stability. That will create a gap in managers in five years, 10 years. It will also create social unrest and distress.”

Employees in the United States now spend considerably less time at the office than they did prepandemic. According to researchers from institutions, including Stanford and Princeton, college-educated workers spend about one-third of their time working from home, an amount that has barely budged over the last two years. (In some countries — including Denmark, Japan, and China — people spend far more time in the office.)

This can mean that on the days when American workers gather in the office, schedules are packed. So there’s less opportunity for young people to connect with those who are more senior. Which doesn’t mean that being fully in-person is the solution — or even that completely remote workplaces are bad — but rather that companies must do more to prioritize and integrate mentorship.

Rodney said she frequently speaks with heads of human resources at large companies, “and they are in a mode where they have to completely restructure their organization in most cases.” She said that sort of “intense pressure” — which comes from factors including tech, economic, and geopolitical disruption — means that “long-term talent strategies have been put a little bit on the back burner.”

Reorganizations — like those at Meta and Salesforce — have made headlines in recent months, underscoring the turmoil and chaos in corporate America.

Meanwhile, Ellis said, in far-flung companies that had once envisioned quarterly full-staff get-togethers, “that plan doesn’t actually happen because it’s a big, visible line item on the budget.”

The problems might run even deeper.

Ruchika Malhotra said we may be witnessing a general “pullback or contraction in empathy,” in part because of digital interactions. And part of that pullback, she says, “is mentorship. At the core of mentorship is you really putting your time and energy into someone else where you’re not getting a direct gain.”

Malhotra, author of the book “Uncompete,” said college-educated women with young children, in particular, have exited the workforce over the last two years. Their labor force participation rate has dipped from nearly 80 percent to just over 77 percent — which, she said, “appears to be the most significant drop over 25 years” — while the labor force participation rate for men with young children has actually increased slightly. One driver of women leaving the workforce has been return-to-office mandates, she said, which can reflect less empathy for women with young kids.

She said all this may have “far-reaching consequences for how much people feel like they can progress and belong in the workplace.” Though large companies may be better equipped to weather a reduction in mentorship, “in small businesses, it’s crushing.” Losing workers and trying to constantly replace them, she said, can be very costly.

And then there’s the workers themselves. “The pandemic has impacted students’ interpersonal skills and the ways people build relationships,” said Monica Parker-James, an associate dean at Boston University’s Questrom School of Business. She said leaders believe mentorship “will happen organically, but it won’t.”

The psychologist Jean M. Twenge said that in 2003, Americans between the ages of 15 and 25 socialized more than 60 minutes a day in-person. Now, people between 15 and 25 socialize in-person about 35 minutes a day, an enormous drop.

Just prior to the pandemic, when he worked in public relations, Henken, the MBA student, said he had a manager who tried to protect him when he made mistakes. And, for him, that manager is still the “gold standard.” He points out that “even now, as I’m going down this divergent career path, I still reach out to him and talk to him.”

For young workers who can figure out a way to connect with more senior workers, mentorship can be enormously rewarding.

Jenny Jordahl, 25, who is based in Cambridge and works at a software firm, said she interned at her current company before graduating from college.

“I had a really amazing intern manager, and she served as a mentor of sorts.” The manager encouraged Jordahl to set up “informational networking calls,” which resulted in 20 to 30 internal calls during the internship. Jordahl learned about a variety of company projects, and asked how more-experienced peers had landed in their current positions.

She believes in the importance of “having people that are in your corner,” and having different types of mentors: “a mentor for guidance, and someone that will advocate for you in different rooms that you might not be in, whether that’s for a promotion or for joining the project.”

Creating those sorts of relationships is key, said Parker-James. She points to a study showing that more than 80 percent of Gen Z workers said a mentor is crucial to their career. But only about 50 percent have one.

“So there is a huge disconnect,” she said, “between what [young people] feel is important and what they’re getting.”
