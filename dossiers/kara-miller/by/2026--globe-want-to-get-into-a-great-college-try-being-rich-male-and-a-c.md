# Want to get into a great college? Try being rich, male, and a champion squash player.

*A 2023 Supreme Court decision barred colleges from considering race in deciding who gets in. But what has resulted is anything but equitable.*

## Full text

Getting into college has never, ever been purely meritocratic.

Indeed, much of the current admissions process — the essay, the recommendations, the extracurriculars — was specifically designed to curb the number of Jewish students in elite schools. In the early 1920s, Harvard President A. Lawrence Lowell debated how to reduce the number of Jews at the university, urging particularly rigorous criteria for “the Hebrew race.”

Decades later, debates over admitting women into Ivy League schools were equally fierce. “Oh save us,” the Yale Daily News wrote in 1956, “from the giggling crowds, the domestic lecture, and the home economics classes of a female infiltration.” Yale did not admit its first female students until the fall of 1969.

But over time, many selective schools have sought to become more equitable — however they define that — and more representative of America.

For much of the last 60 years, the nation’s leaders have tried to move in a similar direction. In 1961, shortly after he was inaugurated, President John F. Kennedy signed an executive order coining the term “affirmative action.” He and Vice President Lyndon Johnson hoped to take stronger action against government contractors who discriminated on the basis of race. In 1969, President Richard Nixon said his goal for contractors was: “to show ‘affirmative action’ to meet the goals of increasing minority employment.”

In higher education, affirmative action eventually became the norm for many schools. Then in 2022, there was an earthquake.

Natasha Warikoo, a sociology professor at Tufts, was wrapping up a book called Is Affirmative Action Fair? when the Supreme Court decided to revisit the issue.

The case — Students for Fair Admissions vs. Harvard — alleged that the school had spent years unfairly discriminating against Asian applicants, rating them lower on “personal qualities” than both white students and non-Asian students of color.

Court filings revealed that Harvard had written internal reports on Asian applicants that, as Harvard Magazine has noted, were “circulated among Harvard’s top administrators” and “concluded the school’s admissions practices have ‘negative effects’ on Asian-American applicants to the College.”

The rejected applicants were represented by Edward Blum, a conservative activist who has recalled the antisemitism his family experienced when he was growing up. “It turns out that the suspicions of Asian-American alumni, students, and applicants were right all along,” SFFA argued. “Harvard today engages in the same kind of discrimination and stereotyping that it used to justify quotas on Jewish applicants in the 1920s and 1930s.”

In her book, Warikoo argues that affirmative action should not be judged on an individual level, but on an institutional level. In essence, admissions becomes “a way for universities to fulfill their missions, which are most often related to contributing to our shared social world.”

And the Supreme Court had long backed affirmative action. So, initially, Warikoo thought it wouldn’t take up the SFFA case, since, in her mind, “there was nothing new” to consider.

But in the fall of 2022, it did. This is going to be big, Warikoo thought. “And it probably is not going to go well if you believe in race-conscious admissions, as I do.”

The Supreme Court announces its rulings on Thursdays in June, and Warikoo waited through four Thursdays in the spring of 2023 without any word on the affirmative action case. When it finally came on the fifth Thursday, the verdict was what she had thought — and feared.

But, of course, the real impact was yet to come. How would admissions offices — especially at highly selective schools — change who they admitted? Warikoo knew it would take years for the data to roll in. Two and a half years later, some of it has. At many highly selective institutions, Black enrollment has plummeted (for data on selective New England colleges, see chart below). At Amherst, Cornell, and Wellesley, for example, the number of Black students has declined more than 30 percent from the years just prior to the Supreme Court ruling.

Hispanic enrollment is more complicated: it has decreased at some schools (MIT) but increased at others (Dartmouth). Asian enrollment has been similarly mixed, increasing at some selective institutions (Columbia) while decreasing at others (Duke). White enrollment — at least at New England schools — is a similarly complex picture.

“We are seeing a resegregation of higher education,” says Anthony Abraham Jack, the faculty director of the Newbury Center at Boston University’s Wheelock College of Education & Human Development.

Though “the Supreme Court case said nothing about on-campus programs” to support students of color, Jack believes that there has been “a removal of resources on the campuses that still do have Black students.” Many schools are simply “obeying in advance, or doing away with things, because they never wanted to do it in the first place.”

When Angel Pérez was the vice president for enrollment and student success at Trinity College in Connecticut from 2015 to 2020, he “had access to a student’s race and demographics,” he notes. “And so we were able to take race into consideration as we thought about diversifying our class.”

Now, what’s particularly challenging, Pérez says, is that “the majority of those institutions would still tell you today that they certainly believe that having a diverse student body is a part of their values, a part of their mission. And so achieving that goal has become a lot more challenging.”

But though race can no longer be considered as part of a student’s application, other sorts of admissions preferences march on, unaffected by the Supreme Court’s ruling.

These preferences create a powerful incentive system that shapes young people’s lives, especially in affluent areas. Warikoo, whose most recent book is Race at the Top: Asian Americans and Whites in Pursuit of the American Dream in Suburban Schools, has written about the enormous amount of money many families spend on the quest for “extracurricular excellence.”

She says highly selective colleges shouldn’t pretend they are “fair, and everybody has an equal chance to get in here, which is clearly not the case.”

In fact, if you are looking to get a leg up in the admissions process, there are lots of ways to tilt the playing field in your favor. Let’s explore some options:

#### Be great at sports

“Recruited athletes have for a long time and still remain in a totally different pool than the rest of an institution’s applicants,” says Emmi Harward, executive director of the Association of College Counselors in Independent Schools.

She says that coaches’ priorities are driven by ever-changing dynamics. “Do you need a goalkeeper this year for women’s soccer? Do you need a [defense specialist] for men’s volleyball?”

In upper-middle class towns, you’ll find large numbers of students pouring time and money into crew, squash, fencing, and whatever else they think might give them an edge. These sports are particularly alluring because — unlike track and basketball, which require little specialized equipment and are therefore available to millions of students — sports with expensive equipment and hard-to-find coaching expertise are likely to be available only to a wealthy few.

“Have we gotten out of hand with how much attention we pay in this country to sports and connect it to higher education?” asks Pérez, who’s now CEO of the National Association for College Admission Counseling. “I think we need to do a lot of soul searching.”

“I still have the trauma of being an admissions dean,” he says. “I had so many challenges with the coaches who wanted me to take this kid. And the development office who wanted me to take that kid.”

If anything drove home the special treatment that admissions offices give athletes, it was the Varsity Blues scandal, which broke in Boston in 2019. The multi-year saga — which ensnared actresses Lori Loughlin and Felicity Huffman, along with a bunch of non-famous people — centered around a private college counselor who, among other things, bribed coaches and college officials to accept his clients’ children as recruited athletes.

But even when the admissions process is untainted by scandal, the privilege given to athletes can still feel scandalous. In part, that’s because privileging great athletes can amount to privileging wealth. “ I know how expensive it is to hire coaches,” says Pérez. “And to be trained in some of these sports, you have to get in them when you’re five or six years old.”

#### Be rich

The sports advantage dovetails nicely with this next advantage: If you’re looking to get a major leg up in the college admissions process, being rich is enormously helpful.

And that enormousness is hard to overstate. The Harvard-based nonprofit Opportunity Insights notes that if you compare kids whose parents earn in the top 1 percent of Americans with kids whose parents earn in the bottom 20 percent, the kids at the top are a whopping “77 times more likely to attend elite colleges and universities.”

In 2017, when a group of economists led by Raj Chetty (then at Stanford, now at Harvard) dug into these statistics, they found that lots of competitive schools — including Duke, MIT, Harvard, Stanford, Yale, Brown, and Dartmouth — had more kids from the top 1 percent of the income distribution than from the bottom 50 percent. Take a minute to wrap your head around that stat.

Of course, money confers advantages in a variety of ways. If your family can donate large sums — a “development” case, in admissions lingo — the college can plow that money into endowed professorships, building maintenance, or even scholarships for needy students. If you fall in the “development” category, count yourself very, very lucky.

But few rich people are that rich. So money mostly tends to be helpful in burnishing a student’s résumé: paying for sports camps, math tutoring, SAT prep. Warikoo found that private college counselors who can offer advice and massage a résumé have become popular in affluent communities.

“ If you play the right sports, if you hire the right tutors, or hire the right people to create your application, your curated profile helps you get in,” says Jack of the Newbury Center. Generational wealth, he argues, “makes affording a $5,000, $10,000 coach for college possible.”

Harward notes that being a “legacy” — the child or grandchild of someone who attended the school — might be helpful, but legacy helps more when there’s money tied to it. “Someone who thinks: ‘My hundred-dollar-a-year donation is going to get my child into my alma mater,’ it never really worked that way.” Even without the potential for huge giving, she says, the admissions office might still flag a legacy application as “context” for the applicant — yet race cannot be similarly considered as contextual information.

#### Be a man

Twenty years ago, Jennifer Delahunty apologized in a New York Times op-ed to the legions of girls she had rejected as dean of admissions and financial aid at Kenyon College in Ohio.

That year, 2006, her daughter Madalyn got waitlisted at one of the schools she had applied to; and, as Delahunty told me, “The professional became personal.”

At Kenyon, Delahunty spent her days waitlisting “what I call ‘a sea of perfect Emilys.’ All these young women who were so accomplished. And yet we had to keep our eye on diversity of all kinds, right?”

Delahunty was acutely aware that neither men nor women want to attend a gender-imbalanced school. And there’s a tipping point, she says, where “you lose your marketability” as a college. But to maintain a gender-balanced class at Kenyon, Delahunty had to reject lots of high-achieving women, and she was mad about it. “I mean, I was one of those Emilys!”

In the last 20 years, the application imbalance has only gotten more pronounced. In 2024, according to The Washington Post, Brown University fielded almost twice as many female applicants as male — though it’s important to note that there are almost even numbers of men and women at Brown.

“Young women have been outperforming young men on test scores and things that are related to admissions for more than a generation,” says Jack. “But colleges have taken an affirmative step to balance off the gender ratio.”

This has been a slow-moving tsunami. In 1970, amongst full-time college students, there were about 40 percent more men than women. By 2025, the numbers had flipped dramatically, with about 30 percent more women than men attending college full time. Delahunty says the pandemic took a trend and supercharged it.

“In the space of just a few decades, girls and women have not just caught up with boys and men in the classroom,” writes Richard Reeves, president of the American Institute for Boys and Men and a senior fellow at Brookings. “They have blown right past them.” That’s true in elementary school. It’s true in high school. And it’s true in college.

“As a former dean, this is so hard,” Pérez says. “Because I would get beat up every year by the community: ‘Why can’t you bring us a more balanced class?’ And every year we ended up with more and more women. Even though we were trying to admit as many men as possible.”

Pérez says he worries the problem will get worse. “I talk to admission deans every day who say: ‘No matter how hard I try, the gender balance just never comes in the way we want it to on the campus.’ And so there are campuses that are now approaching 70, 80 percent female. They’re looking internally and saying: ‘We want to shift this, but the demographics are not working on our side.’”

“The girls have to be not just an ounce better,” Delahunty says. “They have to be a pound better.”

Which brings us back to affirmative action, and the idea that it’s wrong to favor some groups of people over others. That may be — there are compelling arguments to be made both for and against affirmative action — but the notion that the current college process is free of preferences is absurd.

“Affirmative action implies that there is this so-called meritocracy. That doesn’t exist, and never did,” says Harward. She argues that it’s wrong “to remove race-conscious admission from the tools that a vice president for enrollment can use in crafting a class” but to “leave athletic recruiting, development and fund-raising, legacy status,” as well as “trying to balance the class for gender.”

“I actually probably have a really unpopular opinion about this,” says Pérez. “I believe that the beauty of higher education is its diversity and how that institution decides to enroll its class. How that institution uses its admissions process to meet the goals of the mission should be up to them. It should not be up to the federal government.”

Warikoo thinks that as recently as a decade ago, “ Most people thought of college admissions as mostly fair, mostly a meritocracy. And the kids who work the hardest or are the quote-unquote ‘smartest’ are the ones who are gonna be chosen.”

But, she notes, Varsity Blues was a lesson in how the system could be gamed, and how — too often — academics play second (or third) fiddle. States such as California began to sour on colleges giving preference to the children of alumni. (It remains the only state that has banned the practice for private schools, as of last September.) In 2024, a bill was introduced in Massachusetts to ban legacy admissions. It is still being considered. And girls’ distinct disadvantages in admissions have started to attract more attention. Warikoo says this has all disrupted “ the respect or the good faith” that people had in higher education.

Perhaps, if there is some value in that mistrust, it’s this: As long as higher education has existed, the admissions process has only ever been partly about merit. In truth, it’s about so many other factors — some of which students can control, and many of which they can’t.

We have to “ stop believing and then stop telling kids that this is a measure of your self-worth,” Warikoo says. And colleges, too, “need to stop pretending” that this is a system “in which we select the best of the best. Stop advertising the admit rates. Why are you proud that you’re rejecting all these amazing people?”
