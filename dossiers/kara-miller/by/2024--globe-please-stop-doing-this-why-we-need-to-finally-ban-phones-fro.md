# ‘Please, stop doing this.’ Why we need to finally ban phones from schools.

*The evidence overwhelmingly shows that phones are bad for students. Taking them away is the only solution.*

## Full text

Nicole Vernon’s students had to go to the bathroom — a lot. And she thought she knew why.

“You guys,” she finally said to her Spanish classes a couple of years ago. “Everybody’s leaving because you just want to check your phone.”

Vernon has spent more than 15 years as a Spanish teacher, most of them at the public high school in Exeter, New Hampshire.

And the growing exodus to the bathrooms — but really to the smartphones — was, in some ways, not the least bit surprising. “I think a lot of the apps that they’re using are obviously designed to get them hooked,” she says. “There’s a level of competition to constantly be responding and sharing and texting.”

Vernon says that, to her, “It really speaks to the addiction that is technology. And I’m not above it.”

Nor am I. Focusing when your phone is around is hard. It’s hard to read long, complicated things. It was hard to focus long enough to write this piece.

Vernon — who is taking a one-year break from teaching — says she noticed a huge shift when cellphones began to become widespread in school, a little over a decade ago.

In her experience, not every kid wears earbuds, or stares at their phone while walking down the hall. But enough do that they often don’t notice her, or can’t hear her.

Still, the most significant impact of cellphones wasn’t hallway zombies or bathroom loiterers. Instead, says Vernon, it was diminishing attention spans in the classroom.

“The students and what they’re able to do now — and I don’t want this to sound like a slight against the students, because I have wonderful students and very intelligent students — but the capacity for production is lower now than it used to be.” Vernon says her classes simply plow through less of the curriculum than they did before cellphones were common.

This might seem merely anecdotal — Vernon says that she has a reputation as a stickler on cellphones — but much of what she observed is backed up by research.

A new addition to that body of research is a working paper by Ronak Jain and Samuel Stemper, who met as grad students at Harvard — Jain was in the economics department, Stemper was at the Kennedy School. Together, they decided to analyze what happens to in-school performance when students spend more time online.

They looked at the rollout of 3G networks in countries across the world. As Stemper reminded me, 3G — a faster mobile network that enabled internet access on smartphones and other devices — made it much easier to message people or download videos. It spread to many countries between 2006 and 2015, and was a lot more like the contemporary “mobile internet we know.”

Immediately, of course, kids across the world spent a lot more time online. And, more importantly, their test scores dropped, with girls’ scores appearing to suffer more than boys’. (As Surgeon General Vivek Murthy has noted, screens appear to have more adverse effects on the well-being of girls.)

As their metric, Stemper (now an assistant professor at the University of Auckland) and Jain (now an assistant professor at the University of Zurich) used the Programme for International Student Assessment, an exam given to 15-year-olds in countries around the world every three years. On average, when 3G came to town, Jain and Stemper found that the decline in students’ scores equated to losing a quarter of a year of school. It was like students were absent for more than two months — except they hadn’t gone anywhere.

Back in 2013, I had a conversation that forever changed the way I thought about screens. It was with Cliff Nass, a Stanford University professor who studied how humans interact with devices. He also sometimes worked as a consultant for companies trying to optimize worker productivity.

Nass — who died later that year at the age of 55 — knew even then that we were in deep denial about how technology hacks our desires.

He told me that when companies hired him to analyze how employees spent their time, he tended to propose a “20-minute rule.” If workers are going to use email, he said, they should use it in uninterrupted chunks of at least 20 minutes. Then, they should stop checking email for an extended period of time, so they can effectively focus on other work.

Executives thought this was a terrible idea. Devoting 20-minute chunks of time solely to email seemed excessive, they thought. Why couldn’t people do their real work — whether it was accounting or analyzing stocks — and just dip into email for a few minutes at a time?

So, Nass took a more conciliatory approach. “OK,” he’d say, “let’s clock how many minutes you use email a day.” He and his team would track exactly how employees spent their time. As it turned out, they frequently spent upward of three hours on email — but they did it in “little squirts,” as Nass put it.

Now, on average, we check email 77 times a day, according to research by Gloria Mark, a professor at the University of California, Irvine. (Though I’m pretty sure I check it more than that.) And these “little squirts” pull workers away from projects that involve deep thinking: complicated presentations, research, coding.

Over the past decade, mountains of research have underscored the fact that humans feel compelled to check messages — whether they’re in the form of texts, comments on Facebook, or replies to a post on X. We want to know who needs us, who likes us, who is saying bad things about us. And it erodes our ability to concentrate.

It can take more than 20 minutes for your brain to fully reacclimate to the work you were doing before you checked your texts, email, or social media, according to Mark.

I think about Cliff Nass every time we debate whether phones should be allowed in schools. And I think about his advice to company leaders.

If we care about education, if we care about kids learning math or the history of the Civil War, or how to write a decent paper, why would we allow phones in our classrooms? Why would we spend billions of dollars on education, only to mix our carefully-crafted English curricula with Instagram and DraftKings?

Banning phones in schools — as a wave of Boston-area schools have begun to do this fall — is a no-brainer. The question is why we’ve taken so long to get around to it, and whether it’s enough.

---

Last summer, while vacationing north of Quebec City, a TV report caught my eye: The entire province of Quebec was considering banning cellphones in schools.

This felt revolutionary to me. Really? The entire province?

But, according to the newscaster, it wasn’t revolutionary at all. After all, France had banned tablets and phones in schools (for children 3-15) in 2018. Two regions in Spain had banned phones by 2015. The Netherlands had just announced a ban. Italy. China. Great Britain. All had experimented with phone bans.

In fact, it was America that was an outlier. It was Americans who were strange in thinking that under-the-desk texting in trigonometry might not be a problem.

By 2014, more than 60 percent of American kids with phones were sleeping next to them, and more than half of those kids used them after they were supposed to go to sleep, according to one study. Research has since shown that the use of devices in the hour-and-a-half before bedtime reduces the quality of kids’ sleep and makes them more lethargic the next day. Using tech at night, and after lights-out, also appears to be associated with both reduced sleep quality and a higher body mass index. Amazingly, having a device in the room — even if it just sits there unused — reduces kids’ sleep quality, according to a study from King’s College London.

Phillip Done, a former California Teacher of the Year, has written about depressing conversations with third-graders, who spent much of the previous night glued to screens. “Some digital health experts call screens ‘electronic cocaine,’” wrote Done. “And our kids are overdosing.”

Done grew up in Silicon Valley and taught there (Palo Alto Unified School District), and it was not lost on him that those immersed in the business of innovation often eschewed tech when it came to their own kids. They understood that learning and phones mix poorly.

“The Waldorf School of the Peninsula,” he wrote, “one of the most sought-after private schools in the area, bans the use of electronic devices for students under eleven. The school teaches the children of Facebook, Apple, and Google how to cook, knit, and build go-carts instead.”

Kevin Kelly, the founding executive editor of Wired magazine and a prolific tech commentator, has noted that his approach to parenting was pretty traditional. “My three kids grew up without TV,” Kelly wrote in the book What Technology Wants, “and we still don’t have broadcast or cable in our home. I don’t have a laptop or travel with a computer, and I am often the last in my circle to get the latest must-have gadget . . . I see my friends leashed to their vibrating handhelds, but I continue to keep the cornucopia of technology at arm’s length so that I can more easily remember who I am.”

When you think about it, Kelly’s approach is completely logical. Doing anything worthwhile, smart, and in-depth — including writing treatises on technology — requires keeping interruptions to a minimum.

Which is why it’s been heartening to see some American leaders finally start to pay attention to the role of phones in the classroom. In 2023, Florida took the lead, banning cellphones in schools. Indiana and South Carolina, among other states, have now done the same. And in August, California Governor Gavin Newsom called on school leaders in his state to “act now to restrict smartphone use on campus as we begin the new academic year.” New York Governor Kathy Hochul also wants phones out of schools.

But make no mistake: America is late to the party. And for a country that endlessly debates which policies help make school better — How effective is No Child Left Behind? Is the MCAS useful? Should we launch more charter schools? — the elimination of phones from the classroom should be the least controversial, least expensive, least political reform in years.

Oh yeah, and one of the most effective. A range of research from around the world has linked high use of cellphones to “digital stress, low self-esteem, worries and angers, loneliness, anxiety, depression and mood disorders,” as one study from 2022 put it.

In April, the Pew Research Center found that 72 percent of high school teachers felt that phone use was a major problem in their classrooms. About a third of middle school teachers felt the same. But in the United States — unlike in many other countries — decisions about education tend to be made at the state level, from how to regulate phones to what to put in history textbooks.

In Massachusetts, Governor Maura Healey has not called for a cellphone ban in schools, but she should. Doing so would allow local school leaders to focus on implementation, rather than relying on hundreds of individual districts to spend weeks or months hammering out new policies.

---

“I’ve been teaching for twenty years, and I would say the most profound changes I’ve noticed within the last five to eight years,” says Devon Houghton, who teaches elementary school in Plainville. “It’s like night and day.”

Houghton estimates that more than half her fourth-graders now have cellphones, a percentage which seems to grow every year. “I’m seeing — I think we’re all seeing in education — big changes with attention, stamina, perseverance, those sorts of things.”

I talked to Houghton about an hour after I talked to Nicole Vernon, the Spanish teacher in Exeter. And I felt like I was having the same conversation — only one was about 9-year-olds, and one was about 16-year-olds. The exact same concerns came up, unprompted.

And then there’s the issue of bullying, and how it intersects with phones. Houghton says that even in fourth grade, she has seen kids creating TikToks about other kids. She has seen group chats where kids are talking unkindly about their peers. “I just feel that developmentally they’re not ready for it.”

At Houghton’s school, phones cannot come out of backpacks during the day. But the consequences, and the sadness, sometimes bleed into the classroom.

So what do you do?

Vernon believes phones should be out of sight from the beginning of the school day until the end. It’s good to have them stowed away during classes, but it’s also important for kids to acknowledge their peers and teachers in the hallways, and at lunch, rather than being sucked into screens.

But it’s not at all clear that parents want schools to take children’s phones away. Some polling has indicated that parents have come to depend on their child having a phone in hand. Still, most educators I spoke with say they’ve seen little or no pushback from parents when a new, more-restrictive policy has been implemented.

Banning phones outright in high school — taking them away from the start of the day to the end — does not appear to be the route that most schools are taking. Teenagers are going to have to live with phones as college students and as adults, administrators have told me.

This year, the new policy at Exeter High School is that classrooms are a “no-phone zone,” which has been “very positive for teachers,” reports the principal, Michael Monahan. Students slide their phones into cellphone caddies at the start of each class, and they collect them when the class ends.

Houghton says she supports taking phones out of school, but, for her, that’s not quite enough. She thinks kids who don’t have access to smartphones are happier overall. And she believes parents have to be brought into the discussion.

“All parents do the very best that they know how,” she says. “And I think sometimes, it’s just that they don’t understand the dangers. Or they don’t understand the impact this is having on brains.” She argues that we need to share research with parents. “And I think most parents would be flabbergasted and say: ‘Oh my gosh, I don’t want that for my kid.’”

Dr. Carl D. Marci believes it’s just a matter of time before we realize how destructive phones can be for kids. Marci is the chair of the MGH Endowment for the Advancement of Psychotherapy at Massachusetts General Hospital and an assistant professor of psychiatry at Harvard Medical School. He is the author of Rewired: Protecting Your Brain in the Digital Age, a book about how devices are changing our brains.

Think of it this way, he says. The first Model T car rolled out of Henry Ford’s factory in 1908. Cars were — for the first time — accessible and affordable to the masses. But the laws around cars were almost nonexistent. People drove through farmers’ fields. There were no stop signs (the first one was put up in 1915) or electric stoplights or seat belts.

It took us decades to figure out to be safe — or safer — on the road. Drivers now have to be a certain age to drive. They have to take multiple tests. They have to obey signals on the road. They have to wear seat belts.

The danger posed by phones, Marci says, can be “much more subtle” than the danger posed by cars, “but equally pernicious.” Remember, he points out, apps are designed “by people who know brain science better than the teachers teaching it — and know how to get people hooked and form new habits. And that’s going to have consequences.”

Elena Beleno Carney, the upper school dean of students at Bancroft School, a private Pre-K-12 school in Worcester, told me that what she “observes most around the phones in school is [kids’] anxiety level to have to pay attention to two things at one time.” Last year, she notes, one student told her of social media, “I wish someone would just take it away from us.” Bancroft does not allow students to use their phones during class, except in unique circumstances, with permission.

“One of the jobs in teaching is helping students make decisions that will help them,” says Julia Dahlin, who teaches science at Full Circle High School in Somerville. Dahlin points out that diplomas “are a long way away” for younger students. So it can be hard to tell a kid that engaging in a given task will help them in three years, when they’re thinking: My friends are texting me right now!

When Dahlin and I spoke, she had just started a new school year with a new school-wide phone policy: Teachers put students’ phones in a cabinet at the start of each class, and give them back at the end of the period. She expected weeks of pushback from students, but it never materialized. Most everyone acclimated within a day or two.

She said that students now participate more, and they start to dig into classwork faster. Plus, they are less likely to go into the hallway to meet up with friends during class. “There’s nothing to do but work,” she says, adding that she has actually locked her own phone in the cabinet too, along with her students’ phones. “And I actually feel like I’m noticeably doing better.”

Putting phones in a cabinet or box at the start of each period is something that many schools have recently begun doing. In other schools, such as Brockton High School, kids insert their phones into pouches, which are then locked.

Lisa Robbins, the assistant director of the middle school at Dana Hall, a private school in Wellesley, says that students in grades 5 through 8 now put their phones in a lockbox each morning, and get them back at the end of the day. Not having your phone all day means that you’re more likely to have a face-to-face conversation at lunch, rather than on FaceTime. And you’re less likely to see an upsetting text in the hall that weighs on your mind during history class.

But, she emphasizes, “If you’re taking away something, it’s also good to give something.” So they’ve amped up recess with jump ropes, tetherball, chalk, bubbles, and friendship bracelets. She said it’s been wonderful to see how much fun kids are having with each other.

Still, students are rarely initially excited about their phones being taken away, says Jill Walsh, a lecturer at Boston University who has worked with both public and private schools on developing phone policies. But she notes that once a policy is enacted, kids often express relief. “They actually come to appreciate it. And I think it’s a sign that they do need adult help with this.” Indeed, lots of young adults who use social media seem to have misgivings; according to a recent Harris Poll, about half of 18- to 27-year-olds wish TikTok, X, and Snapchat had never been invented.

Economists think of this as a “collective trap” problem, says Stemper. “If everyone in your school uses their phone constantly, and you are the adolescent whose parents don’t buy them a phone — or tells them they can’t take their phone to school — it can be really harmful to your social life.” Schools can “break this cycle,” he says. They can make it so kids aren’t sending or receiving messages during the day, so they’re less likely to feel like they’re missing out.

But even if every governor, and every principal, decided to ban phones tomorrow, there would be complications. Some kids have after-school jobs and need to be able to get texts about their shifts. A parent might need to tell a child that they’ll be late picking them up, or that they should take the bus home. And in a country where school shootings make headlines far too often, people want to be able to get in touch with their children in an emergency.

Houghton has, at least, a partial solution: When kids get to the point where they need a phone, give them the kind that dials numbers, but doesn’t do much else. “Let’s be honest,” she says. “A phone is a computer. We’re putting computers in these kids’ hands.”

When she started teaching two decades ago, Houghton notes, “The rarity was the kiddo who struggled with attention.” Now, she says, that’s the norm, so she teaches material in smaller chunks.

“Some days,” she told me, “I just want to stand on a mountaintop and be like: ‘Please, stop doing this.’”
