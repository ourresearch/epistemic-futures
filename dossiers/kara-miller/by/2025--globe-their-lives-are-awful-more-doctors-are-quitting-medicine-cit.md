# ‘Their lives are awful’: More doctors are quitting medicine, citing burnout and workplace issues

## Full text

There was a time when Amy Fogelman couldn’t imagine leaving medicine.

For nearly ten years, she worked part-time as a primary care physician at Massachusetts General Hospital. She loved her patients. They trusted her. They shared inside jokes. On the side, she helped teach residents and served on committees to fix problems she saw in the system.

But her part-time job demanded more than 40 hours a week. She was meticulous about writing detailed patient notes. She left home at 5:30 in the morning to beat the traffic. “I was exhausted,” she says. “And the commute was killing me.”

So Fogelman switched to a practice in Chestnut Hill, hoping that less time in the car would fix things. It didn’t. “Everything felt like it was about billing,” she says.

Once, she suspected that the wrong billing code was being used for an appointment. The front desk staff assured her that the code was correct, and, when it later turned out to be wrong, she got a disapproving email from her employer for using the wrong code. Another time, she got an angry email from a patient about bills for lab work. He assumed the lab work was included in the annual physical, but that’s not how the system worked.

She hadn’t gone to school to learn about billing or insurance. She had been trained to treat patients. And though she was as careful as she could be, a lot of the billing problems were out of her control. She felt like she was being treated “like a worker in a factory.”

“Why am I doing this?,” Fogelman asked herself. “My husband said: ‘Amy, you don’t have to do this. You can stop.’ And I was like, ‘What else would I do?’”

After mostly stepping away from clinical medicine in 2018, Fogelman started her own business, which she loves. It changed her relationship with her daughters. She felt like she became a nicer person. When she was practicing medicine, she says, " I was like a shell at home. Because I was keeping it all together at work.”

Now, as a small-business owner who, in part, matches doctors with lawyers who need expert advice and witnesses, Fogelman says a lot of physicians reach out to her because they want to leave medicine. “I get emails or messages on Facebook from all over the country. It is really, really, really a lot of people.”

A 2023 study published in the Journal of the American Medical Association found that large numbers of doctors now hope to leave clinical practice. Of the nearly 19,000 physicians surveyed, “approximately one-third reported moderate or greater intention to leave.”

Burnout and lack of professional fulfillment appeared particularly likely to spur that intention. Among specialists, anesthesiologists, pulmonologists, and ER doctors reported high levels of burnout. According to one report, more than 70,000 physicians — over 6 percent of the country’s physicians — left medicine just between 2021 and 2022.

For doctors seeking to enter other industries, there are lots of options, including biotech, consulting, venture capital, medical devices, and pharmaceutical companies. Some even join insurance companies, evaluating the claims that come in from clinicians.

Nisha Mehta, a radiologist who runs the Facebook groups “Physician Side Gigs” and “Physician Community” — which, together, are the most popular physician groups on Facebook — says, in her experience, the most popular destination for doctors exiting clinical practice are jobs in insurance companies.

As someone who hears from lots of doctors seeking to leave medicine, Mehta worries that too few people see how problematic this trend is. “It’s just terrifying,” she says, “when you think about what the workforce will look like. Who will take care of us?”

Karen Leitner, a doctor who now runs her own business coaching female physicians, talks to lots of folks who feel burnt out. “They’re really struggling to figure out how to sustain this career... They want to stay. They love their patients, but their lives are awful and they feel undervalued.”

She says that patients sometimes blame doctors for the rules of insurance companies, believing — wrongly — that doctors have designed the system. “So it’s like managed care and the insurers have forced a wedge in between patients and physicians.”

Ironically, Leitner says, doctors also suffer from corporate efforts to cut costs and increase productivity. “The revenue comes from patient volumes. So when [administrators] start to see that they’re in the red, they’re like: We need to see more patients to make up for this. So we need to make more slots, and we need to see people in the evening.”

After the workday is over, many doctors spend time catching up on patient notes. But Leitner points out that electronic medical records tend to be “clunky and not user-friendly,” making that task even more onerous. Recently, she said, a local hospital opted for a cheaper software option, for which they didn’t have enough technical support. Leitner heard from doctors who said that “not only did it make it impossible for them to do their job, but it was affecting patient care.”

In 2015, ER doctor Jeff Weintraub noticed that his dream job was becoming tougher. At that point, he had been working at a hospital in Connecticut for more than a dozen years. Then, he and his colleagues started to feel their autonomy slipping away.

“Between 2015 and 2020, corporate America was crawling more and more into the everyday practice of medicine,” Weintraub says. “There was more of an emphasis on saving money rather than quality and optimizing the best possible patient care.”

The final straw for Weintraub came during COVID, when his hospital hired a consulting firm that recommended the hospital cut staff and pay, due to lower-than-normal patient volumes. Many doctors, including him, had spent the pandemic caring for people who were sick and dying — sometimes with inadequate gear to protect themselves.

Nevertheless, the hospital listened to the consultants. “They cut the nursing,” says Weintraub. “They cut the support staff. They cut the secretarial staff. They cut the physician staff. They cut our hours.” And when patients returned to the hospital post-COVID, the remaining staff struggled mightily. “We couldn’t handle it. It was completely predictable.”

Weintraub still does a few shifts of emergency medicine each month, but he has mostly transitioned to life as a financial advisor. “I absolutely love it,” he says. “I am back to where I was [in the early days of practicing medicine]. I’m like: This is the greatest job anybody can have.”

Karen Leitner argues that, as dissatisfaction among physicians has grown, some of the best doctors have headed for the exits. “The people who are the most caring, who want to go the extra mile, who feel the most responsible, who want to spend the most time with their patients, are oftentimes the ones that burn out the most,” she says. “That’s why the people who really love and felt called to medicine can’t stay. Because we will never meet the need, and we will kill ourselves trying.”

I asked Leitner what she thinks will happen to medicine, given doctors’ frustrations and already-acute physician shortages. “I think the health care system is going to collapse and have to be rebuilt,” she said. “I’m very afraid for what happens in health care.”
