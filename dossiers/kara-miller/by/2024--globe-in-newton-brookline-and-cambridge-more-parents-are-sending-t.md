# In Newton, Brookline, and Cambridge, more parents are sending their kids to private school. Why?

## Full text

Top-rated public schools are a huge asset for towns. They bump up real estate prices and attract families eager for a high-quality education.

But in three of Boston’s largest suburbs — Brookline, Cambridge, and Newton — the percentage of students attending public school has dropped over the last decade. And a larger proportion of children are opting for private schools.

In 2014, 16 percent of Cambridge students attended private or parochial school. By 2023, it was more than 23 percent. In 2013, 13 percent of children in Brookline attended private school. Now it’s 19 percent.

And in Newton, the percentage rose from 18 percent to more than 22 percent — while at the high school level, nearly 25 percent of Newton students were not enrolled in public school during the 2023-2024 school year.

Meanwhile, at Milton Academy, a private K-12 school in Milton, there has been a 20 percent increase in applications for day student slots since 2013, says head of school Alixe Callen. (The school also accepts boarding students from around the country and world.) In 2024, the school — which was established in 1798 — saw a record number of day student applicants.

Taken together, Cambridge, Brookline, and Newton have almost 30,000 school-age children — nearly half the size of Boston’s school-age population. And more than 1 in 5 kids in those towns now attend private or parochial schools.

So how did we get here?

“COVID made a big impact,” says Carol Kinlan, an educational consultant based in Chestnut Hill. “And we’re still seeing it.”

In September 2020, many private schools brought students back full-time, while many public schools remained remote for much of the fall. Some kids who switched from public to private school never left.

Thomas Dee, a professor at Stanford University’s Graduate School of Education, has chronicled nationwide declines in public school enrollment during the pandemic. And he singles out private school enrollment surges in five states: Massachusetts, Rhode Island, Tennessee, Texas, and Washington.

Post-COVID, Kinlan sees more children with anxiety and organizational issues. Some get help from their local public schools, but others don’t. And she says that private schools are increasingly hiring staff who can help provide extra support to students.

Kinlan also believes that COVID may have led to greater differences in students’ abilities. While some students returned to school considerably behind in math, for example, others took enrichment classes and zoomed ahead. Advanced students might feel especially bored and discouraged — and crave a more challenging environment.

In Brookline and Cambridge, particularly, there have been intense debates about whether to eliminate advanced classes, ranging from English to history to math.

In Cambridge, such debates may have had a notable effect. By the time the 2021 school year began, when almost every school was back in session full-time, just 16 percent of children in Cambridge attended private or parochial schools. Two years later, that number had surged to more than 23 percent.

Across the country, almost half of private schools experienced a rise in enrollment between the 2022-2023 and 2023-2024 school years, while only a quarter saw a decline, according to The Cato Institute.

Local challenges, such as budget cuts or strikes, can lead to “an uptick in interest for private schools,” says Grace Cotter Regan, the president of BC High, an all-boys Catholic school in Boston.

Cara Candal, vice president of policy at the nonprofit ExcelinEd and a Newton resident, notes that parents are seeking small class sizes and personal attention, which can be harder to find at a public school. “In this country, not just in Massachusetts, we’re still operating schools that look a lot like they did in 1976,” she says.

Callen, the head of school at Milton Academy, has worked at both private and public schools — including Acton-Boxborough High School and Needham High School. And she agrees that many families come to Milton seeking small class sizes, along with better assessment.

“If you’re a teacher with 140 kids at the high school level — which some public school teachers have — that’s really, really hard,” she says. “And the quality of teaching in public schools is excellent. I don’t notice a difference [over time]. What I do feel like is that the conditions of teaching are different.”

Candal believes that, to some degree, Massachusetts’ educational lead has shrunk, which is something that parents might hear about in the news. In 2013, for example, Massachusetts fourth-graders significantly outscored their counterparts in Florida on the math section of the National Assessment of Educational Progress. By 2022 — the last year for which data are available — the gap had essentially closed; there was only a one-point difference between the two states on fourth-grade math.

In most states — though not Massachusetts — there is now some type of private school choice program, which can allow children to use credits to help pay for private school. This has prompted public school systems to launch microschools, magnet schools, and themed schools to attract children back, Candal says. She laments that this sort of competition isn’t taking place here.

In fact, she argues, there is more demand for private school in Massachusetts than the numbers reveal. But two factors limit growth. First, many families can’t afford tuition. (Milton gives out more than $14 million a year in financial aid, but the ability to fund scholarships at such a high level is rare.) Second, most schools can’t scale up to meet demand.

Sara Wilson, who runs the Association of Independent Schools in New England, notes that schools are constrained by campuses, buildings, and staff. “It is not easy to add three new fourth-grade classrooms or add a new middle school,” she says.

What’s unclear is how the uptick in private school enrollment will affect Brookline, Newton, and Cambridge. Will it alter community cohesion or commitment to public education?

Certainly, the shift comes at a tough time for public schools. Budgets are being trimmed, and the number of school-age children is shrinking rapidly. Massachusetts has the fourth-lowest fertility rate in the country; between 2010 and 2020, the number of people under 18 in the state fell by almost 17 percent.

Over time, schools will need fewer teachers and fewer school buildings. Every service that caters to children — from schools to tutoring centers to summer camps — will increasingly jostle to fill their slots. Economic competition will be fierce.

This spring, Newton Superintendent Anna Nolin suggested the city consider a limited school-choice program to help boost revenue and enrollment numbers; most incoming students would bring $5,000 from their home district, augmenting the budget. The School Committee ultimately voted against the proposal.

Still, the demand for private schools doesn’t appear to be abating, Kinlan says. “It seems to be going strong,” she says. “It’s just amazing.”
