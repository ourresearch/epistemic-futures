# How marriage divides us

*Can we extricate ourselves from the culture wars?*

## Full text

For most of my childhood, I was parented (very well) by a single mom. So I’ve always been somewhat allergic to people telling me what kinds of families are better than others.

Which is why I first approached economist Melissa Kearney’s book - The Two-Parent Privilege: How Americans Stopped Getting Married and Started Falling Behind (University of Chicago Press) - with skepticism.

And I told her as much when I interviewed her in 2023 for The Boston Globe.

But, as I noted in my Globe piece:

Kearney makes a convincing case that children who grow up in one-parent households are — on average — at a major economic disadvantage.Two incomes, not surprisingly, tend to be greater than one. And two parents tend to have more hours to spend on their children. (Of course, averages are just that — some single parents have plenty of money. Some have plenty of time. And some have both.)

Kearney is keenly aware that marriage isn’t for everyone, and that some marriages are not good. But she’s also aware that - at the macro level - America is increasingly home to two wildly different realities, when it comes to marriage.

[You can see the show here on YouTube, and listen to on Apple Podcasts and Spotify]

In 1980, both richer and poorer children mostly lived with married parents. The average was upwards of 80%. Now, 84% of kids whose moms have college degrees live with married parents. But among kids whose moms don’t have college degrees, less than 60% live with married parents.

The knock-on effects are enormous.

Married families are consolidating their wealth and, especially at the top, lavishing it on their children - a 2007 paper showed that the top 10% of households were pulling away from everyone else, spending more than $6K per kid, while the folks with middle-of-the-road household incomes were spending more than $1K per kid.

Class gaps are being cemented and compounded.

Well-educated families use their dual incomes to move into fancier neighborhoods and send their children to better schools. And if that child is struggling in math or reading, they have the disposable income to get them a tutor. And on it goes.

If you want to dive deeper:

At the end of 2025, the Census reported that “fewer than half (47%) of U.S. households in 2025 were married couples — a significant shift from 50 years earlier, when nearly two-thirds (66%) were.” (Also, “the estimated median age at first marriage increased to 30.8 for men and 28.4 for women, up from ages 23.5 and 21.1, respectively, in 1975.”)

In early 2026 - as I discuss in the new audio version of the show - The Heritage Foundation released a strongly-worded report entitled “Saving America by Saving the Family.” It got a lot of press coverage, such as this piece in Politico.
