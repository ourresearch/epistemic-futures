# Longevity research may just be wrong.

*How do you get to 100?*

## Full text

More than 20 years ago, National Geographic published a blockbuster article.

It was headlined “The Secrets of Long Life,” which was just about as popular as you might imagine. In fact, it would end up being one of the most read pieces in National Geographic’s history.

Perhaps even more striking than the text were prominent, full-page portraits of hundred-year-olds waterskiing, filling up the gas tank, and working the land.

[You can see the show here on YouTube, and listen to on Apple Podcasts and Spotify]

If I’m doing any one of those things when I’m 100, I’ll count it as a huge victory.

For the American public, the article introduced the idea of Blue Zones, places in the world that had stumbled on a magical combination of lifestyle and genetic factors which resulted in ultra-long lives. And if mere mortals could learn from these legions of centenarians, we might hope to become like them.

The Blue Zones became a very lucrative venture for the article’s author, Dan Buettner. You can buy Blue Zone cookbooks, and you can invite Blue Zones speakers to your next conference (a decent number of whom are related to Buettner).

Blue Zones LLC was sold for $78 million, reportedly.

Now, the question is: Was the whole premise bunk?

That’s what a new book called Morbid by Saul Justin Newman (MIT Press) argues.

[You can see the show here on YouTube, and listen to on Apple Podcasts and Spotify]

This week, we tackle Newman’s claim - and what he thinks it tells us about who lives to 100, and why. He also explains why - in his view - the Blue Zones approach includes some very poor recommendations.
