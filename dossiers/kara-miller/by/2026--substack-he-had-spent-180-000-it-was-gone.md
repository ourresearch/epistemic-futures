# He had spent $180,000. It was gone.

*Welcome to the betting states of America.*

## Full text

We tend to ignore the early rumblings of epidemics.

This week’s show isn’t about opioids, but I’ll use it as an example: In just about a decade (1999 to 2010) opioid deaths more than doubled. But it wasn’t until 2016 - by which time the epidemic had spilled over into heroin, leading to another huge enormous spike in deaths - that Congress passed the Comprehensive Addiction and Recovery Act.

On the new It Turns Out, we talk about a trend - and potential epidemic - sweeping through American culture: gambling.

The Journal of the American Medical Association found that sports betting spiked from about $5 billion in 2017 to more than $120 billion in 2023. It’s hard to wrap your head around that level of growth.

But gambling isn’t just for sports anymore. There are plenty of bets placed on meme coins, online stock options, and world events.

On sites like Polymarket, you can wager on just about anything. As I write this, you can bet on:

- When US troops will enter Iran (if they do)

- Who will win the Texas Republican primary

- Where Apple stock will trade at the end of March.

But gambling (or betting or wagering) is changing America.

[Watch the show on YouTube, Apple Podcasts, or Spotify]

Timothy Fong, a Clinical Professor of Psychiatry at UCLA and co-director of the UCLA Gambling Studies Program, told me that it has become normalized, and we’re starting to see profound consequences.

There is a “growing concern,” Fong says, that the number of people who have disordered gambling is rising. “And it’s rising because there are simply more ways to gamble. We learned that from the opioid crisis in the late 90s and early 2000s. You had more opiates out there available. Cheaper, faster, and more potent forms. You develop more addiction. It’s just math.”

To understand how pervasive gambling has become, especially among young people, Fong tells the story of a recent patient - a 19-year-old who had gotten into a good college.

“Very lovely young man,” Fong recalls. He had done some sports betting during high school, with the permission of his parents. And then he “got into college, pledged at a frat where everyone in the frat was betting on sports. And everyone in the frat was also investing into crypto or meme coins... So he kind of rode that wave of peer pressure and got into it. He liked doing it, technologically savvy. You fast forward a year-and-a-half. He had spent $180,000. It was gone.”

That young man had been lucky enough to cobble together a healthy college fund from parents, grandparents, birthday money, and bar mitzvah money.

But Fong says that what can be insidious about gambling is the way in which it preys on people who have very little money, and who hope gambling can help them achieve the American dream.

“I hear a lot why people turn to gambling and risky activities,” he says. They say: “I can’t afford basic things, and I turn to these products as a way of getting discretionary income to be able to buy things.”

When Fong challenges them and asks: “Why don’t you just get a job?” They’ll tell him: “Jobs are too slow. Why should I work a six-to-eight-hour shift to earn $100 and then have $20 taken out for tax? I could make $80 in five seconds.”

Though, often, the reason patients are seeing Dr. Fong is because they didn’t make a raft of money on betting.

Meanwhile, betting companies have spawned a circle of ultra-rich founders, who generally have hundreds of millions - or billions - of dollars.
