# Demographics of scholarly publishing and communication professionals

## Full text

For more than 350 years, scholarly publishing has played a pivotal role in the dissemination of ideas and theories.There is a substantial amount of scholarly research about certain parts of the scholarly publishing community, especially financial and editorial issues.For example, researchers have access to: (1) detailed financial data about many of the scholarly publishing companies active in this sector (Milliot, 2015), (2) lists of high-impact institutions or the pre-eminent journals in certain fields (Thomson Reuters, 2015), and (3) the impact factor of scholarly books in the humanities and the social sciences for the years 2006-2011(Torres-Salinas, Robinson-Garcia, & Delgado Lopez-Cozar, 2012).However, only a few studies have studied the employees of the firms who edit, produce, market, and distribute these important scholarly books and journals (Bookcareers.com, 2013;Carden, 2015;Skillset, 2012;STM, 2014).To better understand the publishers, editors, etc who play a pivotal role in the scholarly publishing process, we conducted an international survey of scholarly publishing employees to collect substantive demographic data about the individuals who are so important in the diverse scholarly publishing ecosystem.

A draft questionnaire was prepared and circulated for review by a number of experts in the scholarly communications field.Eight hundred and twenty-eight usable, anonymous, responses were received from approximately 6,121 society contacts in 33 countries, for a 13.53% response rate.However, not every respondent answered every question on the survey.The raw data from the responses were posted on figshare: http://dx.doi.org/10.6084/m9.

This was not a random survey where every 'n th ' individual was asked to complete the survey.It was a self-selective sample, and an individual who received the survey decided to respond or not respond to the survey.Clearly, this type of web-based survey could generate results that might not be representative of the entire population.We are well aware of the potential methodological problems caused by this type of survey (Duffy, Smith, Terhanian, & Bremer, 2005;Kongsved, Basnov, Holm-Christensen, & Hjollund, 2007).

While the end result of the survey may not be representative of the broader academic publishing community, our endeavour, even with self-selective responses, represents the first international survey of individuals working in this general field.

The largest number of completed questionnaires came from the U.S. (477 questionnaires representing 57.61% of the total).The U.K. was second (237 responses; 28.62%) with a smaller number of responses from Canada (30; 3.62%).Eighty-four completed surveys (10.14%) were received from 30 other countries in Europe, Latin America, Asia and the Pacific Rim, Africa, and the Middle East.See Table 1 for the complete list of these nations and the number of respondents from each country.

As for the gender of the respondents, females submitted 518 questionnaires (62.56%), and males sent in 296 questionnaires (35.75%).A number of individuals (14; 1.69%) declined to indicate gender.It should be noted that some studies have indicated that women are more likely to respond to surveys than men (Smith, 2008); although it is unclear, this may have happened with this questionnaire.

Data were also collected regarding the type of organizations where these individuals worked.Society or non-profit publishers were the largest employers of females ( 225) and males (96) with commercial publishers a distant second for both females (102) and males (75).The survey also revealed that just over half of the respondents worked in the non-profit sector, with 321 (39.43%) at society or other non-profit publications and an additional 92 (11.30%) at university presses.If you include librarians and administrators, the non-profit component reached 442 (54.30%).While this was a useful sample of the members of the four organizations, more questionnaires from commercial scholarly publishers (21.74% of the total respondents) would have been useful.Table 2 shows the relevant data points for the number of respondents who answered this question.

The survey clearly indicated that scholarly book and journal publishing has an exceptionally experienced workforce; and the vast majority of employees (85.07%) have worked in this sector for more than 5 years.While a significant number of individuals (14.93%) entered the scholarly publishing workforce in the last 5 years, the amount of turnover has been rather limited, which poses a problem for firms eager to diversify their workforce.Table 3 shows the data on the workforce.

The ethnicity data were revealing.The vast majority of individuals (90.79%) indicated that they were white/Caucasian.A small number of respondents indicted Asian (2.65%) and mixed/multiple ethnic groups (2.56%), exceeding the tallies for Black African/Caribbean (1.66%) and Hispanic/Latino (0.77%).Some individuals checked the 'Other' category (1.53%), and 46 people declined to indicate ethnicity.Table 4 shows the statistics on this question.

A list of job categories were provided for respondents to select, and the respondents provided information regarding job functions.

Many individuals often performed more than one job at a publishing operation, so the statistical results exceed the 100% mark.Editorial was the largest group of employees, closely followed by management.Marketing and publicity emerged as an important job classification as did production, product/platform management and, interestingly, new ventures.Table 5 lists all of the job functions, although it was not possible to display usable data for females and males performing various job functions here.

Similarly, we found that many individuals also worked on more than one product line.Scholarly journals dominated the list, with 84% of the respondents employed in this sector while scholarly monographs posted a 35% response.A large number of respondents worked on textbooks (22%), with 12% involved in trade books.

The respondents were allowed to provide free-text answers, which provided some intriguing observations about their view of scholarly publishing.These free-text comments were not aggregated into the statistical tables as many of the comments did not fall neatly into a specific question on the survey, but these comments are worth additional analysis if a second international survey is undertaken.

First, the free-text answers revealed that a significant number of respondents were self-employed and worked as freelancers or consultants; and others indicated they worked for associations, vendors and research institutes.Other job functions mentioned included:

research and development (R&D); advocacy; analytics and communications; and consulting.Respondents also mentioned many other content types they worked with, including proceedings; standards; blogs; multimedia; and data.These responses could be mined in follow-up surveys.

Second, there were more individuals with doctoral degrees in editorial (67% of the respondents) and managerial roles.A somewhat higher percentage of women had bachelor and master degrees with a higher percentage of men holding doctoral degrees.

Third, a greater percentage of females than males had been in the profession in the under 15 years of employment category.A greater percentage of non-whites than white individuals were new to the profession.A higher percentage of men (24%) versus women (12%) worked in the technology sector; 25% of the men and 18% of the women were in the new ventures category; 50% of the men and 44% of the women held managerial titles; men moved into managerial roles earlier in their careers than women (who tend to take longer to move up the managerial ladder: Meadows, 2013, August 21;Wilson, 2012); and more women than men worked in production and marketing.
