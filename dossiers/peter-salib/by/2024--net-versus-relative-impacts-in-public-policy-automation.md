# Net versus relative impacts in public policy automation: a conjoint analysis of attitudes of Black Americans

## Full text

## 1 Introduction

Algorithms, especially those leveraging artificial intelligence (AI), have become a growing part of the public policy landscape. Currently, such algorithms are being used to assist judges in making decisions about criminal sentencing (Angwin et al. [2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR1 "Angwin J, Larson J, Kirchner L, Mattu S (2016) ProPublica machine bias—ProPublica. 
                  https://www.propublica.org/article/machine-bias-risk-assessments-in-criminal-sentencing
                  
                ")), automate the process of assessing parking and moving violations, (Hopkins and Sanchez [2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR43 "Hopkins E, Sanchez M (2022) Chicago’s ‘race-neutral’ traffic cameras ticket black and Latino drivers the most. ProPublica. 
                  https://www.propublica.org/article/chicagos-race-neutral-traffic-cameras-ticket-black-and-latino-drivers-the-most
                  
                . Accessed 17 Aug 2023")), help identify situations of gerrymandering (Fifield et al. [2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR25 "Fifield B, Michael H, Imai K, Tarr A (2020) Automated redistricting simulation using Markov chain Monte Carlo. J Comput Graph Stat 29(4):715–728")), evaluate the validity of mail-in ballots (Graham [2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR30 "Graham DA (2020) Signed, sealed, delivered—then discarded. The Atlantic. 
                  https://www.theatlantic.com/ideas/archive/2020/10/signature-matching-is-the-phrenology-of-elections/616790/
                  
                . Accessed 17 Aug 2023.")), assist police in determining where to patrol (Shapiro [2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR72 "Shapiro A (2017) RefGorm predictive policing. Nature 541(7638):458–460")), determine the allocation of public goods (Green and Franklin-Hodge [2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR31 "Green B, Franklin-Hodge J (2020) The smart enough city: putting technology in its place to reclaim our urban future. The MIT Press, Cambridge")), and decrease the administrative load of bureaucrats (Bastian [2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR4 "Bastian M (2023) Tokyo government aims to save at least 10 minutes a day with ChatGPT. THE DECODER. 
                  https://the-decoder.com/tokyo-government-aims-to-save-at-least-10-minutes-a-day-with-chatgpt/
                  
                . Accessed 17 Aug 2023")), among many other applications.

There has, however, been a backlash against this increasing use of algorithms to assist in public decision-making. Advocates and scholars have noted that such algorithms often exhibit biases against minority groups (Benjamin [2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR7 "Benjamin R (2019) Race after technology. Polity Press, Cambridge"); Brayne [2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR10 "Brayne S (2020) Predict and surveil: data, discretion, and the future of policing. Oxford University Press, Oxford"); Cowgill et al. [2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR18 "Cowgill B et al (2020) Biased programmers? Or biased data? A field experiment in operationalizing AI ethics. 
                  https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3615404
                  
                . Accessed 17 Aug 2023"); Hayes et al. [2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR39 "Hayes P, Van De Poel I, Steen M (2020) Algorithms and values in justice and security. AI Soc 35:533–555"); Kılıç and Kahyaoğlu [2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR50 "Kılıç M, Kahyaoğlu SB (eds) (2023) Algorithmic discrimination and ethical perspective of artificial intelligence, 2003 edition, 1st edn. Springer, Singapore"); Lambrecht and Tucker [2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR54 "Lambrecht A, Tucker C (2019) Algorithmic bias? An empirical study of apparent gender-based discrimination in the display of STEM career ads. Manag Sci 65(7):2966–2981"); O’Neil [2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR63 "O’Neil C (2016) Weapons of math destruction: how big data increases inequality and threatens democracy. Reprint edition. Crown"); Sinha et al. [2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR74 "Sinha A et al (2024) Assessing and mitigating bias in artificial intelligence: a review. Recent Adv Comput Sci Commun 16:1–10")). These biases can occur because of biased training data or data that excludes or underrepresents particular groups, the choice of algorithm and threshold, lack of diversity in programmers, and/or feedback mechanisms involved in implementing the algorithm’s results. This scholarship has led some to declare that the use of algorithms in public decision-making is inappropriate (Benjamin [2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR7 "Benjamin R (2019) Race after technology. Polity Press, Cambridge")) because it perpetuates direct and indirect discrimination (Blount [2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR8 "Blount K (2022) Using artificial intelligence to prevent crime: implications for due process and criminal justice. AI Soc. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1007/s00146-022-01513-z
                  
                ")), and, potentially, allows such biases to scale (O’Neil [2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR63 "O’Neil C (2016) Weapons of math destruction: how big data increases inequality and threatens democracy. Reprint edition. Crown"); Varona and Suarez [2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR75 "Varona D, Suarez JL (2023) Social context of the issue of discriminatory algorithmic decision-making systems. AI Soc. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1007/s00146-023-01741-x
                  
                ")).

Studies that have explored these issues further, however, have uncovered a much more complex ethical landscape with regard to algorithm fairness (see Varona et al. [2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR76 "Varona D, Lizama-Mue Y, Suárez JL (2021) Machine learning’s limitations in avoiding automation of bias. AI Soc 36(1):197–203") for an overview of this literature). The well-publicized evaluation of the popular COMPAS algorithm for bail decisions, which is largely credited with bringing this discussion to the forefront of public consciousness, is illustrative. Investigative journalism by _Pro-Publica_ found that the COMPAS algorithm was twice as likely to evaluate Black defendants as high risk for recidivism when they did not go on to commit a subsequent crime (false positive) and twice as likely to evaluate White defendants who did commit a subsequent crime as low risk (false negative) (Angwin et al. [2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR1 "Angwin J, Larson J, Kirchner L, Mattu S (2016) ProPublica machine bias—ProPublica. 
                  https://www.propublica.org/article/machine-bias-risk-assessments-in-criminal-sentencing
                  
                ")). Using the same data, subsequent research found that the COMPAS algorithm was no more accurate, on average, than a survey of participants on Amazon’s MTurk system (Dressel and Farid [2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR20 "Dressel J, Farid H (2018) The accuracy, fairness, and limits of predicting recidivism. Sci Adv 4(1):eaao5580")). In response, the company that makes COMPAS (Northpointe, now owned by equivant) conducted its own analysis of the data and found that predictions were equally accurate for both Black and White defendants (equivant [2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR23 "equivant (2018) Response to ProPublica: demonstrating accuracy equity and predictive parity. equivant. 
                  https://www.equivant.com/response-to-propublica-demonstrating-accuracy-equity-and-predictive-parity/
                  
                . Accessed 17 Aug 2023")). More interesting, however, was the discovery of an impossibility theorem between _Pro-Publica_’s definition of fairness (group-level equality of false positives and false negatives) versus that of the company’s (equal prediction accuracy). It turns out that, except under very specific conditions, it is impossible to achieve both criteria of fairness simultaneously (Hellman [2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR40 "Hellman D (2020) Measuring algorithmic fairness. Va Law Rev 106(4):811–866"); Lagioia et al. [2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR53 "Lagioia F, Rovatti R, Sartori G (2023) Algorithmic fairness through group parities? The case of COMPAS-SAPMOC. AI Soc 38(2):459–478")). To do so would require separate models for Black and White defendants, which would, in theory, violate the legal and ethical tradition of equal treatment under the law (Chesterman [2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR15 "Chesterman S (2022) We, the robots?: Regulating artificial intelligence and the limits of the law. Cambridge University Press, Cambridge"); Fischer et al. [2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR26 "Fischer M, Parab S, GPT 3 (2021) Regulating AI: what everyone needs to know about artificial intelligence and the law. Independently published")). An entire field has emerged within computer and social science to define these trade-offs and understand how algorithms can address different criteria of fairness (Christian [2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR16 "Christian B (2020) The alignment problem: machine learning and human values. W.W. Norton & Company, New York")).

Some proponents of algorithms, however, have taken issue with the shape of this debate. They point out that algorithmic bias is likely not as severe as the bias embedded in status quo conditions by human decision-makers. Further, algorithmic decisions are likely more accurate than those made by humans (Fry [2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR27 "Fry H (2018) Hello world: being human in the age of algorithms, Reprint. W. W. Norton & Company, New York"); Kleinberg et al. [2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR51 "Kleinberg J et al (2018) Human decisions and machine predictions*. Q J Econ 133(1):237–293")), which can increase public trust in the systems that deploy them (Morin-Martel [2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR62 "Morin-Martel A (2023) Machine learning in bail decisions and judges’ trustworthiness. AI Soc. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1007/s00146-023-01673-6
                  
                ")). They also argue that even if algorithms are biased and even if their application would increase such biases, they can still be a net positive for traditionally disadvantaged communities. According to these arguments, algorithms are justified in their use, even under certain strict definitions of justice (e.g., Salib [2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR69 "Salib PN (2023) Abolution by algorithm. Working paper, University of Houston")). There is some evidence that suggests using currently available algorithms could have a massive net impact on marginalized communities, eliminating between 42 and 86% of Terry stops (Goel et al. [2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR29 "Goel S, Rao JM, Shroff R (2016) Precinct or prejudice? Understanding racial disparities in New York City’s stop-and-frisk policy. Ann Appl Stat 10(1):365–394")), freeing 40% to 80% of pre-trial or parole-eligible incarcerated persons (Kleinberg et al. [2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR51 "Kleinberg J et al (2018) Human decisions and machine predictions*. Q J Econ 133(1):237–293"); Laqueur and Copus [2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR55 "Laqueur HS, Copus RW (2022) An algorithmic assessment of parole decisions. J Quanti Criminol. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1007/s10940-022-09563-8
                  
                ")), eradicating nearly all traffic stops (Júnior et al. [2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR46 "Júnior JF et al (2017) Driver behavior profiling: an investigation with different smartphone sensors and machine learning. PLoS ONE 12(4):e0174959"); Li et al. [2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR56 "Li ZL, Wang HX, Zhang YW, Zhao XH (2020) Random forest-based feature selection and detection method for drunk driving recognition. Int J Distrib Sens Netw 16(2):1550147720905234")), and removing police patrols from 50 to 80% of city blocks (Kang and Kang [2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR47 "Kang H-W, Kang H-B (2017) Prediction of crime occurrence from multi-modal data using deep learning. PLoS ONE 12(4):e0176244"); Mohler et al. [2015](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR61 "Mohler GO et al (2015) Randomized controlled field trials of predictive policing. J Am Stat Assoc 110(512):1399–1411")). Theoretically, this de-criminalization could occur without increasing crime rates, by simply removing the need for active police presence or by making forecasts about future offenses more accurate (see e.g., Salib [2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR69 "Salib PN (2023) Abolution by algorithm. Working paper, University of Houston"); Kleinberg et al. [2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR51 "Kleinberg J et al (2018) Human decisions and machine predictions*. Q J Econ 133(1):237–293"); Kang and Kang [2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR47 "Kang H-W, Kang H-B (2017) Prediction of crime occurrence from multi-modal data using deep learning. PLoS ONE 12(4):e0176244")). These predictions imply that even though algorithms may reflect some status quo biases, the net effect would be millions of people in traditionally disadvantaged groups no longer subjected to unnecessary—and potentially dangerous—interactions with law enforcement over the course of a decade (Salib [2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR69 "Salib PN (2023) Abolution by algorithm. Working paper, University of Houston")). Automated traffic and parking enforcement exemplify this potential. When vehicular violations are automated, they tend to result in more tickets generally. Due to inequalities in the transportation system, these tickets tend to be issued disproportionately to minority groups, which not only financially penalizes members of these groups but also infringes upon their mobility rights (Sawhney [2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR70 "Sawhney N (2023) Contestations in urban mobility: rights, risks, and responsibilities for urban AI. AI Soc 38(3):1083–1098")). _Pro-Publica_’s evaluation of Chicago’s red light camera program demonstrated that even a program that randomized the distribution of cameras still produced a disproportionate share of tickets in minority neighborhoods (Hopkins and Sanchez [2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR43 "Hopkins E, Sanchez M (2022) Chicago’s ‘race-neutral’ traffic cameras ticket black and Latino drivers the most. ProPublica. 
                  https://www.propublica.org/article/chicagos-race-neutral-traffic-cameras-ticket-black-and-latino-drivers-the-most
                  
                . Accessed 17 Aug 2023")).

Yet, if the use of such systems can substantially decrease traffic stops, the increase in ticketing disparity might still be justified from the perspective of pure anti-discrimination principles. As Salib ([2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR69 "Salib PN (2023) Abolution by algorithm. Working paper, University of Houston")) shows, a system that substantially reduces the number of people subjected to a costly outcome, like a traffic stop, will also tend to reduce the number of people subjected to costly outcomes because of their race. That is, the total instances of discriminatory conduct can be reduced, even if the racial distribution of tickets is held constant or worsened.

Other normative desiderata should count, too. Traffic stops are among the most dangerous police actions for both the police and drivers, as well as traffic accidents, which disproportionately kill minorities (Raifman and Choma [2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR66 "Raifman MA, Choma EF (2022) Disparities in activity and traffic fatalities by race/ethnicity. Am J Prev Med 63(2):160–167")). The extra safety afforded by automated systems may therefore be considered worth an increase in ticketing disparity. Such arguments about a net decrease in police encounters are especially salient given the negative impacts of entanglement with the criminal justice system in terms of employment, social relationship, mortality, recidivism, child welfare, and a number of other quality of life issues (Dobbie et al. [2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR19 "Dobbie W et al (2018) The intergenerational effects of parental incarceration. 
                  https://www.nber.org/papers/w24186
                  
                . Accessed 17 Aug 2023"); Kao et al. [2014](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR48 "Kao JC et al (2014) Associations between past trauma, current social support, and loneliness in incarcerated populations. Health Justice 2(1):7"); Massoglia and Remster [2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR58 "Massoglia M, Remster B (2019) Linkages between incarceration and health. Public Health Rep 134(1_suppl):8S-14S"); Siennick et al. [2014](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR73 "Siennick SE, Stewart EA, Staff J (2014) Explaining the association between incarceration and divorce. Criminology 52(3):371–398")). Salib ([2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR69 "Salib PN (2023) Abolution by algorithm. Working paper, University of Houston")) makes this argument explicit. Arguing for a “Super Difference Principle,” which draws from both prioritarian (Olsaretti [2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR64 "Olsaretti S (2018) The Oxford handbook of distributive justice. Oxford University Press, Oxford")) and Rawlsian (Rawls [1999](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR68 "Rawls J (1999) A theory of justice, 2nd edn. Belknap Press, Cambridge: An Imprint of Harvard University Press")) concepts of justice, Salib suggests a policy can be justified if: (1) among alternatives, a policy has the highest “floor” of outcomes for the worst off; and (2) it delivers the largest total advantage to those who, under the status quo, were least advantaged. Under this principle, he argues that many policy designs relying on state-of-the-art algorithms as a substitute for human judgment—rather than as an advisory to it—could be justified.

Research has also probed the public’s opinion on these trade-offs. A conjoint experiment fielded on a sample of 9030 Americans finds that the public exhibits a slight preference for humans rather than algorithms to make high-stakes decisions such as pre-trial release (Bansak and Paulson [2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR3 "Bansak K, Paulson E (2023) Public opinion on fairness and efficiency for algorithmic and human decision-makers. OSF Preprints, 19")). Regardless of the pre-trial decision-maker, however, members of the public are willing to forego some racial parity in outcomes in favor of reductions in errors, such as releasing individuals who go on to commit additional crimes and denying bail to individuals who would not recidivate. Other work finds that the public is willing to accept red light cameras, but only if their presence is perceived to result in gains to public safety (Higgins et al. [2011](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR42 "Higgins LM, Douglass Shaw W, Egbendewe-Mondzozo A (2011) Attributes affecting preferences for traffic safety camera programs. Accid Anal Prev 43(3):1042–1048")). These results suggest that members of the public do weigh various factors when deciding whether to support policies that incorporate AI.

What is missing from this important discussion is the unfiltered opinion of those most affected by such debates. Would individuals who identify as Black or African American, who have lived experience being disproportionately disadvantaged by the status quo legal system, support the use of biased algorithms if it meant a reduction in negative outcomes for members of their community? Previous studies have provided reasons for thinking that the answer could be either yes or no. On the one hand, Black and African Americans may prefer the use of these algorithms if they believe their inherent biases do less harm to their communities than the biases embedded in status quo policies. In one survey experiment, Black respondents supported the use of red light cameras more than police officers’ placement at intersections to a greater degree than White respondents when reminded of the lack of minority representation on the police force (Miller and Keiser [2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR60 "Miller SM, Keiser LR (2021) Representative bureaucracy and attitudes toward automated decision making. J Public Admin Res Theory 31(1):150–165")). On the other hand, Black and African Americans may oppose the use of algorithms if they believe their biases are potentially more harmful to their communities than the biases inherent in the status quo. For example, polling finds that 61% of Blacks—compared to 49% of Whites—believe that criminal risk scores generated by AI are unfair to individuals up for parole (Pew Research Center [2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR65 "Pew Research Center (2018) Public attitudes toward computer algorithms. November 16")). Alternatively, Black and African Americans may demonstrate indifference between existing policies that rely on human decision-making and those that rely on AI. One study finds that although Black respondents prefer Black bureaucrats over AI to make government decisions, they were indifferent between White bureaucrats and AI being given decision-making authority (Gaozhao et al. [2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR28 "Gaozhao D, Wright JE, Gainey MK (2023) Bureaucrat or artificial intelligence: people’s preferences and perceptions of government service. Public Manag Rev. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1080/14719037.2022.2160488
                  
                ")). Another finds that Black respondents do not statistically differ from White respondents in their support for police reliance on an AI-based tool that predicts where crime is likely to occur to indicate where to focus officers’ community presence (Schiff et al. [2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR71 "Schiff KJ, Schiff DS, Adams IT, McCrain J, Mourtgos SM (2023) Institutional factors driving citizen perceptions of ai in government: evidence from a survey experiment on policing. Public Admin Rev. 
                  https://doi.org/10.1111/puar.13
                  
                ")). Not only are the results of this research inconsistent, but most of these studies do not purposefully explore the opinions of the Black and African American community. Rather, the analysis of minority opinions is conducted via unrepresentative sub-group analyses or underpowered explorations of heterogeneous treatment effects. Moreover, none of these cases explicitly present Black and African American respondents with the trade-offs they may face when algorithms are actually introduced to public policies that impact their communities. These trade-offs, however, are often highly visible in the debates surrounding the use of AI in local policy (e.g., Beiderman [2002](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR6 "Beiderman M (2002) SAFETY; are red-light cameras aimed at safety or fines? The New York Times. October 23"); Hauser [2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR38 "Hauser C (2019) Texas is latest state to pump the brakes on red-light cameras. The New York Times. June 8"); Metz and Satariano [2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR59 "Metz C, Satariano A (2020) An algorithm that grants freedom, or takes it away. The New York Times. February 6–7")).

In the limited studies that do specifically probe Black and African Americans’ opinions about the trade-offs inherent in policy change, however, the incorporation of AI in new public policies is not covered. One such study examines public attitudes regarding policies intended to abolish, defund, or reform police departments. This research finds that although Black and African Americans show more support for these policies than Whites, they—like Whites—exhibit concerns when cuts in police budgets potentially result in negative consequences for public safety (Vaughn et al. [2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR77 "Vaughn PE, Peyton K, Huber GA (2022) Mass support for proposals to reshape policing depends on the implications for crime and safety. Criminol Public Policy 21(1):125–146")). These findings suggest that Black and African Americans’ policy support depends on multiple factors, including the racial biases embedded in the status quo systems, the racial biases in the proposed policy, and the general consequences of the proposed policy that are unrelated to racial biases.

Indeed, a range of studies suggests that even when there is a net benefit to an action, people often reject outcomes that they see as unfair (Camerer and Thaler [1995](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR13 "Camerer CF, Thaler RH (1995) Anomalies: ultimatums, dictators and manners. J Econ Perspect 9(2):209–219"); Guala and Mittone [2010](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR32 "Guala F, Mittone L (2010) Paradigmatic experiments: the dictator game. J Socio-Econ 39(5):578–584")). Some have even argued that this aversion to unfairness is hard-wired into human evolution, as primate studies have shown that subjects will reject rewards if they see another receiving a more desirable reward (Brosnan and de Waal [2003](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR11 "Brosnan SF, de Waal FBM (2003) Monkeys reject unequal Pay. Nature 425(6955):297–299"), [2004](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR12 "Brosnan SF, de Waal FBM (2004) Fair refusal by capuchin monkeys. Nature 428(6979):140–140")). The use of algorithms makes evaluations of unfair outcomes even more uncertain. Some primate studies have found that, while less desirable rewards are rejected when offered by a human, they are accepted when offered by an automated process, potentially suggesting that the aversion to unfair decisions by humans may not apply to decisions made by algorithms (Engelmann et al. [2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR22 "Engelmann JM, Clift JB, Herrmann E, Tomasello M (2017) Social disappointment explains chimpanzees’ behaviour in the inequity aversion task. Proc R Soc B Biol Sci 284(1861):20171502")). Put simply, despite the now voluminous literature on this topic, we still lack an understanding as to how disadvantaged communities actually view these trade-offs. Inquiry into this perspective is important, both because the implementation of any system in law will likely require some level of public support and because it helps us to understand the practical ethics of those most affected by policies that incorporate algorithms (Lütge et al. [2014](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR57 "Lütge C, Rusch H, Uhl M, Luetge C (2014) Experimental ethics: toward an empirical moral philosophy. Springer, Berlin"); Henin and Le Métayer [2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR41 "Henin C, Le Métayer D (2021) Beyond explainability: justifiability and contestability of algorithmic decision systems. AI Soc 37:1397–1410"); Krijger [2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR52 "Krijger J (2022) Enter the metrics: critical theory and organizational operationalization of AI ethics. AI Soc 37(4):1427–1437")).

This study is a first step to address this lacuna in the literature and to bring public opinion to the fore of these ethical debates. To do so, we conducted two pre-registered experiments, both with a sample that identifies as Black or African American.[Footnote 1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#Fn1) In the first experiment, participants evaluated algorithms for criminal sentencing; in the second experiment, they evaluated programs for automating the issuance of traffic tickets.

Our findings suggest some evidence for the argument that traditionally disadvantaged communities might support the use of algorithms and automated procedures when they decrease negative impacts on their community, even if it results in similar or, sometimes, greater differences in the rate of adverse outcomes relative to another community. This result is, however, bounded, as respondents try to balance the net beneficial effects and the differential in these effects between communities. In the conclusion, we suggest several implications of these findings.

## 2 Sample and methods

We sampled 973 respondents who were identified as Black or African American in the Forthright Access panel. The advantage of Forthright, compared to other online survey panels, is their comparatively thorough vetting and verification process to ensure that respondents are correctly representing themselves and provide quality responses to survey questions (see e.g., Bakker et al. [2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR2 "Bakker BN, Lelkes Y, Malka A (2020) Understanding partisan cue receptivity: tests of predictions from the bounded rationality and expressive utility perspectives. J Polit 82(3):1061–1077"); Bolsen et al. [2015](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR9 "Bolsen T, Druckman JN, Cook FL (2015) Citizens’, scientists’, and policy advisors’ beliefs about global warming. Ann Am Acad Pol Soc Sci 658(1):271–295"); Clifford et al. [2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR17 "Clifford S, Sheagley G, Piston S (2021) Increasing precision without altering treatment effects: repeated measures designs in survey experiments. Am Polit Sci Rev 115(3):1048–1065"); Druckman et al. [2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR21 "Druckman JN et al (2022) (Mis)estimating affective polarization. J Polit 84(2):1106–1117")). Our power analysis, as well as a demographic breakdown of our sample and comparison to the US Census, is available in the Online Appendix.

Based on previous controversies in the literature, our experiments focused on two potential uses of automated systems: pre-trial detention and automated traffic cameras. We selected fields where the trade-offs between net effects and bias could be clearly described, were salient to policy, and, about which we had clear information regarding the reasonable hypothetical scale of these trade-offs. We also tried to select areas where the implications were different to provide some generalizability of our results to other policies. In the first experiment, we asked respondents to evaluate hypothetical pre-trial detention algorithms. Respondents were given the details of a fictional city, with statistics roughly mirroring those of two major U.S. cities (Chicago and Houston). Respondents were told that this city was hoping to reduce the number of people unnecessarily sent to jail by using an algorithm to evaluate the risk that a defendant would commit another crime if released. Status quo numbers were chosen to be easily evaluated by the respondent. The population of the city was set at 1,000,000, of which 80% of the population was White and 20% was Black. For the total population, about 1% of residents would be placed in jail, with Black residents three times more likely to be jailed per capita than their White counterparts. From this baseline scenario, we varied two primary items that would result from the use of an algorithm: (1) the jailed population would be reduced from 5 to 50%, incremented at 5% intervals and (2) the relative incarceration of Black and White residents would range from a decrease of 20% to an increase of 20% by 10% intervals.

From these two variables, a set of four factors about each algorithm were presented to respondents: the total number of people incarcerated; the total number of Black residents incarcerated; the total number of White residents incarcerated, and the difference in incarceration between Black and White residents. For each of these factors, the approximate percent increase or decrease compared to the status quo was presented in parentheses.

We also included the potential change in the crime rate, ranging from a decrease of 15% to an increase of 15% (by 5% intervals) and—based on previous literature—the developer of the algorithm (Northpointe, a private company; the county probation department; the Department of Justice; the University of Cincinnati; or Harvard University) (Kennedy et al. [2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR49 "Kennedy RP, Waggoner PD, Ward MM (2022) Trust in public policy algorithms. J Polit 84(2):1132–1148")). Respondents were given the expected change in crimes committed in percent change from the status quo and the name of who developed the algorithm.

Our second experiment revolved around the use of automated traffic cameras. We once again gave respondents a fictional city with the same easily evaluated numbers that were loosely based on Chicago and Houston. Respondents were told about the dangers of traffic stops and how the city hopes to decrease these stops by using automated enforcement. They were also informed that the use of cameras could increase the number of tickets issued and, because of inequalities in the transportation system, Black residents might see the proportion of tickets they receive increase compared with White residents. Each year, the city’s police department made about 5000 stops and issued 2500 tickets, with Black residents about 1.5 times more likely to be stopped and ticketed. There were three variables for respondents to evaluate: (1) changes in the total number of tickets issued ranging from a 10% decrease to a 70% increase by 20% increments, (2) changes in the total number of traffic stops, ranging from a 20 to 100% difference by 20% intervals, and (3) changes in the relative ticketing between Black and White residents per-capita. The relative proportion of stops was held at 1.5 for all scenarios, unless the automated system eliminated all traffic stops, in which case the difference between stop probabilities became zero.

From these three variables, respondents were presented with a set of eight derived statistics to consider: the total number of stops per year, the total number of tickets per year, the number of stops of White residents, the number of stops of Black residents, the number of tickets issued to White residents, the number of tickets issued to Black residents, the difference in stops of White and Black residents, and the difference in tickets issued to White and Black residents. As before, all these statistics were accompanied by the approximate increase or decrease in the statistic compared with the status quo.

For both the sentencing algorithm and the traffic enforcement automation scenarios, the total number of potential profiles is 125. To evaluate this relatively large number of potential profiles, we utilized a conjoint experiment setup. Conjoint experiments are traditionally associated with market research but have exploded in popularity among social scientists (Hainmueller et al. [2013](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR35 "Hainmueller J, Hopkins DJ, Yamamoto T (2013) Causal inference in conjoint analysis: understanding multidimensional choices via stated preference experiments. Polit Anal 22(1):1–30"), [2015](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR36 "Hainmueller J, Hangartner D, Yamamoto T (2015) Validating vignette and conjoint survey experiments against real-world behavior. Proc Natl Acad Sci 112(8):2395–2400")). Conjoint experiments present respondents with a choice between two potential profiles. Respondents choose which of the randomized profiles they prefer, as well as ranking how attractive they find each profile individually. Figure[1](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#Fig1) shows examples of both conjoint experiments. Each respondent is tasked with rating five different choices, meaning that each respondent rates ten profiles during the experiment for each of our two scenarios. We follow Hainmueller et al.’s (2014) method to randomize the order of the category presentation between respondents to decrease cognitive load and to fully randomize the outcomes of the program within the respondent-choice set.

**Fig.1**

Example conjoint choice treatments. **A** An example from the criminal detention algorithm experiment. All values of the category variables are fully randomized for each algorithm, and the order of presentation is randomized at the beginning of the survey and then consistently presented throughout subsequent choices. **B** An example from the traffic stop experiment. Values and category orders are randomized in the same manner as for the criminal detention experiment

Respondents first select which of the two programs they prefer and then rate how likely they are to support each of the two programs in comparison to the status quo. Diagnostic tests for these experiments are available in the Online Appendix.

From these choices and ratings, the Average Marginal Component Effect (AMCE) can be estimated utilizing standard regression techniques with clustered standard errors to account for the repeated nature of the responses by respondents. The AMCE can be interpreted as the causal effect of each variable in reference to a baseline category, with the other variables held constant. Conjoint experiments have a number of advantages. First, they allow us to evaluate a large number of profiles in a tractable and high-powered manner (Hainmueller et al. [2013](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR35 "Hainmueller J, Hopkins DJ, Yamamoto T (2013) Causal inference in conjoint analysis: understanding multidimensional choices via stated preference experiments. Polit Anal 22(1):1–30")). Second, some evidence suggests that respondents are less susceptible to social desirability biases when answering conjoint choices than when responding to standard experimental vignettes (Horiuchi et al. [2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR44 "Horiuchi Y, Markovich Z, Yamamoto T (2022) Does conjoint analysis mitigate social desirability bias? Polit Anal 30(4):535–549")). Finally, there is also evidence that conjoint experiments exhibit a relatively high degree of external validity to people’s actual preferences (Hainmueller et al. [2015](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR36 "Hainmueller J, Hangartner D, Yamamoto T (2015) Validating vignette and conjoint survey experiments against real-world behavior. Proc Natl Acad Sci 112(8):2395–2400")).

We followed standard practice and code profiles that were chosen as 1 and profiles that were not chosen as 0. We also dichotomized our 7-point scale of preference for each profile compared to the status quo by coding values greater than 4 as 1 and those less than or equal to 4 as 0. We analyzed the results using logistic regression with clustered robust standard errors estimated using pairs cluster bootstrapped estimates (Cameron et al. [2008](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR14 "Cameron AC, Gelbach JB, Miller DL (2008) Bootstrap-based improvements for inference with clustered errors. Rev Econ Stat 90(3):414–427"); Esarey and Menger [2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR24 "Esarey J, Menger A (2019) Practical and effective approaches to dealing with clustered data. Polit Sci Res Methods 7(3):541–559")). Unlike most conjoint experiments, since most of our variables are linear and numeric in nature, we evaluated them in a parametric manner, rather than entering each value as an individual category in a fully non-parametric manner. The trade-off with this design choice is that we will not be able to observe potentially significant nonlinearities, but the parametric model increases our statistical power and also provides potentially more generalizable results under the assumption of a bias-variance trade-off in the model (Hastie et al. [2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR37 "Hastie T, Tibshirani R, Friedman J (2016) The elements of statistical learning: data mining, inference, and prediction, 2nd edn. Springer, New York")). This generalizability is important for both the analysis of potential support for policy profiles discussed below as well as for subsequent public policy analysis. However, given that conjoint models are usually estimated in a fully nonlinear manner and there are some situations where we may expect nonlinearities (detailed below), we pre-registered and reported both a nonlinear analysis and a linear analysis that uses a saturated logit model.[Footnote 2](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#Fn2)

## 3 Results

Figure[2](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#Fig2) shows the results of the sentencing experiment. Figure[2](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#Fig2)A illustrates respondents’ choices between algorithms. Respondents were less likely to choose an algorithm that increased the total number of people jailed. Specifically, each 5% decrease in the number of people jailed resulted in an approximate 1.3% increase in the likelihood of that algorithm being chosen (_p_< 0.001). Across the range presented to respondents, this result was approximately a 12% increase in the likelihood of an algorithm being chosen as the decrease in the number of people jailed moves from 5 to 50%. Respondents were also sensitive to increases and decreases in the ratio of Black to White defendants jailed. For every 10% increase in this ratio, support for an algorithm was reduced by about 3% (_p_< 0.001). This result was, again, about a 12% decrease across the range of the variable. Finally, for change in the crime rate, each 5% increase in the expected crime rate resulted in an approximate 1% decrease in support for that algorithm compared with alternatives. This result was about an 8% swing across the full range of the variable. Contrary to previous results, we found little evidence that the developer had a significant impact on preferences. The Department of Justice was the only actor that showed a significant difference from the private company baseline (_p_< 0.05) and this difference made respondents approximately 3% more likely to support the use of the algorithm. Figure[2](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#Fig2)B shows similar results for the probability that respondents prefer algorithms to the status quo. As with the results in the choice between algorithms, every 5% decrease in the number of people jailed resulted in about a 1% increase in support for the algorithm over the status quo (_p_< 0.001). Every 10% increase in the ratio of Black to White defendants jailed produced about a 2.6% decrease in support for an algorithm (_p_< 0.001). Finally, every 5% increase in the crime rate produced about a 1% decrease in support for using an algorithm (_p_< 0.001).

Using the predicted values from these results, we can see how our respondents view these trade-offs. To do this, we simulated an algorithm that is made by a private company and produces no change in the crime rate. We then plotted the support for the algorithm against the status quo based on the total number of people jailed and the level of disparity between communities. Figure[3](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#Fig3) shows the results of this exercise. We found that respondents do increase their support for using an algorithm over the status quo as the net number of people jailed is decreased, and, as a result, the number of Black individuals incarcerated is concurrently decreased. However, the extent to which the algorithm was supported by a majority of respondents depended on the effect of the disparity between the Black and White communities. When the disparity was decreased by 20%, algorithms that decrease the number jailed by a little over 15% have majority support. When the disparity is decreased by 10%, algorithms that decrease the total number jailed by 30% or more have majority support. When the disparity did not change, however, the decrease in the total number jailed needed to be reduced by 45% or more to get majority support.

Figure[4](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#Fig4) shows the results from our traffic automation scenario. For the change in traffic stops, every 20% decrease in traffic stops resulted in an approximate 4% increase in the likelihood of the algorithm being preferred (_p_< 0.001). Across the range of the variable, this was about a 16% difference between a program that decreases stops by 20% versus one that eliminates traffic stops entirely. The total number of tickets also had a significant effect on preferences (_p_< 0.001), but the size of the impact was somewhat smaller. For every 20% increase in the number of tickets issued, there was about a 3% decrease in support for that program compared with an alternative program. Across the range of the variable, this difference was about 13% from the highest values offered to the lowest. Finally, in terms of the relative ticketing, for each 10% increase in the per-capita ratio of tickets to Black versus White residents, there was about a 2.5% decrease in support for that program relative to the alternatives. This change was a 10% decrease across the full range of values included in this study (Fig.[4](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#Fig4)A). Figure[4](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#Fig4)B shows the results in terms of preference for the current system. For every 20% decrease in traffic stops, there was approximately a 2.4% increase in the algorithm’s preference over the status quo (_p_< 0.001). In terms of the total number of tickets, every 20% increase in the number of tickets issued resulted in about a 2.2% decrease in support against the status quo (_p_< 0.001). Finally, for every 10% increase in the per-capita ratio of tickets to Black versus White residents, there was approximately a 1.6% decrease in support (_p_< 0.001). These effects were generally lower in magnitude than the results that compare algorithms against each other.

Here again, we can use the predicted values from the model looking at the choice versus the status quo to see more clearly how these factors impact preferences in our sample. Figure[5](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#Fig5) shows the result of decreasing the number of traffic stops on the probability that respondents will prefer the algorithm to the status quo, across values of the change in the number of tickets issued and the change in the disparity between communities in tickets. The chart indicates that when tickets are decreased by 10% and the disparity by 20%, a 25% reduction in traffic stops was sufficient to gain majority support. Similarly, if there is no change in the disparity and a 10% drop in the number of tickets, a 43% decrease in stops was sufficient to produce majority support. However, as the number of tickets increased, these preferences shifted. If there is a 10% increase in the number of tickets and no change in the disparity between groups, traffic stops need to decrease by 80% to generate majority support. This finding suggests not only that the sensitivity to net positive effects is conditioned upon the level of disparity, as shown in the incarceration experiment above, but also to other net negative outcomes like the number of fines issued by a particular system.

Overall, these results suggest a more nuanced view of these issues amongst the Black community. While there is some support for the idea that a traditionally disadvantaged community will support biased algorithms if they produce net benefits for the community, they will support these net effects at a much higher level if the disparity is reduced. Moreover, there are multiple effects that need to be considered—if an automated system decreases one type of negative impact, but increases another, it will likely be more difficult to gain the community’s support.

## 4 Nonlinear analysis

While the linear analysis conducted in the last session is consistent with the linearity assumptions scholars often make in analysis and the predictions from such an analysis are useful for policy evaluation, skeptical readers might have issues with such an assumption for several reasons. First, the traditional method for evaluating conjoint experiments is to use a fully nonlinear model, estimated by saturating the model—entering each value as its own dummy variable (Hainmueller and Hopkins [2015](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR34 "Hainmueller J, Hopkins DJ (2015) The hidden american immigration consensus: a conjoint analysis of attitudes toward immigrants. Am J Polit Sci 59(3):529–548")). While often this method is used because treatments are nominal in nature, this approach can also be helpful for identifying discontinuities in effects for continuous variables. Second, the linearity assumption can be tenuous, even for continuous variables, since curvilinear and threshold effects may be more likely in social phenomena than we expect (Beck and Jackman [1998](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR5 "Beck N, Jackman S (1998) Beyond linearity by default: generalized additive models. Am J Polit Sci 42(2):596–627")). Finally, there is a specific reason to believe we might encounter a nonlinearity in the results of the experiment on traffic stops. When the traffic stops are eliminated completely, the bias in the number of stops is also eliminated. This compounding might produce a threshold effect between complete and partial elimination of stops.

Figure[6](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#Fig6) shows the results from the saturated model for pre-trial detention decisions. Figure[6](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#Fig6)A suggests that the results for preferring one algorithm over another were largely linear. Though there was some curvature in terms of the total number of people jailed, as well as a somewhat larger effect for decreasing the disparity by 20% than for subsequent changes, these are relatively minor and largely without substantive interest, especially given that such small perturbations in linearity are unlikely to be replicated in repeated samples (Hastie et al. [2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR37 "Hastie T, Tibshirani R, Friedman J (2016) The elements of statistical learning: data mining, inference, and prediction, 2nd edn. Springer, New York"); Ioannidis [2005](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR45 "Ioannidis JPA (2005) Why most published research findings are false. PLoS Med 2(8):e124")). Figure[6](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#Fig6)B shows that the patterns were also close to linear in most cases, with minor curvatures in changes in the total number of individuals jailed and in a small threshold between the 20% and 10% decrease in disparity. The one exception was the expected crime rate, where there appear to be two thresholds. The first was between a 15% and a 10% decrease, and the second was when moving from no change to an increase in crime. The substantive differences, however, were still quite small, and, therefore, should not be exaggerated in importance (Ioannidis [2005](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR45 "Ioannidis JPA (2005) Why most published research findings are false. PLoS Med 2(8):e124")).

In Fig.[7](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#Fig7)A, we see that, once again, the results were largely linear. Here again, however, the substantive differences were relatively small, suggesting some caution in making too much of these patterns.

In sum, while there were some indications of nonlinearity, none of them were large enough to fully embrace the nonlinear interpretation of the results for extension into policy conclusions. It is quite possible that with a larger range provided of potential outcomes, future studies will be able to verify and extend these patterns such that these nonlinear estimates will prove robust enough for informing decisions. This possibility, however, remains an issue for future work.

## 5 Conclusion

This study brings the opinions of communities usually discussed in the abstract directly into the burgeoning literature on the use of algorithms in public administration and criminal justice. Unlike previous research, we focus exclusively on the multi-dimensional perspectives of the community members most likely to be impacted by the incorporation of algorithms in public policy. We folded in representatives of these voices by asking a large sample of respondents who identify as Black or African Americans to evaluate a range of potential outcomes from the use of algorithmic systems against each other and against the status quo. The results suggest that members of this group have a more nuanced view of algorithm use than is usually provided in the arguments of ethicists, policy advocates, and legal scholars.

Several important conclusions can be drawn from this study. First, the clear comparison between the choice of algorithms and the status quo shows that respondents were willing to tolerate some level of bias in algorithms if they provided net benefits to members of their community, but such support is bounded and is limited by other potential negative outcomes. This result echoes the findings from research that samples the broader public (Bansak and Paulson [2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR3 "Bansak K, Paulson E (2023) Public opinion on fairness and efficiency for algorithmic and human decision-makers. OSF Preprints, 19"); Higgins et al. [2011](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR42 "Higgins LM, Douglass Shaw W, Egbendewe-Mondzozo A (2011) Attributes affecting preferences for traffic safety camera programs. Accid Anal Prev 43(3):1042–1048")). Second, achieving real reductions in racial bias matters to Black and African Americans’ decisions on whether to support policies that incorporate algorithms. Third, the responses in this study suggest that both reductions in bias and improvements in net positive effects are highly salient to communities disproportionately affected by these issues: respondents do not take an absolutist attitude about equality or distributive justice. Finally, the results reveal the importance of other ancillary impacts of the use of algorithms, such as the effects on crime or fines issued. In theory, algorithms can be tuned to address such concerns, but any optimization will—by necessity—need to be multi-dimensional. Without studies like this one, developers will be without crucial insights needed to fine-tune these algorithms effectively. While considerable research has concentrated on debiasing the data used to train algorithms, there has been a comparative dearth of investigation into preferences regarding the calibration of algorithms to produce outcomes that effectively navigate trade-offs, especially among the community members most directly impacted by such policies.

Like any research, this study has limits. Although our experiment presented respondents with a clear evaluation of the AI policy vs. the status quo, in the real world, getting data about the level of disparity in police enforcement is difficult and often subject to manipulation by advocates of particular policies. Members of the public may not necessarily have access to accurate information regarding the trade-offs in these policy outcomes and may need to rely instead on more subjective evaluations of these metrics.

Despite this limitation, results from this study set the table for a wide variety of additional inquiries. We were quite conservative about the power of the experiment, both to ensure we received efficient estimates and to test our linearity assumptions. The results, however, suggest that future research can evaluate these trade-offs in a much broader range of potential outcomes. Subsequent research that focuses on other policies that both incorporate AI and require trade-offs in evaluations can examine predictive policing, risk assessments in child protective services, and determining eligibility for housing assistance. All these policies involve concerns about racial disparities and trade-offs between the benefits and drawbacks for the individuals and communities targeted by these policies. We would expect the members of the communities most impacted by these trade-offs to exhibit similarly sophisticated reasoning when choosing whether to support AI in policy. We might, however, also expect that these views may differ from those found above, as some research has indicated that general views of AI differ between different areas of policy (e.g. where the policy deals with the distribution of benefits, as opposed to the imposition of punishment) (Raviv [2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00146-024-01975-3#ref-CR67 "Raviv S (2023) When do citizens resist using AI algorithms in public policy? SSRN working paper. 
                  https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4328400

In addition, though we provided limited background information to respondents and attempted to avoid normative arguments that might favor one outcome over another, future studies should evaluate how much these results might be affected by framing, partisanship, and differing arguments. Finally, this study was limited to a particular community—chosen because of the disproportionate impact of these decisions on that community. There are, however, a number of other communities, as well as intersectional identities, that are tied in with debates about the use of algorithms. Expanding this research to other communities and allowing comparison between groups would likely yield valuable insights. This broader approach to research would not only enrich our understanding of the impacts of algorithms but also highlight the need for inclusive and equitable policies surrounding their use. By centering the diverse experiences and perspectives of communities most affected by the integration of AI into public policy, we can develop more informed and socially responsible policy frameworks for the future.
