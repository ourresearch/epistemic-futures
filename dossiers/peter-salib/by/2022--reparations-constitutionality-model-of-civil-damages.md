I.
INTRODUCTION
The Tulsa Race Massacre was, as recounted by others in this Symposium, arguably
the worst discrete incident of racial violence in American history.' That is saying
something. From America's Original Sin slavery-to the reign of white supremacist
terror that ended Reconstruction, to the century of Jim Crow, America has much to answer
for.
But how will it answer? One possible answer, discussed elsewhere in this
Symposium and increasingly popular among progressives, is with monetary reparations to
Black Americans. Reparations, of course, cannot undo centuries of white supremacist
violence. But they could function as a first step toward recognition of and atonement to
the millions of Americans who still suffer from that legacy today.
Reparations, however, face a serious legal hurdle. Insofar as they distribute benefits
to individuals on the basis of those individuals' race, they raise Equal Protection

Reflection on the Tulsa Massacre, however, suggests that not all reparations
programs are created equal, constitutionally speaking. It is one thing to make standardized
payments to all Americans of a particular race, in penance for an inexhaustible array of
racial crimes— from slavery to Jim Crow-spanning hundreds of years, and affecting
those alive today in innumerable different ways. Such programs look particularly like the
present-day Constitutional doctrine may forbid, for better or worse, as "positive
discrimination."
But there is another paradigm-constitutionally unproblematic-for distributing
benefits because of race: civil damages for harm suffered because of race. It is no
constitutional violation to award, say, Black job applicants compensation when denied
employment because they are Black? Nor for municipal governments to pay Black
residents for the harm of racially-motivated housing policies. 4
A reparations program for the Tulsa Race Massacre strikes us as being much more
like the latter than the former. In this essay, we explore why. In addition, we suggest
broader implications for other possible reparations policies. When are reparations more
like constitutionally uncontroversial civil damages based on race? Moreover, how might
reparations for a particular atrocity be designed—as a policy matter—to make them more
constitutionally sound?
The answers, grounded in longstanding tort and other civil law principles, may be
surprising. For one thing, constitutionally permissible race-based compensation need not
be based on the absolute certainty that the recipient suffered a race-based harm. On the
contrary, civil liability requires only a preponderance of the evidence. Nor does that
evidence need to be particularized to every compensable individual. Title VII, for example,
permits aggregate, statistical proof of a disparate impact. Compensable harms may
sometimes be inchoate, as in tort cases where the "lost chance" doctrine applies. And

III. REPARATIONS FOR THE RACE MASSACRE
The Government of Oklahoma might begin to atone— partially, imperfectly-for
these heinous acts by issuing reparations. But what might they look like? And which kinds
of reparations programs are likely to avoid unconstitutionality?
Below, we argue that even a fairly generous, broad-based set of reparations to Black
Tulsa for civil rights violations.3' But the court dismissed their claims, ruling that the
statute of limitations had expired. 32 Oklahoma could surely amend that statute to render
timely the claims of survivors, and that move might resemble a kind of reparations
program. We cannot imagine that such an amendment would raise Equal Protection
concerns.
But such reparations would be narrow and woefully inadequate. Notably, this
Symposium marks the Massacre's 100th year anniversary, when so few survivors are still
alive. Moreover, the ability to establish a valid civil rights claim may not be co-extensive
with the moral entitlement to reparations. What about the children of Black Tulsans whose
businesses were burned? What about those whose parents, having witnessed horrific
violence aimed at them on account of their race, suffered from post-traumatic stress and
other psychological disorders? What of the Black Tulsans who were not themselves
beaten, looted, or murdered, but who heard white Tulsa's threat loud and clear? Maybe
they withdrew from public commerce. Maybe they left town, sacrificing community,
opportunity, and investment.
A more universal reparations program could begin to repair such damage. But who
would be entitled to compensation? We can imagine several kinds of designs. One
possibility, following HBO's Watchmen series, is that reparations could be available only
to descendants of victims of the Massacre. 33 "Victim" here could be defined narrowly-
to include, e.g., just those who were injured, looted, or killed. Or it could be defined more
broadly—to include, e.g., all Black citizens of Tulsa in 1921. Reparations could then
extend to descendants of victims, under both the narrow and broad definitions. An
alternative plan might be broader still, making restitution available to Black Tulsans-and
their descendants—who started living in the city only in the years following the Massacre.
Here we focus our attention on the broader plans, which would award funds based
just upon race and a connection to Tulsa around the time of the Massacre. There are two
reasons for this. First, we think that the broader plans would more feasibly disburse
monetary payments to those who may have been impacted by the Tulsa Race Massacre.
Second, we think that the broader plans may raise eyebrows among reparations skeptics,
who might raise constitutional concerns. In contrast, we think that although the narrow

Below, we consider three principal objections to the broader versions of reparations
plans. First, there is the objection that many people affected in some way by the Massacre
did not suffer injuries that the law has traditionally treated as redressable. Second is the
objection that, even if some do have traditionally redressable harms, a program awarding
funds to all Tulsans of a given race is overbroad and thus runs afoul of Equal Protection
rules. Finally, we consider the objection that, as regrettable as the Massacre was, it was
primarily caused by private action, and because of the limited role of government in the
Massacre, reparations from the government are not permitted.
Ultimately, we contend that each of these objections fails with respect to the Tulsa
Race Massacre. In our view, one crucial distinction between reparations for the Tulsa Race
Massacre and reparations for American slavery and racism more generally is the former's
particularity. The Race Massacre involved specific actors who caused concrete injuries to
a cognizable group at a particular time. Such wrongs are generally right-able via legal
doctrines and remedies found in civil rights and tort law. True, the fact pattern of the
Massacre raises complications not present in, for example, a simple automobile accident
or instance of police brutality. But civil rights and tort law have developed specific
doctrines to predictably and effectively deal with these complications.
IV. THE THREE OBJECTIONS AND THREE RESPONSIVE DOCTRINES
A. Identifying Injuries and the "Lost Chance" Doctrine
Even recognizing the true horror of the Tulsa Massacre, one objection arises from a
perception of the Tulsa Massacre as a limited incident, with a limited number of casualties
and destroyed businesses requiring compensation. Consequently, an objector might
contend that a broad reparations program is unnecessary: Those who suffered death,
serious injury, or destroyed property-perhaps, around 1,000 people-have redressable
harms, and they should be compensated if such harm may be proven. Others do not have
a claim to reparations and giving them reparations would be providing them with a
windfall.

understand the full extent of tortious harms. To understand why, we must consider the
"lost chance" doctrine in tort. 35 The "lost chance" theory arose out of the recognition that

A prototypical example of the lost chance theory, from Professor Joseph King's
pathbreaking article on the subject,4l is the case where a doctor negligently fails to
diagnose a patient's cancer, which then becomes a terminal condition. Assume that even
had the cancer been properly identified at the earlier stage, the patient only would have
had a thirty percent chance of surviving the cancer. As observed by King, under the
traditional approach to tort damages, the patient would not recover anything— because this
loss of a thirty percent chance of survival does not seemingly meet the preponderance
standard. 42 The only damages that may be recoverable are on the loss of time of life or on
increased suffering. However, neither may manifest, because it could be that early
detection really would increase just the chance of full recovery, but not necessarily
lengthen or improve the quality of life if that full recovery did not occur. King explains
that the "more rational approach" would compensate the patient for the loss of chance
itself, because "[while the plaintiff here could not prove by a preponderance of the
evidence that he was denied a cure by the defendant's negligence, he could show by a
preponderance that he was deprived of a thirty percent chance of a cure."43 Importantly,
generally for the lost chance doctrine to take foot, there must be some loss suffered by the
plaintiff. 44 The question thereafter is how the defendant's conduct may have impacted the
chances that such loss occurred.

Tulsa Massacre caused a decreased chance of success and prosperity for Black residents
of Tulsa. On the merits, that strikes us as obvious. But, even if not, the loss chance doctrine
lowers the burden for justifying a broad reparations award for the Black residents of Tulsa.
B. Identifying Reparation Recipients and Disparate Impact Liability
Assume that many Black Tulsans-present or past—have traditionally-redressable
injuries stemming from the Massacre. Which Tulsans are they, and what kind of proof
should they be required to produce to be eligible for compensation? One answer is to
require detailed, particularized evidence of a physical injury or missed opportunity. A
more generous program might award compensation based on evidence of just two factors:
race and residence ancestral or personal—in Tulsa.

damages to everyone facially affected by a discriminatory practice, based just on the color
of their skin. Such "disparate impact" liability is available, for example, in Title VII49 and

C. Joint and Several Liability
The final objection we consider here concerns the extent of liability of the
government, and what reparations are consequently owed. Objectors may contend that the
role of the government in the Tulsa Massacre was minor, and that the damage was largely
perpetrated by private actors. As a result, the government is not responsible for the extent
of the damage and should not pay reparations. This is private violence, which demands
repair from private parties.
This too is mistaken. The doctrine of joint and several liability shows why. Joint and
several liability applies principally in tort cases where there are multiple potential
tortfeasors."º Under this doctrine, each defendant found liable is responsible for the whole
of the judgment, or any portion of it, and the victim may seek to recover damages from
any of the liable defendants, regardless of the extent of their comparative liability. 61

V. CONCLUSION
The Tulsa Race Massacre was among the worst discrete incidents of racial violence
in American history. The Massacre was perpetrated with the intention of decimating Black
life, culture, and prosperity. And this was
sanctioned by the government, through
government actors themselves, through providing aid to private actors, and through
culpable omissions in stopping the pogrom.
Accordingly, this brings to mind the aspirational maxim: Ubi Jus Ibi Remedium.70
For every wrong, the law provides a remedy."' Here, the harms were devastating and long-
lasting and for many years ignored. These harms demand reparations. In this essay, we
have argued that a broad reparations program, that uses race as a primary marker of
eligibility for receiving reparations, is justified on constitutional grounds. The key is to
understand the particularized, discrete nature of the wrongful conduct and consequent
harms in the Tulsa Massacre and to apply foundational doctrines in tort and civil rights
law to ground the remedy of broad reparations. In particular, we argue that three of the
doctrines, namely, the lost chance doctrine, disparate impact theory, and joint and several
liability, respectively. We contend that these doctrines provide robust support for the
constitutional viability of broad reparations awards, especially when redressing such
particularized wrongs.
Importantly, we emphasize the proper context for such constitutional analysis of
reparations programs. Here, as with many difficult constitutional issues, multiple
constitutional rights and powers are raised. Often, these live in tension. That is largely
what makes these issues difficult. The question is then how to resolve such tension and
how to justly harmonize these rights and powers. Challenges to reparations programs often
focus on their deficiencies: Are the programs properly tailored? Do the programs provide
windfalls? Do the programs wrongly utilize race? And as shown above, we think that tort
