# Radical Collective Intelligence and the Reimagining of Cognitive Science

## Full text

Abstract

To introduce our special issue How Minds Work: The Collective in the Individual, we propose “radical CI,” a form of collective intelligence, as a new paradigm for cognitive science. Radical CI posits that the representations and processes necessary to perform the cognitive functions that humans perform are collective entities, not encapsulated by any individual. To explain cognitive performance, it appeals to the distribution of cognitive labor on the assumption that the human project runs on countless interactions between locally acting individuals with specialized skills that each retain a small part of the relevant information. Some of the papers in the special issue appeal to radical CI to account for a variety of cognitive phenomena including memory performance, metacognition, belief updating, reasoning, and problem-solving. Other papers focus on the cultural and institutional practices that make radical CI possible.

1 Introduction

Human beings are ignorant (Lupia, 2016 ), biased (Nickerson, 1998 ), emotional (Pessoa, 2008 ), suggestible (Loftus, 2005 ), overconfident (Dunning, 2011 ), poor at assessing their own knowledge (Rozenblit & Keil, 2002 ), and only moderately good at assessing their own abilities (Zell & Krizan, 2014 ). Yet humanity has sent robots to Mars, created antibiotics, built spectacular buildings, sequenced the human genome, and generated a stupefying corpus of cultural works.

How do we do it? One possibility is that these classic patterns of findings are incomplete or flawed and that people's cognitive and emotional capacities have been unfairly maligned. We explore a different reason: Human beings overcome their individual shortcomings by working as a community. Given our extraordinary success from a species perspective, individual cognition should be thought of as a component in a larger system of collective human activity rather than a faculty of a lone agent. The aim of this special issue is to contemplate what we see as a shift in the cognitive sciences toward this view.

The claim that cognition is collective is not new, surfacing in work by Hutchins ( 1991 ), Wegner ( 1987 ), and many others (Banks & Millward, 2000 ; Carley, 1986 ; Clark & Wilkes-Gibbs, 1986 ; Gatewood, 1983 ; Goldman, 1999 ; Hardwig, 1985 ; Hinsz, Tindale, & Vollrath, 1997 ; Larson & Christensen, 1993 ; Mohammed & Dumville, 2001 ). In one sense, it is uncontroversial; obviously, people rely on one another and that is why communication evolved. The more controversial claim that this special issue will explore is that individual cognitive systems are designed to operate in conjunction with other people and that the functions that describe human computation involve multiple individuals, often many.

To make this concrete, consider confidence, a long-studied metacognitive process (Nelson, 1990 ). It has been observed that confidence is predictive of an individual's own performance (Koriat, 2012 ). But confidence can be transmitted from one individual to another (Bang et al., 2017 ) even when experimental manipulations ensure that confidence is unwarranted (Cheng et al., 2021 ). It is hard to make sense of such findings when treating the individual as the unit of analysis. Rather, they suggest that confidence judgments can be recast as processes to generate outputs for conspecifics—to facilitate collective behavior—and some authors have already endorsed this way of understanding metacognition (Shea et al., 2014 ). This special issue asks whether it is time to think about many other cognitive processes in this way. For confidence judgments are not the only features of cognition to display this peculiar property. There is evidence for individual-to-individual transmission of time perception (Conway, 2004 ), risk perception (Scherer & Cho, 2003 ), and sense of understanding (Sloman & Rabb, 2016 ; Rabb, Han, & Sloman, 2021 ). And while it is less focused on particular cognitive processes, the vast literature on social norms shows that people tend to conform to the behavior of the people around them in consequential domains like health even when their lives are at stake (Bruine de Bruin, Parker, Galesic, & Vardavas, 2019 ; Christakis & Fowler, 2013 ; Geana, Rabb, & Sloman, 2021 ).

The consequences of this shift in thinking are substantial. The idea that we wish to reject—that the individual is the final unit of analysis—is not just common in the cognitive and psychological sciences but underlies the broader cultural assumption that people's attitudes and behaviors are determined by reasoning from evidence and arguments. This assumption is manifest in the notion that we can bring about social change through educational campaigns (e.g., Ranney & Clark, 2016 ) or fend off fake news by developing individual critical reasoning skills (Bronstein, Pennycook, Bear, Rand, & Cannon, 2019 ; Lutzke, Drummond, Slovic, & Árvai, 2019 ). And while its analogue in science communication, the so-called deficit model, has been criticized (Simis, Madden, Cacciatore, & Yeo, 2016 ), this assumption lives on in the widely held idea that noncompliance with science's best public health guidelines is due to noncompliers having bad information (Zarocostas, 2020 ). In each of these cases, the interindividual transmission of evidence and arguments is assumed to be the route by which attitudes are formed and actions are determined.

But there is an alternative. One form of collective intelligence (we might call it “radical CI”) asserts that knowledge—the representations as well as the organization and processing of information necessary to perform the functions that human groups perform so well—does not sit in individual heads at all but is rather held by the collective. It takes seriously the idea that cognitive labor is distributed, that the human project runs on countless interactions between locally acting individuals with specialized skills that each retain a small part of the available information.

Looked at one way, this idea is not radical at all but a natural extension of ethology. Many other animals have solved the problems of surviving and thriving without transmitting evidence and arguments. We assume that individual termites do not represent sets of instructions for how their group should go about constructing mounds nor do they make the case to their peers that these instructions are the most sensible approach. Instead, individuals depend on local cues from conspecifics and feedback from the environment, and through these interactions construct their remarkable works (cf. Werfel, Petersen, & Nagpal, 2014 ). Another recent special issue on collective cognition focuses largely on nonhuman animals and robots (see Whiten et al., 2022 ), but if radical CI is true, then it will be profitable to reconsider long-studied human cognitive processes as mechanisms that generate cues for coordination rather than self-contained procedures of reasoning and decision-making.

Drawing further on ethology, we characterize the interactions that afford group behaviors as direct signaling (deliberate calls, speech, and gestures), undirected alignment (spontaneous organization of behaviors through situation feedback or other cue use), or stigmergy (leaving cues for conspecifics in the environment for use at a later time). Human collective behavior is extraordinarily complex and adaptive (Galesic et al., 2023 ), so the aims of these interactions are likely achieved through multiple, context-dependent strategies. In what follows, we consider a number of classic areas of study in cognitive science—memory, metacognition, belief updating, reasoning, and problem-solving—through this lens.

2 Cognitive processes

2.1 Memory

Almost 40 years ago, Wegner, Giuliano, & Hertel ( 1985 ) noted that encoding and retrieval of memories is a group process. For instance, if a group is shown a set of stimuli, people will naturally take responsibility for remembering the subset that is closest to their area of expertise, letting others encode the remaining subsets according to their respective expertise. The intervening years have seen an explosion of work on group memory processes including how group pressures affect individual mnemonic processes (e.g., Coman & Hirst, 2015 ) and how remembering in groups differs from remembering as an individual (e.g., Marion & Thorley, 2016 ). Greeley, Chan, Choi, & Rajaram ( 2024 ) explore a phenomenon of the latter kind. When participants were asked to recall a set of unrelated words that all individuals were shown, the words were recalled in a more organized way after individuals had collaborated on an interim recall than if individuals had recalled independently. Perhaps most telling is that the amount of organization that developed over recall attempts was the same for groups whose members were reconfigured from one attempt to another as it was for groups composed of the same partners, suggesting that the organization belonged to the collective, not to individual group members. Results like this resemble undirected alignment, as participants were neither instructed to nor had any obvious reason to organize their unrelated word lists.

2.2 Metacognition

Although Mahr ( 2024 ) also discusses memory, the substance of his proposal is that a memory-related metacognitive process serves an intra-individual coordination purpose. The claim is that the ability to distinguish whether a given representation is a memory or an imagined simulation arose so that individuals can inform others whether or not some bit of information comes from direct experience and thus has special epistemic status. A key individual behavior in Mahr's account is what he calls “broadcasting,” determining that some piece of information enjoys this epistemic privilege by interpreting metacognitive markers of the underlying representation and then signaling this to others. We might extend Mahr's thought to the urgent question of how collectives determine whether representations they can access (e.g., photos or videos) are episodic “memories” (faithful recordings of the purported events) or “imaginings” (deepfakes or other fabrications; see Nieweglowska, Stellato, & Sloman, 2023 ). Exploring this issue will implicate not just direct signaling (asserting that some photo or video is what it is said to be) but also stigmergy in the form of online traces used to assess source credibility (Liao & Mak, 2019 ; Lin, Spence, & Lachlan, 2016 ; Luo et al., 2022 ).

2.3 Belief updating

Once a speaker has asserted something, a hearer must determine whether to update their beliefs accordingly. Because most human knowledge creation depends on testimony, a hearer's trust in a speaker is critical to human affairs (Rabb, Fernbach & Sloman, 2019 ). Even in cases that Mahr ( 2024 ) considers privileged, where a speaker has directly witnessed what is being described, the hearer must take their word for it. Li, Deangelis, Glaspie, & Koenig ( 2024 ) suggest that parties to testimonial exchanges of this kind enter into an unspoken contract, and children can be remarkably sensitive to the attendant commitments. For instance, young children are more likely to update their beliefs when directly addressed rather than overhearing the same information, even though they remember the information in both cases. They also are more likely to believe unusual claims from in- than outgroup members. The interactions supporting collective knowledge are important enough to influence quite early behaviors.

2.4 Reasoning (motivated and otherwise) in networks

Although we have argued for radical CI, where human knowledge is best characterized as a property of the group, humans do make arguments, offer evidence, and engage in reasoning. Our claim is not that the Enlightenment caricature of the autonomous rational agent is wrong but that it is incomplete, leaving out the most important part. What sways individual reasoners is prior commitments (Washburn & Skitka, 2018 ), quite often their groups’ commitments (Kahan, Peters, Dawson, & Slovic, 2017 ), as much as logical validity and truth of premises. While being swayed in accordance with group commitments is often construed as motivated reasoning, it is hard to see how things could be otherwise when most information comes from testimony and hence depends on trusting one's community.

Fernbach and Bogard ( 2024 ) report on a convention for people who believe the earth is flat. Contrary to the authors’ expectation that convention goers would be intransigent and paranoid, the proceedings consisted of extensive arguments and evidence plus a mood of genuine curiosity. The authors suggest that the trappings of collective knowledge—highly technical descriptions of the appearance of an eclipse from a flat versus round planet, chalkboards full of equations—went some way toward reassuring listeners that speakers’ claims were true. Whether such trappings are best characterized as direct signals or indirect alignment cues is a question for future investigation, but Fernbach and Bogard's observation is revealing because the claim under question—that the earth is flat—is plainly not true. For most scientific claims, the case is not so cut and dried. Indeed, it is sobering to consider the similarity of all communities’ epistemic positions. We doubt the reader will independently verify the claims in this article, instead relying on heuristics like “it was in a high quality peer-reviewed journal, so it was properly vetted.” Even if they attempted verification, they would quickly run into the need to accept testimony (e.g., that a research assistant did X, that the instruments in a laboratory read Y).

A distribution of cognitive labor usually presupposes that the members of the group will be collaborating, not working independently. But this raises the question of how the group is formed. If the group is self-selected, one might expect that members will display homogeneous reasoning because we know that people tend to think and work in insular echo chambers, and this could exacerbate bias. Krebs, Oeberst, and von der Beck ( 2024 ) address this topic by evaluating the ecologically valid content produced by collaborative groups that have accuracy as a primary goal (text written for Wikipedia) as opposed to collaborative groups with more ideological goals (writing text for the right-leaning Conservapedia and left-leaning RationalWiki). The Wikipedia entries were more balanced in their perspectives than the more ideologically driven articles. They also found that, given a choice, people opted to write about topics they had a positive attitude about. People select into groups to do projects consistent with their prior attitudes. We note that other authors have characterized wikis as coordination by stigmergy (Heylighen, 2015 ).

2.5 Problem-solving

Researchers have also studied human groups actively engaged in problem-solving for at least 40 years (e.g., Stasser & Titus, 1985 ). But the growing ease of doing such studies through web recruitment and carefully programmed online designs brings considerable promise for the field. Goldstone, Andrade-Lotero, Hawkins, & Roberts ( 2024 ) discuss experiments that reveal straightforward examples of nondirected alignment; participants were prevented from communicating. In the absence of explicit coordination, participants nonetheless adopted specialized roles to distribute the labor, for instance, by one member of a pair resampling the same locations over multiple rounds of a repeating hidden object task, while the other member samples different locations each round. As the authors note, such specialization is seen in nonhuman animal groups as well (Duarte, Weissing, Pen, & Keller, 2011 ). Goldstone et al.’s framework also emphasizes the importance of group members inferring their fellow members’ intentions and capabilities (again, even absent explicit communication). While they find evidence for this in simple games played by ad hoc groups, it is also documented in studies of real, actively communicating teams. For instance, performance on a naval training simulation is predicted by team members’ ability to anticipate other members’ informational needs (Espevik, Johnsen, & Eid, 2011 ).

Almaatouq, Alsobay, Yin, & Watts ( 2024 ) report results that challenge the radical CI concept. They administered a carefully controlled group task and did not prevent participants from communicating to perform it. They found that individual skill on a problem-solving task was the best predictor of group success on other instances of the same task relative to skill diversity and other group properties. This may seem unsurprising, but it is inconsistent with well-known modeling results (Hong & Page, 2004 ) and prior data (Woolley, Chabris, Pentland, Hashmi, & Malone, 2010 ). Nevertheless, the volume of explicit communication (number of chat messages sent) was associated with reduced efficiency and longer time to completion, but the frequency of turn-taking showed the opposite relations with both efficiency and time. In other words, groups that took more turns worked more efficiently, so it could be that undirected alignment (responding to information received from merely observing others) made coordination easier than direct signaling did.

3 How collectives develop and sustain radical CI

The radical CI view is buttressed by recognizing the power of cultural evolution to provide systems for sharing and deploying information, such as institutions for learning and discovery, and tools for processing information, including both methods (like reading, writing, and arithmetic) and content (like scientific theories and cultural narratives). To the extent we can account for behavior that seems intelligent in terms of culturally acquired concepts, we do not need to appeal to individual characteristics. Henrich and Muthukrishna ( 2024 ) show how cultural evolution has accelerated humans’ ability to process information effectively. One of their critical points concerns multiple discovery, the phenomenon of major knowledge breakthroughs being achieved by multiple parties working independently (e.g., calculus). The frequency of such events is easy to explain under a radical CI account. Major breakthroughs are enabled by the right cultural conditions and by other thinkers who have provided the concepts, data, tools, and questions that make the breakthrough possible.

One aspect of cultural evolution that makes progress possible is the development of cultural institutions (Lupia, 2024 ). Elections, the legal system, and practices for effective negotiation between governments and between government and private citizens have all resulted from centuries of trial and error informed by scientific study and experiential feedback. More importantly for present purposes, they are designed to overcome individual limitations by exploiting the collective nature of behavior. For instance, Lupia agrees with many other political scientists in suggesting that partisan cues are frequently valid cues. Knowing a candidate is in one's group gives a reasonably accurate sense that their future behaviors will be consistent with group priorities. Moreover, systems are in place (such as U.S. congressional committee assignments) to guarantee appropriate action by punishing elected officials who violate expectations too flagrantly. Thus, party membership provides the voter with a decent shortcut in lieu of a cognitively demanding assessment of every candidate's position. While far from perfect, these institutions have provided the means for humans to collaborate with less violence than there otherwise would be to harness the power of collective thought and knowledge.

4 Conclusion

This special issue captures the energy of cognitive science and its near neighbors in the study of mind and behavior at an inflection point. The idea that human cognition is collective, so long posited (Banks & Millward, 2000 ; Carley, 1986 ; Clark & Wilkes-Gibbs, 1986 ; Hinsz et al., 1997 ; Hutchins, 1991 ; Larson & Christensen, 1993 ; Mohammed & Dumville, 2001 ; Sloman & Fernbach, 2017 ; Wegner, 1987 ), is now being investigated with the kind of systematicity afforded by a critical mass of interest and exciting new possibilities for measurement. The interest is reflected in a simple keyword search on Web of Science. The phrase “collective cognition” returns two article hits in 1990 and increases nearly every year until reaching 213 in 2022, with “group cognition” following the same pattern but with many more hits. In the spirit of multiple discoveries, we also note two other concurrent special issues on the theme, one focusing on animal behavior (Whiten et al., 2022 ) and another on implications for the public good (Garcia, Galesic, & Olsson, in press ). Our volume has focused on familiar cognitive processes like memory and problem-solving. Rather than throwing out decades of work on these areas of individual cognition, we believe this work can be reevaluated as pieces in a larger puzzle of group coordination.

Regarding new possibilities for measurement, studies of behaving human groups have and will continue to benefit from the comparative ease of combining computer tasks and online recruitment (relative to bringing ad hoc groups of undergraduates into physical lab spaces). Studies of this kind might increasingly borrow methods and hypotheses from work on nonhuman animals. For example, some collective human and nonhuman animal behaviors elicit matching behavior in conspecifics (confidence judgment transmission and firefly synchrony, respectively), while others use sophisticated differentiation (role specialization). For highly flexible animals like humans, it makes sense to ask which environmental or task constraints dictate which coordination strategy groups will deploy and what cues facilitate them. In addition, computational modeling of collective knowledge is an understudied area that could benefit from simulations of the kind that have garnered remarkable insights on network structure and information spread (see Momennejad, 2022 ). What is more, one bright side of humanity's shift to life online is that naturally behaving humans leave ecologically valid records of their behavior (e.g., wikis) that are ripe for extended field study.

Many dark sides of the shift are well documented and have garnered considerable alarm in the research community. Henrich and Muthukrishna ( 2024 ) observe that more interconnectedness is not always better—excessive connectivity hinders cultural innovation because suboptimal ideas may receive enough attention to outperform optimal but underdeveloped ideas. They pointedly note that electric cars were invented at the dawn of automotive engineering but were quickly displaced by Ford's combustion engines. More generally, the current pace of technological change certainly exceeds the pace at which human minds change under evolutionary pressures (Marsh & Rajaram, 2019 ). Whether it will exceed the human capacity to collectively adapt is an open question. Humanity has sent robots to Mars, created spectacular buildings, and all the rest. We hope that this groundswell of effort to study the human system itself can help mitigate the shocks to come.
