# How Do We Believe?

## Full text

## Abstract

My first 30-odd years of research in cognitive science has been driven by an attempt to balance two facts about human thought that seem incompatible and two corresponding ways of understanding information processing.The facts are that, on one hand, human memories serve as sophisticated pattern recognition devices with great flexibility and an ability to generalize and predict as long as circumstances remain sufficiently familiar.On the other hand, we are capable of deploying an enormous variety of representational schemes that map closely onto articulable structure in the world and that support explanation even in unfamiliar circumstances.The contrasting ways of modeling such processes involve, first, more and more sophisticated associative models that capture progressively higher-order statistical structure and, second, more powerful representational languages for other sorts of structure, especially compositional and causal structure.My efforts to rectify these forces have taken me from the study of memory to induction and category knowledge to causal reasoning.In the process, I have consistently appealed to dual systems of thinking.I have come to realize that a key reason for our success as cognizers is that we rely on others for most of our information processing needs; we live in a community of knowledge.We make use of others both intuitively-by outsourcing much of our thinking without knowing we are doing it-and by deliberating with others.

renowned memory researcher Endel Tulving as an undergraduate at the University of Toronto, I felt like I had come home.

Prof. Tulving was not a fan of cognitive science.My sense was that it was because cognitive science had not found an effective way to talk about the phenomenal quality of conscious experience.It still has not.Tulving's most enduring contribution to psychological theory is the distinction between episodic and semantic memory, memory for one's personal experience versus memory for impersonal facts about the world (Tulving, 1983).Fundamentally, the distinction rests on phenomenal experience: Remembering an experience you have had feels different than memory for something impersonal, like the history of the '60s (assuming you were not there).There is certainly evidence for the distinction-behavioral evidence from list-learning studies and neuropsychological evidence from the study of amnesia-but the inspiration and strongest motivation for the distinction remains the intuition that the two kinds of memory have different sources, lead to different experiences, and have different effects (for instance, episodic memories shape one's personal identity; semantic memories contribute but they are more generic).My own attitude is that this is cognitive science at its best: Using multiple and varied sources of evidence (including phenomenal experience) to make a claim that is both important and has empirical content.

Nevertheless, these kinds of introspections are sometimes pooh-poohed in cognitive science.Science does not rest on intuition, the argument goes; science involves rigorous formal models that fit the data.Such models can show the fallibility of intuition.For instance, McKoon, Ratcliff, and Dell (1986) argued that the semantic/episodic distinction was not well enough specified to support meaningful progress in the study of memory.Early on, I believed that one has to choose whether one wants to study questions of phenomenal experience or cognitive science.I chose cognitive science; at least, I thought I had.But what I have discovered over the years is that introspective experience is the richest source of insight into the workings of human cognition.To be sure, hypotheses only become credible if they also obtain support from rigorous empirical studies and prove conceptually coherent, and computational modeling is a great tool to achieve both.But introspection is a form of evidence.More important, introspection is the primary source of good ideas about human thought, like the episodic/semantic distinction or other dual system distinctions to be discussed.Other sources of ideas-like hypotheses that derive from the elegance of a computational model-do not have as much prima facie plausibility because they do not derive from the system under study.Introspections about cognition might be misleading, but at least they come from cognition.Hypotheses motivated by theory can be misleading too, and their motivating theory might be completely unrelated to the workings of cognition.

I was left frustrated by the study of memory.We have known since the early '70s that the greatest driver of memory performance is understanding ("depth of processing" according to Craik & Lockhart, 1972).But how do people understand?This has been my motivating question ever since: How do people process information so as to develop the beliefs, knowledge, and attitudes that guide their memory and action?The answers that I have come to have changed quite dramatically over the years.

### The problem of induction

I went to graduate school in the '80s, the era of the connectionist (parallel-distributed processing) revolution in cognitive science.This is the idea that, instead of thinking of the mind as a computer with a powerful central processor, we should think of intelligence as emerging from the activity of billions of relatively stupid processors hooked up in a rich network, in analogy to how the brain works.I arrived at Stanford just before one of the fathers of connectionism, Dave Rumelhart.By the time he arrived, I had already had a significant dose of Amos Tversky, a grandmaster in the study of judgment and decision making.My dream was to marry the two approaches.I tried in my dissertation.I developed a very simple connectionist interactive activation model that tried to capture my conclusion that people got stuck in mental sets when generating reasons regarding a social issue: The initial framing of an issue mattered more than later framings.In particular, when generating reasons on both sides of an issue, the first set generated had more influence on subsequent attitudes than the second set.This was an initial foray, not terribly compelling in the end either to me or (as I later learned) to my dissertation committee.

My next attempt to marry connectionist approaches with issues of judgment was more successful.As a post-doc, I developed a very simple connectionist model that was able to explain about 13 phenomena of inductive reasoning (Sloman, 1993).These phenomena concerned the induction of properties from one category to another, a paradigm developed by Osherson et al. (1990).For example, people think that it is more likely that all members of a superordinate category have an unfamiliar property after learning that a typical member does.To illustrate, people judge it more likely that all mammals have an ulnar artery if told that all dogs (typical mammals) do as opposed to learning that all weasels (atypical mammals) do.After developing the model, I realized it was simple enough that it was best described mathematically; the connectionist aspect was secondary.The basic idea involved projecting one vector onto another: People considered an argument consisting of premises and a conclusion to be strong in proportion to the magnitude of the projection of the premises onto the conclusion.This notion of projection has gained some traction 20 years later: It is at the heart of an approach to modeling judgment under uncertainty that is inspired by physics, quantum probability theory (Busemeyer & Bruza, 2012).

Despite the success of my simple model, its limitations quickly became apparent.Like many models in cognitive science, its representations lacked structure (a point made most famously at the time by Fodor & Pylyshyn, 1988).It could not, for instance, satisfactorily capture the different inferences supported by the statements "chickens are animals" and "chickens are delicious."The model had no way of identifying the very different features of chickens that matter in the two cases, never mind the different relational structures and background knowledge.

I made a little progress on this problem by addressing an easier question: Why are different properties of a concept more or less important (Sloman et al., 1998)?There is wide agreement, for instance, that having muscles is more central to being a cheetah than having spots, even though spots may be more important for identifying cheetahs.Our attempt to explain the source of intuitions about importance came in the form of another connectionist model whose basic idea was that features of a concept are central to the degree that other features depend on them.The model was also mathematically simple (it was linear) and, like my earlier model, fit a variety of data, this time about judgments of mutability and other forms of counterfactual reasoning.I discovered later that the model we developed independently was based on the same idea as Google's original PageRank search algorithm.

### Dual systems

An offhand comment by the editor of my 1993 paper suggested a framework for thinking about the kind of automatic quick inferences that I had been studying and the richer kind of structural thought that I had come to worry about, a framework that served as a starting point to analyze the contributions to thinking of stored knowledge, on one hand, and more constructive forms of understanding, on the other hand.This led to my paper The Empirical Case for Two Systems of Reasoning (Sloman, 1996), where I argued that associative and rule-based reasoning were underwritten by two distinct cognitive systems operating in parallel, a position similar to that of Epstein et al. (1996).The idea was obviously in the air as other dual system distinctions were published around the same time (Evans & Over, 1996;Stanovich & West, 1998) though they conceived of the systems as sequential: a fast associative process followed by a slow analytic one.These papers predate Daniel Kahneman's (2011) book Thinking Fast and Slow-an argument for the sequential distinction-by 15 years.De Neys and Glumicic (2008) work supports the parallel view over the sequential one.

Evidence for the distinction has emerged from all directions.Motivated by work in anthropology, Sperber (1997) argued for a distinction between intuitive and reflective belief.My motivation for the distinction was to resolve a debate that had begun in the early 20th century and remains alive today in cognitive science and artificial intelligence (AI).Today, the debate is often framed in terms of the relative merits of predictive accuracy versus explanatory power (Lake et al., 2017).Does understanding a phenomenon entail a model that offers an explanatory structure, a description of the structure or process that generated the phenomenon?Or, is it good enough to just accurately predict when the phenomenon will arise and when it will not?The modern distinction is motivated by the fact that we now have deep learning models based on connectionist principles and learning algorithms that have extraordinary predictive power by harnessing billions of parameters and encoding huge amounts of data but generally leave the reason for any particular prediction mysterious.Explanatory models that try to represent the environment with logic, probability, causal models, or compositional structure simply do not do as well at prediction but at least leave an account of how and why they produced the output that they did.

The issue parallels my youthful concern about the role of memory versus what I referred to above as "understanding": Memory is to prediction as understanding is to explanation.Prediction without explanation always makes use of some kind of very high-dimensional statistical representation of input data.This is also a good way to model memory: a system that summarizes experience in such a way that it can be employed to complete patterns in familiar ways.This is the basic idea of Endel Tulving's second most important contribution to the study of memory, the encoding specificity principle (Tulving, 1983).Retrieval likelihood is proportional to the similarity between what is encoded and retrieval cues.Both prediction and memory retrieval are types of pattern completion.Explanation has an additional component that is one meaning of the word "understanding": A relational description or representation that is homomorphic to some relational system in the world.

The history of cognitive science and AI can be described as a back-and-forth between predictive models that usually come in the form of associative networks (e.g., McCulloch & Pitts, 1943;Minsky & Papert, 1969;Rumelhart & McClelland, 1988;LeCun, Bengio, & Hinton, 2015) and models that offer explanations in the form of mental models (Craik, 1943), computer programs (e.g., Newell & Simon, 1972), compositional structure (Chomsky, 1965), probabilistic generative engines (e.g., Pearl, 1988), or causal structure (e.g., Pearl, 2000).Associative models turn out to be promising models of memory retrieval, visual object identification, text and audio segmentation, and other tasks, but the difficulty theorists face in articulating a mapping between associative structure and structure in the world being represented (Lake et al., 2017) means that such models have not revealed much about how people reason when they are trying to explain, when deliberating over a structure that maps more directly onto the symbolic forms we use to represent the world at a conscious level in thought and language.

So the proposed resolution in my 1996 paper was that the two kinds of models are incommensurate but we need them both; in other words, there are two systems of thought, an associative one that reasons based on the statistical structure of the world-or an approximation of it-and one that reasons by generating and analyzing various kinds of symbolic representations.The course of cognitive science has only strengthened my view.Indeed, this view has come to permeate the field, under the system 1 versus system 2 distinction, a nomenclature I find quite disturbing due to its theoretical neutrality.I agree with those who complain that the distinction is meaningless if treated as generic and devoid of empirical content.In a small effort to provide some theoretical constraint, I nowadays refer to the distinction as that between intuition and deliberation, in part for reasons I elaborate below.

There is some tension between how the distinction has evolved in the reasoning and the decision-making literatures; the former focuses on cognitive processes, whereas the latter tends to distinguish cognitive from "emotional" processes.In my view, only the reasoning literature gets it right, but the decision-making literature is correct in drawing attention to the issue of affect.Cognition is always linked to an affective response and indeed, there are different emotions associated with each system of thought (Darlow & Sloman, 2010;Lerner et al., 2015), so the distinction between cognition versus emotion is not the same as the distinction between deliberation and intuition.

### Categorization is not categorization

The two systems distinction has infiltrated my thinking for decades now, at least sometimes productively.Perhaps the clearest application was to a dual systems view of how people judge probabilities given both background data and case data (Sloman et al., 2003) in the analysis of what is often called "base-rate neglect."We proposed that people combine an intuitive assessment of similarity as captured by Kahneman and Tversky's (1972) representativeness heuristic with a rule-based assessment that makes use of nested-set structures.Probability theory is based on sets of events.When information is presented in a way that makes set structure transparent, people are more likely to get qualitative probabilistic relations correct.For instance, set structure is more transparent when talking about frequency rather than probability and when talking about exemplars of a category (like ravens) rather than properties of the category (like having wings).The view was spelled out in greater detail and defended more fully in a Behavioral and Brain Sciences target article (Barbey & Sloman, 2007).

A more general way of making this point is to consider the difference between an inside and an outside view of any kind of class or set.An inside view means thinking about the internal structure of the set, its features, properties, and the relations among them.For instance, we are taking an inside view if we are thinking about how a type of object works-its causal structure-or what the compositional structure is of (say) an organization.Stereotypes involve an inside view because they focus us on the (purported) internal structure of a social category.This is a natural way to think and largely effective.But it fails to give us a certain kind of information that we get when we take an outside view, when we think about the multiple instances that comprise a class or a set.An outside view reveals how frequent the set is, and how it relates to other sets.If we ask ourselves, "Of 100 women who were politically active in college, would more of them be bank tellers, or feminist bank tellers?" it is obvious that more would be bank tellers because the set of women that are feminist bank tellers is contained within the set of those who are bank tellers (Fiedler, 1988).

I showed a similar effect in the kind of inductive task I mentioned earlier.If you ask people, "If all mammals have an ulnar artery, what is the probability that all weasels do?" the answer you generally get is somewhere between 0.7 and 0.8 even when people later affirm that all weasels are mammals.People do not automatically treat subordinate categories as inheriting the properties of their superordinates.But inducing an outside view changes responses.If you tell people that every individual mammal has an ulnar artery and then ask about weasels, now the vast majority of people say 100%.I reported this result in Categorical Inference is not a Tree: The Myth of Inheritance Hierarchies (Sloman, 1998).

Because I had been studying category structure, it was impossible to avoid the issue of what a category is.In a series of papers with Barbara Malt, we distinguished linguistic categories (how people name things) and more conceptual categories (how people think about things).In one study, we showed that speakers of American English, Mandarin Chinese, and Argentinian Spanish name objects differently (their linguistic categories are cross-cutting).Yet, they judge the similarity of the objects identically (Malt et al., 1999), suggesting that they conceive of them the same way.In work with Silvia Gennari, we showed a similar linguistic versus conceptual contrast for motion events (Gennari et al., 2002).In this particular way, the Whorfian hypothesis is wrong; the language we use to describe categories does not determine thought.

### Causal models

In the early 2000s, I came upon an idea that struck me as offering a large chunk of the answer to my question about how people represent and reason about structured relations, how people construe objects and events from the inside.I had been avoiding appealing to causal structure because I was not sure what it was.I had encountered theories of causal learning, but they failed to adequately distinguish cause from correlation.But then I came across Judea Pearl's (2000) book on causal Bayesian networks, a normative mathematical approach to representing causal structure with graphs.It was part of an emerging view in philosophy and statistics defining causality in terms of intervention: A causes B if a sufficient intervention by an agent that changes the value of A leads to a change in B. As an experimentalist, this definition seemed exactly right.The power of an experiment, in contrast to a mere observation, comes from the fact that it involves an intervention on an independent variable.

So, I set off studying causal models: how they are learned, how they are used to categorize, how they are used to reason and make judgments, and the question closest to my heart: how they are used to make decisions.The issue of decision making was particularly difficult and subtle as it raised questions about how much control we have over our decisions.Are humans agents with the freedom to choose, or is this freedom an illusion?Are we just channels for forces that operate outside our ability to deliberate about options?In the language of causal models (Pearl, 2000), is choosing an option really an intervention on the world?Based on evidence that people make inferences about decisions that conform to the logic of intervention, my answer is "yes" (Hagmayer & Sloman, 2009;Sloman & Hagmayer, 2006).The issue led me to the study of self-deception (Fernbach, Hagmayer, & Sloman 2014;Sloman, Fernbach, & Hagmayer, 2010).When people self-deceive, they either believe they are choosing freely (intervening) but are not (e.g., teenage smoking), or they believe they are not choosing at all but in fact are (e.g., child abuse).

Later, I started examining one of the key roles of causal models: explanation.Much to my surprise, my colleagues and I found that one of the most common assumptions in this field-that people prefer simple explanations over more complex ones-may not be correct in general (Sloman et al., 2019).People like explanations that overdetermine what they are trying to explain; they like the object of their explanation to seem inevitable.For instance, people like an explanation for Ronald Reagan's election as U.S. President that enlists many independent factors but do not like explanations that only list a single cause.This may have to do with the fact that explanations are often for others' benefit, not just for sense-making but also for persuasion.

Understanding how the world works allows prediction, effective action, and consideration of alternative worlds.As such, I continue to believe that causal structure serves as the infrastructure of thought.But I have become wary of the idea that knowledge is represented in the mind by causal Bayesian networks.Such networks are probabilistically coherent, yet people are not (Kahneman, Slovic, & Tversky, 1982).People also make errors in causal reasoning.They often fail to respect the Markov property or screening-off principlefoot_0 (Chaigneau, Barsalou, & Sloman, 2004;Park & Sloman, 2013;Rottman & Hastie, 2014), they neglect alternative causes when reasoning from cause to effect (Fernbach, Darlow, & Sloman, 2010, 2011a, 2011b), and neglect data when they have a qualitative causal explanation available (Bes et al., 2012).Overall, the evidence suggests that people use causal structure in a qualitative way; they do not update their beliefs according to Bayesian dictates.

### Ignorance and the community of knowledge

I also concluded, by virtue of unpublished studies asking people to generate causal graphs, that people's causal thinking is remarkably superficial.This is consistent with other demonstrations of human ignorance (Dunning, 2011) and evidence that people do not understand causal mechanisms as well as they think they do (Lawson, 2006;Rozenblit & Keil, 2002).My colleagues and I applied Rozenblit and Keil's technique to the domain of political policies.We asked people for ratings of understanding and found that asking them to explain how the policies worked caused their judgment of their own understanding to decline: Most people failed to generate much of an explanation and this burst their illusion of understanding (Fernbach, Rogers, Fox, & Sloman, 2013).In subsequent work, we found the effect does not hold for everyone.Specifically, it does not hold for people who are cognitively reflective (Fernbach, Sloman, St. Louis, & Shube, 2013).

Why do people think they understand how things work better than they do?Sloman and Fernbach (2017) argue it is because they fail to distinguish what others know from what they themselves know; we live in a community of knowledge in the sense that we treat all knowledge that we have access to as contributing to our understanding whether it is in our heads, somebody else's head, or even elsewhere like on the Internet (Ward, 2013).It is well-known that people tend to think that others know what they know (the curse of knowledge); our argument is the converse: Both phenomena can be explained on the assumption that people fail to draw a sharp distinction between what they know and what others know.The curse of knowledge arises because we attribute knowledge in our heads to others; the illusion of understanding arises because we attribute knowledge in others' heads to ourselves.

The idea that knowledge is communal in ways that we are not aware of is an old idea, consistent with a variety of data.In philosophy, the study of epistemology (theories of knowledge) has often assumed historically that knowledge was a social entity (Fricker et al., 2019).The idea obtained urgency in the study of propaganda and its contribution to war (Lippman, 1922) and was taken up to explain how people are able to achieve reference using scientific terms that are understood and verifiable by only a small group of experts ("semantic externalism"; Putnam, 1975).

Collective thinking comes in at least two forms: Collaboration involves a relatively small, highly interactive group (e.g., families, colleagues, and church community) of people who often share a common goal.Collaboration generally involves an explicit division of cognitive labor, frequent communication, regular feedback, and sometimes a social hierarchy.But collective thinking also occurs at a much larger scale; specifically, we outsource much of our thinking to people with whom we share a culture.This outsourcing sometimes happens in obvious ways (e.g., calling an unfamiliar expert to solve a new problem), but more often, we outsource without knowing that we are doing so (e.g., we use terms like "antibody" with only the vaguest sense of what we are talking about).Unlike collaborative processes, cultural networks rarely share a goal, but rather arise organically as the union of many interest groups and diverse backgrounds.They are thus both a rich source of knowledge, skills, and experience but also a source of divergent perspectives and interests.

It is helpful to think about collaboration versus outsourcing in terms of dual systems (Hemmatian & Sloman, 2020), each system representing one means of accessing the community: Deliberation generally accesses the community through collaboration, the intentional creation, and use of information by a group.Collaboration can generate intuitions, but the structure of intuitions generally includes outsourced data, information that one does not know oneself but assumes that someone or something else (known or unknown) can supply.

The sine qua non that cognition is collective is evidence that people depend on others to do their thinking for them without knowing that they are doing so; that they outsource reasoning, judgment, and decision making without awareness.We have found that people outsource their sense of understanding: merely telling people that experts understand a scientific phenomenon or a policy made them feel like they understood it a little better themselves, even though we provided no information about how the phenomenon or policy works (Rabb, Han, & Sloman, 2020;Sloman & Rabb, 2016).They also outsource their explanations: People judge a clearly circular explanation to have more value if it is entrenched in the community than if it is not (Hemmatian & Sloman, 2018).The fact that other people call on the explanation gives it value even if it offers no information.The appeal of vacuous but socially entrenched explanations may arise from our general tendency to depend on the division of cognitive labor that resides in our communities of knowledge.Many examples of such dependence can be found in the anthropological literature (e.g., Henrich, 2015).

Among others, anthropologists have long argued for the social nature of knowledge, pointing out many of the ways that cultural influences shape beliefs and knowledge (cf., Gatewood, 2012, for a brief review).To the degree that anthropologists have theorized about the structure of knowledge, they tend to take the standard view that knowledge is information that has been transferred among and stored by individuals.For instance, Romney et al. (1996) begin their analysis of kinship terms with the explicit assumption that, "Culture consists of shared cognitive representations in the minds of individuals" (opening sentence of abstract).The community of knowledge idea is different.It is that culture consists of representations that are distributed across the minds of individuals, in the sense that a pop song consists of an emergent property of parts that are distributed across the members of a band.

The community of knowledge idea is essentially that humans are tribal in the most basic sense, in the way they think.This explains the heated, polarized political climate that the United States is currently experiencing.In our original paper (Fernbach et al., 2013), we found that attempts to explain how political policies worked most often resulted in failure; in general, people were unable to meet their own expectations in giving an account of how policies worked.As a result, not only did their judgments of their own understanding of the issues decline, so did their confidence in their attitudes about the policies; they became less extreme.The effect of explanation on humility, on people's sense of understanding, has been replicated many times (Gaviria et al., 2017;Johnson et al., 2016;Vitriol & Marsh, 2018;Voelkel et al., 2018;Zeveney & Marsh, 2016), although support for the effect on extremity is mixed.Crawford and Ruscio (2021) reported a failure to replicate our effect on extremity, although Meyers et al. (2020) found that inducing feelings of ignorance through explanation made people more receptive to the expert opinion of economists.

Polarization is a social phenomenon that results from the fact that people do not think for themselves.We reach conclusions by thinking collaboratively with others and outsource much of our thinking to those with whom we share a political perspective or a set of values.The fact that people outsource their thinking is neither irrational nor blameworthy.The world is much too complicated for any individual to fully understand.We have to take advantage of others for much of our information processing.In order to read an article on a computer, you depend on a wealth of understanding from the hordes of engineers and other workers who created the computer; in order to be a successful hunter, you need weaponry developed by your tribe or culture.Conceiving of understanding as a collective phenomenon explains how to understand fake news (Lazer et al., 2018), what needs to be done to maintain biodiversity (Waeber et al., 2021), and many other things.It has allowed my colleagues and I to show how to induce citizens to make use of evidence without the need to show them any (Sloman et al., 2021).We influenced the opinion of a sample of citizens by simply telling them about how a group's careful deliberation (in the form of a "deliberative poll" or "consensus conference") changed the group's opinions, without telling them any particulars about the deliberation itself.

The community of knowledge hypothesis demands a theory of knowledge representation that appeals to the distribution of cognitive labor (Rabb, Fernbach, & Sloman, 2019).The basic structure of such a collective representation is that individuals share coarse and abstract knowledge about how things work; individuals have much narrower areas of specialization that support their roles as subject-matter experts.Keil (2005) has been investigating how people develop cultural knowledge in a way that mirrors how knowledge is institutionalized.Thinking about knowledge this way has implications for how to understand the role of cognitive neuroscience (Sloman, Patterson, Barbey, 2021).If thinking is a collective process, then it does not go on entirely in any individual brain; it goes on in networks of brains (and also-as work on situated cognition has taught us-in machines and on whiteboards).But those networks are not identifiable for they are constantly changing and include the brains of people who are now dead.To illustrate, Americans live with a legal system grounded in ideas and values that were prevalent over 200 years ago.Much of the task of the U.S. Supreme Court is to root out what those ideas and values were and figure out how they apply today.

### Conclusion

You can see that my approach to my work has been heavily influenced by Endel Tulving's approach to psychology.Each step in my academic career has been guided by an intuitive sense about how the cognitive system works, a sense that derives from a combination of considering what it takes for a cognitive system to be successful in the environment that people find themselves in, familiarity with what different computational frameworks can and cannot model, and exposure to data about human performance.

Maybe such intuitions have led me astray, blinding me to the ineffable complexity at the heart of cognition.Maybe the only way to make progress in cognitive science is to use formal (mathematical and other computational) tools to build models and then to test them.In fact, I believe that cognition enjoys a lot of ineffable complexity that we have no introspective access to.The success of deep learning systems in reproducing a lot of impressive human-like behavior despite the great difficulty experts have in understanding what the systems are doing provides suggestive evidence that a lot of human-like behavior is not the result of processes that the average person can understand, let alone introspect on.

But our behaviors are not entirely divorced from our thoughts.We can articulate many of the reasons for our behavior to ourselves and others (e.g., that we are replacing a specific part of a complex mechanism because we know what is broken; that we are avoiding someone because we know how they behave).Even if these thoughts arise from a complex network that we do not really understand, the very fact that we think them and share them gives them causal force on future behavior.They become reasons that we now have access to the next time we are faced with a similar problem or decision, and they become part of our conversation and thus become norms in our community, giving them additional causal impact.This is true in general, and it is true in particular.Cognitive scientists' reasons for their theorizing may emerge from some ineffable process; but that does not make them wrong or useless.Indeed, the problem of cognition is so hard that we have to bring everything available to bear on the problem, formal tools and intuition combined.

So, what is the answer to my guiding question?How do people process information so as to develop the attitudes and beliefs that guide their action?I still believe we have two systems: an intuitive one that involves massively parallel processing and relies on associative memory processes and a deliberative one that can operate in more analytical ways and that is used to implement the kind of formal tools that we learn in school.But I have also come to the conclusion that the deliberative one is also deliberative in the sense that it often operates through communication with other people.Much of what it does is to help us arrive at the belief that is licensed by our community.Many of the opinions we espouse have less to do with thought than with who we affiliate with that takes a position.
