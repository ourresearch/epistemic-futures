# What Gives a Diagnostic Label Value? Common Use Over Informativeness

## Abstract (from OpenAlex metadata)

A label’s entrenchment, its degree of use by members of a community, affects its perceived explanatory value even if the label provides no substantive information (Hemmatian &amp;amp; Sloman, 2018). In three experiments, we show that laypersons and mental health professionals see entrenched psychiatric and non-psychiatric diagnostic labels as better explanations than non-entrenched labels even if they are circular. Using scenarios involving experts who discuss unfamiliar diagnostic categories, we show that this preference is not due to violations of conversational norms, lack of reflectiveness or attentiveness, and the characters’ familiarity or unfamiliarity with the label. In Experiment 1, whether a label provided novel symptom information or not had no impact on lay responses, while its entrenchment enhanced ratings of explanation quality. The effect persisted in Experiment 2 for causally incoherent categories and regardless of direct provision of mechanistic information. The effect of entrenchment was partly related to induced causal beliefs about the category, even when participants were informed there is no causal relation. Most participants in both experiments did not report any effect of entrenchment and the effect was present for those who did not. In Experiment 3, mental health professionals showed the effect using diagnoses that were mere shorthands for symptoms, despite a tendency to rate all explanations as unsatisfactory. The data suggest that bringing experts’ attention to the manipulation eliminates the effect. We discuss practical implications for mental health disciplines and potential ways to mitigate the impact of entrenchment.

Keywords: Diagnosis, explanation, entrenchment, common cause, circularity

Public Significance Statement

Mental health professionals and the public see labels for psychiatric and medical disorders as better explanations if they are entrenched in a community rather than introduced by experts but not commonly used. Common use of a diagnostic label makes it seem informative regardless of the actual information it carries and can engender beliefs about causes even when respondents are informed there is no causal relation.

Introduction

Immanuel Kant reportedly quipped: “Physicians think they do a lot for a patient when they give his disease a name” (from Lock, Last & Dunea, 2001). Wrong or right, Kant’s observation suggests that clinical diagnostic categories enjoy more status than they deserve, a concern brought to life in the mental health domain by the classic work of Rosenhan (1973; but see Cahalan, 2019). There is now ample evidence that despite lack of scientific consensus about the etiologies of mental disorders, psychodiagnostic labels often come with strong beliefs about the causes and effects of disorders, which in turn may affect treatment decisions and even the empathy afforded to patients by practicing clinicians and laypeople alike (Ahn, Kim & Lebowitz, 2017; Lebowitz & Ahn, 2014).

Psychodiagnostic labels are ubiquitous, presumably because of the efficiency they provide in communicating sets of co-occurring symptoms. Some labels also point to extensive technical knowledge held by experts in the community. Diagnoses may be attempts to refer to often complex psychological and biological mechanisms that are critical for predicting patient etiologies and outcomes, as well as the effects of interventions (American Psychiatric Association, 2013).

Giffin and colleagues (2017) examined whether this common association with more profound knowledge underlies the perceived explanatory value of psychodiagnostic labels. They showed that labeling a maladaptive behavioral tendency is deemed more explanatory than describing it and offer evidence that the labels’ perceived explanatoriness was due to implied causal content. But diagnostic categories seem to have a life beyond the facts they represent. Wilkenfeld et al. (2016) show that laypersons perceive a causal connection between diagnostic categories and their associated symptoms when learning about psychodiagnostic labels. This perception persists even if provided with explicit and detailed information that the labeling was a historical mistake and not even a statistical association exists among the symptoms.

Hemmatian and Sloman (2018) offer one possible explanation for this finding. They find that learning that a community uses a label to refer to a category (that it is “entrenched”) increases the label’s perceived explanatoriness for associated properties even when it contributes no real information. Participants were introduced to characters who encountered made-up biological, natural, and social categories defined as a set of features (for instance stars with variable brightness). In the unentrenched condition, one or a few individuals in the scenario coined a label for the category (e.g., “Evans star”). In the entrenched condition, they learned the label is already commonly accepted. Participants learned afterwards that nothing else is known about the category. The label was then used by a character as an explanation for the defining property (e.g. “The star has variable brightness because it is an Evans star”) in a clear example of a circular explanation. Participants were asked to what extent this explanation answered a question about why a member of the category exhibited the defining properties. Through four experiments, commonly-used (“entrenched”) labels were perceived as better answers than unentrenched labels regardless of characters’ expertise, their familiarity with the label, and whether the label corresponded to a collectively recognized and accepted category. Independent manipulations that added featural information to the labels beyond what the inquirer had observed—creating labels that did carry information about other properties shared by members—did not impact the ratings. Causal information was impactful, but independent of entrenchment. The effect persisted even for categories that were described as haphazard combinations of unrelated features. Hemmatian and Sloman argue that this overreliance on entrenchment arose because the community usually has useful information to provide so people come to rely on community cues even when they are uninformative.

In the current experiments, we examine whether the effect of entrenchment on perceived explanatory value obtains for psychodiagnostic labels that are explicitly devoid of featural and causal information. We chose a variety of labels based on actual diagnostic categories. In some cases, experts’ use of the terms has persisted despite doubts about the very existence of the associated disorder. These include entrenched labels that have been removed from authoritative manuals (e.g., piblokto and Ganser syndrome; American Psychiatric Association, 2013) and common labels that were never a part of such manuals (e.g. Diogenes syndrome; Clark, Mankikar & Gray, 1975).

Similar to Hemmatian and Sloman (2018), we rule out alternative explanations that relate to characters’ expertise, their familiarity with the labels, and the observance of conversational norms in their use. We know from previous research that causal knowledge is especially relevant to psychodiagnostic labels (Ahn et al., 2017). We therefore also look at whether participants’ causal beliefs mediate or are influenced by the effect of label entrenchment. Because experts and laypersons alike have different causal and outcome beliefs about mental and medical disorders (Cooper & Marsh, 2015), we compare responses to label entrenchment and informativeness manipulations for both types of categories. To explore whether the induced beliefs translate into practical considerations about patients, we also measure effects on the perceived treatability of the disorders.

Experts may not be immune to the effects of entrenchment: When failure to consistently confirm the reliability and validity of several disorders prompted the American Psychiatric Association (APA) to remove them from their influential diagnostic manual (DSM-5), most experts opposed the decision (Pull, 2013). One of the top reasons cited for their stance was the categories’ frequent use, raising the possibility that they may use entrenchment as a relevant cue for explanatory value. However, experts may be more attuned to the real informational value of diagnostic labels. Experiment 3 tests these hypotheses by asking mental health experts to rate unfamiliar and uninformative psychodiagnostic categories that are either entrenched or not.

Experiment 1

To determine whether commonly accepted but vacuous diagnostic categories would be deemed explanatory, each participant in Experiment 1 read several scenarios involving experts discussing psychiatric or non-psychiatric disorders who knew only their defining symptoms and rated how explanatory the label was for a set of symptoms. Along with the diagnostic category’s entrenchment, we manipulated whether the label imparted new symptom information about the case to the expert requesting an explanation. We believe this provides a strong test of the influence of entrenchment because the presence of useful information gives participants a concrete basis for their judgment, thus allowing them to ignore entrenchment if they want to. If they do not, that would suggest that the effect of entrenchment is not due to a task demand; respondents use it even when they could appeal to a different experimental manipulation. The results of Hemmatian and Sloman (2018) lead us to expect label entrenchment to increase perceived explanatoriness, but no effect of the informativeness manipulation.

Giffin and colleagues’ (2017) results suggest that an effect of entrenchment on perceived explanatoriness may be mediated by increased belief in a common cause for the symptoms. If so, the effect should be eliminated by controlling for this belief. Alternatively, belief in a common cause for the symptoms may be one of several effects of entrenchment, or the two may be unrelated. To explore these possibilities, we asked participants in all conditions about their degree of belief in a common cause underlying the symptoms and the perceived treatability of the labeled combination, an important outcome rating that has been shown to correlate with common cause ratings (Ahn, Flanagan & Marsh, 2006). We also administered a test of reflectiveness to see if a tendency to examine one’s initial intuitions would lead to better realization of the vacuous nature of the provided explanations.

Method

Participants. Three hundred and sixty-one participants were recruited using Amazon’s Mechanical Turk. Sample size was determined based on power analysis of experiments in Hemmatian and Sloman (2018). No data was recorded for those who failed an attention check before signing of informed consent. The average age of the remaining participants was 34.70 years (SD = 10.28; range: 19 to 70). Two hundred and fourteen participants were male and 145 female, with two participants choosing the “other” option.

Materials and dependent measures. Disorder scenarios. Each participant was provided with four scenarios about either psychiatric or non-psychiatric disorders, each defined by three symptoms with no obvious mechanistic connection to one another (see supplementary materials for the full text of all scenarios). Medical disorders were adapted from diverse conditions in medical textbooks with some modifications to ensure lack of prior familiarity (Leiner’s disease, Melkersson–Rosenthal syndrome, histiocytosis and bronchiectasis; Warrell, Cox and Firth, 2018). Psychiatric disorders and their defining symptoms included disorders from DSM-5 with modified symptoms (schizoid personality disorder, ataque de nervios; American Psychiatric Association, 2013), a disorder included in DSM-IV-TR but removed in DSM-5 (Ganser syndrome; American Psychiatric Association, 2000), and a made-up category modeled after similar disorders (pathological fear of insects). The disorders were chosen to be unfamiliar to non-experts and cover various classes of mental disorders (dissociative, personality, anxiety and culture-bound; American Psychiatric Association, 2013). The scenarios included one category from Hemmatian and Sloman (2018) and another from Giffin and colleagues (2017) to facilitate comparison. Made-up labels, mainly using Latin or French phrases, were used for the disorders to ensure lack of prior familiarity and to imitate clinical jargon.

Each scenario began with a named expert observing either one (informative label condition) or three (uninformative label condition) symptoms in a patient. The genders of both characters and the implied racial/ethnic backgrounds of the named experts were counterbalanced. The expert then asked two colleagues familiar with the patient why she exhibited the symptom(s). In all conditions, the colleagues responded by appealing to a diagnostic label. In the informative label condition, the label implied the presence of two associated symptoms in the patient that the inquiring expert had not personally observed, providing her with novel information about the case. In the uninformative label condition, the inquirer had observed all symptoms associated with the label and therefore received no novel information from the response. In all conditions participants were told that nothing else is known about the relevant diagnostic category, ruling out any further information in the label. For instance, participants in the informative label condition read:

A clinical psychologist named John came across a patient who had unfounded worries about being infected by parasites, but whether any other symptom was present was unclear to him. He asked two clinical psychologist colleagues who knew the patient: “Why does he show this symptom?”. The colleagues knew that the same patient was also fearful of exposure to cleaning products and had sensations of insects crawling under his skin. One of them responded: “Because he has perreptophobia.”

In the uninformative label condition, the expert observed all three symptoms. Again, he asked colleagues for an explanation of the symptoms and was given the diagnostic label as a response.

In the entrenched condition, this was followed by:

It turns out that John’s two colleagues had encountered several patients with similar symptoms before. They had found that the name accepted by the community of mental health experts for these symptoms is perreptophobia. They had shared the symptoms and the name perreptophobia with John a while back, but he had forgotten the name. The name refers only to the combination of all of these symptoms and to nothing else. Nothing else is known about perreptophobia.

The text of the unentrenched condition was identical except that the sentence in italics was replaced by the following: “They had searched for but found no previous record of such a combination of symptoms, and decided to name it perreptophobia themselves.”

Participants were told that the inquirer was already familiar with the category and the label so that the label would not seem less comprehensible in the unentrenched condition. Care was taken to minimize violations of conversational norms (Grice, 1975). The experts providing the explanations were truthful in all conditions, thereby satisfying the maxim of quality. They had legitimate reasons in the informative conditions for sharing the label in the context of the conversation, to communicate the presence of symptoms that the inquirer had not observed, satisfying the maxim of relevance. Regardless of the entrenchment manipulation, the inquirer was familiar with the label and what it refers to, meaning that as far as the explainers knew, it was not ambiguous or obscure (maxim of manner) and succinctly provided as much information as is genuinely available about the category (maxim of quantity).

After reading each scenario, participants were required to answer three questions. The first one asked for the extent the colleagues’ use of the label answered the named character’s inquiry about the symptoms (called extent answered henceforth). Participants chose a number from one to seven, with one labeled as “not at all”, four described as “to a moderate degree” and seven labeled “completely.” The second and third questions, modeled after Ahn and colleagues (2006), asked for the perceived likelihood that the symptoms have the same underlying cause in all patients (called “common cause” henceforth) and that of the syndrome’s “treatability”. The second and third questions were counterbalanced across participants. These measures also used a 7-point scale, with one labeled as “not at all likely”, four described as “moderately likely” and seven labeled “very likely”.

Comprehension check. Four comprehension questions followed the last scenario on the same page for every participant in the same order. The first question related to whether anybody knew anything about the diagnostic category other than the defining features (“implied knowledge” question). The correct answer was “no”. The second probed explicit memory of the entrenchment manipulation by asking whether the diagnostic label was accepted by the expert community (“entrenchment check”). The third question asked whether the inquiring expert had previously heard the label and the symptoms it referred to (“familiarity question”). The correct answer was “yes”. The final question asked about the number of symptoms that the inquiring expert had observed in her patient (“informativeness check”). The possible choices for all questions were “yes”, “maybe” and “no”.

Cognitive Reflection Test. The Cognitive Reflection Test (CRT; Frederick, 2003) measures the degree to which individuals respond analytically as opposed to intuitively. It is composed of three questions with incorrect answers that come to mind easily and correct answers that are not hard to compute, but require suppressing the incorrect intuitive ones.. We divided participants into high- versus low-CRT groups based on whether they responded to a majority of the questions correctly.

Design and Procedure. Entrenchment and label informativeness were manipulated within subjects, while type of disorder (psychiatric versus non-psychiatric) served as a between subjects factor. After an attention check and signing of informed consent, participants were provided with instructions emphasizing that there is no right or wrong answer. Each participant was then shown four scenarios, one from each of the four conditions created by the entrenchment and label informativeness manipulations. The pairing of disorders with conditions was fully counterbalanced. For the order of presentation, an initial random order was determined for all scenarios. A Latin square was used to create four distinct orders across participants.

Comprehension checks, the CRT, and demographic questions followed the scenarios. To examine the role of expertise, demographic questions included whether the participant is a medical or mental health expert. If they responded “no”, they were asked to self-rate their level of knowledge about psychiatric or non-psychiatric disorders on a five-point scale from “very low (hardly know anything about them)” to “very high (on par with a mental health/medical expert).” Participants who rated themselves above three were classified highly knowledgeable. Finally, participants were asked if they noticed any factor significantly affecting their responses. The possible answers were “yes”, “maybe” and “no”. If one of the first two options was selected, they were asked to state the determining factors in an open-ended format.

Results

Extent answered ratings. Figure 1 shows the extent answered ratings from Experiment 1 (see supplementary materials for scenario means and standard errors across conditions and dependent variables).

Figure 1. Average ratings for different levels of label entrenchment (horizontal axis) and label informativeness (different colors), with respect to the degree to which the label answers a question seeking an explanation for associated symptoms. Error bars indicate the 95% confidence intervals for marginal means of different conditions.

A repeated-measures 2 (unentrenched vs. entrenched label) by 2 (uninformative vs. informative label) ANOVA was performed on extent answered. Disorder type (psychiatric vs. non-psychiatric) was a between-subjects variable. We found a significant effect of entrenchment, F(1, 359) = 75.83, p < .001, ηp2 = .17. All scenarios and most participants showed the effect. The main effect of disorder type was not significant (p = .82) nor was its interaction with entrenchment (p = .45). Neither the main effect of label informativeness (p = .97) nor its interaction with entrenchment (p = .29) were significant.

Extent answered and common cause ratings were significantly correlated with each other (r = .49, p < .001). To see if accounting for common cause ratings would eliminate the entrenchment effect on extent answered ratings, an ANCOVA was performed with common cause measurements as a covariate. The main effect of entrenchment remained significant (F(1, 358) = 6.30, p = .013, ηp2 = .02).

When cognitive reflectiveness (high-CRT vs. low-CRT) was added to the ANOVA as a between-subjects independent variable, it was predictive of ratings, F(1, 357) = 33.83, p < .001, ηp2 = .09. Higher CRT resulted in lower extent answered ratings. Its interaction with entrenchment was significant, F(1, 357) = 10.90, p = .001, ηp2 = .03. Higher CRT resulted in a stronger entrenchment effect. A mixed effects linear regression including participants and scenarios as random factors yielded similar results (see supplementary materials).

The main effect of self-reported domain knowledge was significant, F(1, 337) =3.38, p = .035, ηp2 = .03. The self-identified knowledgeable participants gave lower ratings, the others responded more variably (highly knowledgeable: M = 3.60, SD = 0.10; moderately knowledgeable: M = 3.90, SD = 0.14; less knowledgeable: M = 4.32, SD = 0.32). Nevertheless, domain knowledge did not interact with entrenchment (p = .98), only with the label informativeness manipulation (F(1, 337) = 4.22, p = .015, ηp2 = .02). Self-identified less knowledgeable people showed a stronger informativeness effect. Twenty-one percent of the participants reported being experts in either medical or mental health disciplines. Excluding mental health experts assigned to the psychiatric disorder condition and medical experts assigned to non-psychiatric disorder condition did not change any of the reported results (see supplementary materials).

Common cause ratings. To see whether label entrenchment would affect beliefs in common cause for symptoms, we performed an ANOVA with common cause as the dependent variable. The main effect of entrenchment was significant, F(1, 359) = 31.99, p < .001, ηp2 = .08 (entrenched: M = 4.66, SE = 0.06; unentrenched: M =4.28, SE = 0.06). Psychiatric disorders garnered significantly lower common cause ratings, F(1, 359) = 6.42, p = .012, ηp2= .03 (psychiatric: M = 4.32, SE = 0.09; non-psychiatric: M = 4.62; SE = 0.08). Again, neither the main effect of label informativeness (p = .98) nor its interaction with entrenchment (p = .96) were significant.

Treatability ratings. With treatability as the dependent variable, the main effect of entrenchment was weaker but also significant, F(1, 359) = 10.11, p < .01, ηp2 = .03 (entrenched: M = 4.33, SE = 0.06; unentrenched: M = 4.13, SE = 0.06). Extent answered and treatability ratings were significantly correlated with each other (r = .43, p < .001). However, the main effect of entrenchment on extent answered ratings remained significant with treatability ratings as a covariate, F(1, 358) = 16.87, p < .001, ηp2 = .05. Label informativeness and its interaction with the entrenchment manipulation were insignificant contributors to the treatability ratings (range of p-values: [.34, .40]).

Additional analyses. Only 70 participants (19.4 percent) reported noticing any consistent factor affecting their ratings, with 246 (68.1 percent) saying they perceived no trend in their responses and the rest expressing uncertainty. Of those who noticed being affected by the materials or were uncertain, 35 (9.7 percent of the overall sample) specifically mentioned the entrenchment manipulation. The pattern of responses was identical after excluding those participants, suggesting that the entrenchment effect is not dependent on paying conscious attention to the manipulation.

The participants’ explicit memory of scenario content was similarly imperfect, as evidenced by responses to comprehension checks. Of all participants, 17.45% responded incorrectly to the implied knowledge question, 42.94% to the entrenchment check, 20.50% to the familiarity question, and 42.65% to the informativeness check. The high error rate may be attributed to asking the questions at the end of the experiment and the large quantity of relevant information that participants needed to remember. Excluding these participants revealed some impact of the informativeness manipulation on extent answered ratings, but did not affect the results otherwise. These and additional analyses can be found in the supplementary materials.

Discussion

We found a robust effect of diagnostic categories’ entrenchment on their perceived explanatory power and no effect of whether the category label provided an inquirer with novel featural information. This result obtained even though any other information in the label beyond the defining features was explicitly ruled out and a vast majority of participants noticed the absence of information.

Is the diagnostic entrenchment effect mediated by causal beliefs about the categories in question? Not entirely. Psychiatric and non-psychiatric disorders elicited different beliefs in common cause (Cooper & Marsh, 2015). However, there was no significant difference between the two types of categories in susceptibility to the label entrenchment effect. Moreover, adding common cause beliefs to the model as a covariate did not eliminate the effect. Nevertheless, entrenched labels were seen as more likely to represent symptoms with a common cause and, possibly because of the same causal beliefs, were perceived as slightly more treatable.

The results were robust to exclusion based on comprehension checks, memory of being impacted by the manipulation, self-reported domain knowledge, individual differences in responding, and across scenarios. More reflective individuals showed a stronger entrenchment effect possibly because more reflective individuals paid more attention to the scenarios overall.

Experiment 2

A possible rationale for the entrenchment effect in Experiment 1 is that labels in the uninformative conditions were familiar to recipients. In Experiment 2, they were not. The expert asking for an explanation was unfamiliar with the labels and what they referred to, so that the explanation would be incomprehensible to them.

Diagnostic categories generally carry causal information, a fact known to our participants as evidenced by the strong correlations between ratings of explanation quality and common cause in Experiment 1 (Ahn et al., 2017) even though the scenarios explicitly denied the existence of such information. But the entrenchment of a diagnostic category might signal the belief of a potential to accumulate causal knowledge in the future, making entrenchment an indirect but useful cue to the future usefulness of a diagnosis. To disambiguate the effect of entrenchment from present or future causal knowledge, we manipulated the causal coherence of disorder categories independently in Experiment 2. Categories resulting from coincidental co-occurrences of symptoms known to have no reliable cause lack any useful information and thus provide a strong test of the effect of entrenchment.

Alternatively, it may be that the type or amount of knowledge provided about diagnoses in the informative label conditions of Experiment 1 was not adequate for participants to base their judgments on. The information consisted of general properties that influence judgments of explanation quality less than specific mechanistic information (Zemla et al., 2017). Unlike Experiment 1, which did not include any causal information, each causally coherent diagnostic label in Experiment 2 explicitly pointed to a singular mechanism responsible for all associated symptoms in all cases, the gold standard for etiology in clinical disciplines. Continued reliance on entrenchment of a diagnosis in ratings despite such a strong alternative causal basis for judgment would be powerful evidence for the dissociation of entrenchment from the category’s informativeness.

Finally, it may not be the community’s acceptance of the label that makes the difference to judgments, but the presumed collective acceptance of the knowledge that is assumed to come with that label. While the existence of this knowledge was denied in Experiment 1, entrenchment, explanatoriness ratings, and beliefs in a common cause moved in tandem, suggesting that assumptions about implied knowledge may have survived our explicit instructions (see Wilkenfeld et al., 2016, for similar results). Therefore, Experiment 2 tackles the question of whether entrenchment’s influence on judgments comes from its impact on perceptions of labels, or the implied collective acceptance of the knowledge that the labels are assumed to point to. We separately manipulate the expert community’s acceptance of causal information associated with entrenched categories to distinguish between these possibilities.

Method

Participants. Three hundred and sixty participants who passed an attention check were recruited using Amazon’s Mechanical Turk. The average age of participants was 36.35 years (SD = 11.38; range: 18 to 73). Two hundred and four participants were male and 155 female, with one participant choosing the “other” option for gender.

Materials and Dependent Measures. Disorder scenarios. Each participant was provided with six scenarios about either psychiatric or non-psychiatric disorders. Only two psychiatric disorders were identical to Experiment 1, but all disorders were chosen with the same principles in mind. The new psychiatric disorders included a category from DSM-5 (Derealization syndrome; American Psychiatric Association, 2013), a disorder included in DSM-IV but removed in later diagnostic manuals (piblokto; American Psychiatric Association, 1994), a commonly used category that has never appeared in the major diagnostic manuals (Diogenes syndrome; Clark et al., 1975), as well as a made-up syndrome adapted from similar disorders (pathological fear of winds). The six psychiatric disorders were chosen to provide coverage of anxiety, personality, dissociative, and culture-bound categories (see supplementary materials for the full text of all scenarios). Megacolon toxicum and pterygium served as the basis for medical disorders introduced in Experiment 2 (Warell, Cox & Firth, 2018). Counterbalancing was the same as Experiment 1.

Each scenario began with two experts observing patients with several symptoms. The experts either failed to find any record of the combination of symptoms and decided to name it themselves (unentrenched condition), or found the name for it accepted by the expert community (entrenched condition). For instance, an unentrenched scenario would begin:

Two psychiatrists encountered patients who had an unconventional fear of winds, obsessive thoughts about wind, and a feeling of detachment from themselves when encountering it. They searched for but found no previous record of this combination of symptoms and decided to name it favoniphobia themselves.

In the entrenched condition the last italicized sentence was replaced by: “They found that the name accepted by the community of mental health experts for the combination of these symptoms is favoniphobia.”

The causal coherence manipulation came next. To ensure plausibility, if the disorder label was not commonly used, the causal coherence information was presented as unentrenched as well, i.e., only known by the two characters in the scenario. In those conditions, the same two experts either found that the symptoms occurred together by accident in all of the patients and had no common cause (causally incoherent), or that they were produced by the same cause in every patient (causally coherent). For instance, in the causally incoherent condition participants read: “After studying the patients, they discovered that the symptoms occur together by accident and have no common cause”. The same scenario in the causally coherent condition read: “After studying the patients, they discovered that all of the symptoms are caused in every patient by emotionally difficult past experiences having coincided with strong winds.”

Whether the broader expert community had reached consensus about causal coherence of the category was manipulated for entrenched labels. In conditions where there was no community consensus, the entrenchment and coherence manipulations were followed by a sentence highlighting that the two characters have not shared their findings with anybody else yet. This was meant to ensure that the lack of common use or knowledge about the category in the unentrenched conditions was clear. In entrenched conditions with consensus, participants were provided with the same causal information and were told that the two experts found the common cause to be the expert community’s consensus about the disorder in question.

Similar to Experiment 1, it was stated that nothing else is known about the disorders. In all conditions, this was followed by a paragraph in which an expert previously unfamiliar with the label and its associated symptoms came across a patient with identical symptoms. One of the previously introduced experts responded to this new character’s request for an explanation of symptoms with the diagnostic label. Dependent measures identical to Experiment 1 followed the scenarios.

Comprehension check. Four questions similar to those used in Experiment 1 followed the last scenario.

Design and Procedure. Entrenchment, causal coherence, consensus about the coherence of entrenched labels were all manipulated within subjects, while type of disorder (psychiatric versus non-psychiatric) served as a between subjects factor. Each participant was shown six scenarios, one from each of the conditions created by the three within-subjects manipulations. A randomized Latin square was used to determine the pairing of disorders with conditions. An initial random order of presentation was determined. Then a Latin square design was used to create six distinct orders across participants. We included an open-ended question asking all participants how their ratings were determined. Other aspects of the design were identical to Experiment 1.

A repeated-measures 2 (unentrenched vs. entrenched) by 2 (causally incoherent vs. coherent category) ANOVA was performed on the four conditions without consensus about the cause on extent answered. Extent answered and common cause ratings were again significantly correlated (r = .46, p < .001). To see if accounting for common cause ratings would eliminate the entrenchment effect, an ANCOVA was performed with common cause measurements as a covariate. The main effects of entrenchment (F(1, 357) = 7.31, p = .007, ηp2= .02) and causal coherence (F(1, 357) = 7.07, p = .008, ηp2= .02) remained significant. Like Experiment 1, higher CRT resulted in lower overall ratings (high CRT: M = 3.22, SE = 0.09; low CRT: M = 3.93, SE = 0.13). Self-identified less knowledgeable participants showed a stronger entrenchment effect (low: M = 4.73, SE = 0.34; moderate: M = 3.66, SE = 0.13; high: M = 3.25, SE = 0.09).

Treatability ratings. Unlike Experiment 1, we found no main effect of entrenchment (p = .38) and also no interaction between entrenchment and causal coherence (p = .29) on treatability ratings. However, the main effect of causal coherence on treatability ratings was significant, F(1, 358) = 37.34, p < .001, ηp2= .09 (causally coherent: M = 4.51, SE = 0.05; causally incoherent: M = 4.13, SE = 0.06).

The main effects and interactions of consensus were insignificant across all dependent variables. See supplementary materials for details.

Additional analyses. Only 134 participants (37.2 percent) reported noticing any consistent pattern in their ratings, with 95 participants (26.4 percent) saying they perceived no trend in their responses and the rest expressing uncertainty. To see if explicit recollection of being affected by the entrenchment manipulation changes its impact, we excluded all 61 participants (16.9 percent) who mentioned it in any way in response to the open-ended question about the determinants of their ratings. The pattern of results was identical to what was reported above. Similar results were found when excluding the 18.33% and 10.83% of the participants who responded incorrectly to the implied knowledge and familiarity questions, respectively. Discussion

Entrenched diagnostic categories in Experiment 2 were deemed more explanatory, even when they represented haphazard combinations of symptoms with no causal coherence. The incomprehensibility of the label and its meaning to the explanation’s recipient did not eliminate the effect either, providing further confirmation that lack of informativeness does not prevent entrenchment from affecting ratings of explanation quality. As in Experiment 1, variations in disorder type, explicit memory of being impacted by the manipulations, responses to comprehension questions, self-reported domain knowledge and individual differences in responding did not eliminate the effect.

Direct provision of comprehensive mechanistic information did not render the entrenchment cue redundant either: While disorders that represented a unitary mechanism were judged better explanations, the effect was additive with that of the category’s entrenchment. This suggests that the role of entrenchment is not simply to substitute for impactful information when it is not readily available, but is rather impactful itself. Consensus about the causal information had little impact on the ratings. It may be, in line with Hemmatian and Sloman (2018), that the entrenchment effect applies to labels rather than the information they point to. A more mundane explanation is that too much information in the scenarios forced participants to focus on what they deemed most important, the category’s entrenchment and its causal coherence.

Entrenchment may not rely on causal information to affect judgments, but Experiment 2 provided further evidence that it can affect causal beliefs itself. Controlling for these beliefs weakened but did not eliminate the effect, suggesting that it is only one of the outcomes of label entrenchment. The inference that entrenched categories are more likely to represent common causes was in direct contradiction to information explicitly provided in causally incoherent conditions, a fact that most participants reported noticing in the scenarios. This finding highlights the resilience of the entrenchment effect and provides a possible explanation for similar findings by Wilkenfeld et al. (2016), who showed that incoherent disorders assigned labels due to an error were nevertheless seen as causally coherent: Their participants were introduced to common syndrome labels in all conditions and this entrenchment cue may have engendered a belief in causal coherence despite being told otherwise.

There are limits to the reach of entrenchment. Its effect on treatability ratings disappeared in Experiment 2, where ratings were better predicted by the causal coherence manipulation. This suggests that when available, causal information may be preferred for judgments like treatability (and unlike explanation quality) that focus exclusively on outcomes.

Experiment 3

So far our discussion has focused on the effect of entrenchment on lay judgment. Non-experts have less domain-specific knowledge than experts and may therefore need to rely on cues like entrenchment more. Higher self-reported knowledge did not interact with entrenchment in Experiment 1, but was associated with a stronger entrenchment effect in Experiment 2. The finding in Experiment 2 may be due to the performance overestimation that one sees in those with fewer skills (Dunning, 2011).

Experiment 3 examines whether experts show the entrenchment effect. Mental health professionals read two scenarios about made-up psychiatric disorders, introduced as obscure culture-bound syndromes to enhance plausibility. We manipulated whether labels were coined by two expert discoverers and not shared with the broader community yet, or if they were commonly used by experts focusing on the relevant class of disorders. We expected the experts to make better use of the limited information provided about the categories in their judgments of explanatory value and treatability, for instance by adjusting ratings based on the similarity of the symptoms we provided with familiar disorders, but nevertheless use entrenchment as a cue.

Method

Participants. One-hundred and fifty three self-identified mental health professionals were recruited using listservs of various professional organizations (including the Massachusetts branch of the American Psychological Association, the Rhode Island Psychological Association, and the Society for the Science of Clinical Psychology). No eligibility questions were asked. Eighteen participants did not finish the study. The average age was 46.21 years (SD = 14.36; range: 22 to 83). Ninety-eight participants were female (72.6 percent), the rest male.

Ninety-three participants (69 percent) had doctoral and 20 (15 percent) master’s degrees, while another 17 reported having earned professional degrees (13 percent). Five participants (four percent) reported a bachelor’s degree as their highest completed level of education. Clinical psychology was the most prevalent reported degree (60 participants, 44 percent). The average years of being active in the mental health professions was 18.23 years (SD = 12.62; range: 2 to 59).

Materials and dependent measures. Disorder scenarios. Each participant read two scenarios about psychiatric disorders adapted from anxiety and culture-bound disorder categories in Experiments 1 and 2 (American Psychiatric Association, 2013; see supplementary materials).

Each scenario began with two experts documenting a combination of symptoms in several patients living in an isolated location. The choice of the location was meant to explain why participants had not heard of the disorder and why nothing other than the associated symptoms is known about it. The patients’ significant functional impairment was highlighted to be in line with the criteria for psychodiagnosis according to DSM-5 (American Psychiatric Association, 2013). In the entrenched condition, the characters learned that while the category is not a part of major diagnostic guides, it has a commonly used label among experts who specialize in the specified region. In the unentrenched condition, the two experts were the first to document the syndrome and decided to coin a term for it. In both conditions, the information about the label was shared with a named colleague who would later serve as the recipient of the explanation. Finally, to discourage inferring any categorical information beyond what was presented, participants in all conditions were told that nothing else is known about the category. A conversation involving the diagnostic label similar to Experiments 1 and 2 followed. Extent answered and treatability dependent measures (worded as before) were presented on the same page. For instance, the entrenched condition read:

Two mental health professionals observed several patients living in an isolated island in the Pacific Ocean. The patients had persistent worries about being controlled by parasites, fear of exposure to water, and sensations of insects crawling under their skin. The symptoms had caused significant functional impairment for them. Looking it up in the literature, they learned that while not included in any major diagnostic manual, this combination of symptoms has commonly been referred to as perreptophobia by professionals focusing on that region of the world. They shared the symptoms and the name with their colleague John. Nothing else is currently known about perreptophobia.

Later, John came across a patient with the same complaints while studying the island's population. He asked one of the first two professionals: "Why does he show these symptoms?" She responded: "Because he has perreptophobia."

The unentrenched condition was identical, except the sentences in italics were replaced by: “The professionals were the first to document this combination of symptoms and decided by themselves to name it perreptophobia. They shared the symptoms and the name with their colleague John, but have not told any other colleagues yet.” The last sentence was intended to prevent participants from inferring that the label could in any way be entrenched in the community.

Design and Procedure. Entrenchment was the only independent variable in Experiment 3, manipulated within-subjects. The order of presentation and pairing of disorders with conditions was counterbalanced. To keep the experiment short, no attention check, comprehension question or reflectiveness measure was included. Demographic questions followed the scenarios. The final demographic question asked for participants’ degree of belief in the validity of psychodiagnostic categorization, given that not all experts agree with psychodiagnostic constructs and this disbelief can affect their explanation ratings (see, for example, Allsopp, Read, Corcoran & Kinderman, 2019).

Results

Extent answered. In contrast with Experiments 1 and 2 where the condition means hovered between three and four on the 7-point scale, the mean rating in Experiment 3 did not surpass two in any condition (see Figure 3), suggesting that experts largely found the explanations inadequate (see Supplementary Materials for scenario means). Fifty-nine percent of all ratings were at the very bottom of the scale, with 23 percent of the remaining ratings being two. Furthermore, 85 percent of participants gave the same rating to both scenarios presented to them, showing a strong preference for not changing their ratings according to the transparent entrenchment manipulation.

Nevertheless, a univariate repeated-measures ANOVA (unentrenched vs. entrenched label) showed a significant within-subjects difference between the entrenched and unentrenched conditions, F(1, 133) = 4.53, p = .035, ηp2 = .03. There was no effect of assignment of disorders to conditions (p = .66) and no interaction between the assignments and the entrenchment manipulation (p = .40). The entrenchment effect was much smaller in size than in Experiments 1 and 2, possibly due in part to the floor effect (unentrenched: M = 1.62, SE = 0.08, entrenched: M = 1.80, SE = 0.08). Excluding participants based on proclaimed disbelief in the utility of psychodiagnostic categories (lower than midpoint rating on the relevant survey question) made little difference to the means (unentrenched: M = 1.64, SE = 0.11, entrenched: M = 1.82, SE = 0.13). This exclusion, however, rendered the entrenchment effect marginally significant due to a lack of statistical power, F(1,88) = 3.46, p = .066, ηp2 = .04.

Adding order of presentation as a between subjects variable eliminated the main effect of entrenchment  (p = .61), but revealed a significant interaction between order and the entrenchment manipulation (F(1,131) = 4.37, p = .039, ηp2 = .03). Figure 3 shows why. Looking at only the first scenario that each participant rated, we find a between subjects difference between entrenched and unentrenched ratings close in size to the effects in Experiments 1 and 2 (F(1,131) = 8.06, p = .005, ηp2 = .06; unentrenched: M = 1.43, SE = 0.11; entrenched: M = 1.94, SE = 0.14). The changes in average ratings between the first and the second scenarios are in line with the label entrenchment hypothesis, but by the time participants have rated their second scenario, the between-subjects difference becomes insignificant (p = 0.42; unentrenched: M = 1.65, SE = 0.15, entrenched: M = 1.80, SE = 0.13). A similar order effect emerged after excluding participants who expressed general disbelief in the utility of psychodiagnosis, but did not reach significance presumably due to the smaller sample size, F(1,87) = 1.66, p = .20, ηp2 = .02 (first scenario, unentrenched: M = 1.51, SE = 0.15; first scenario, entrenched: M = 1.98, SE = 0.19; second scenario, unentrenched: M = 1.65, SE = 0.18; second scenario, entrenched: M = 1.76, SE = 0.17). This may reflect a tendency by expert participants to maintain their initial ratings once the experimental manipulation became clear.

Average ratings for the first and second randomly assigned scenarios that participants rated (horizontal axis), with respect to the degree to which the label answers a question seeking an explanation for associated symptoms. Colors represent whether the diagnostic label in the scenario was entrenched. The error bars indicate the 95% confidence intervals for marginal means of different conditions.

Treatability ratings. There was no main effect of label entrenchment on treatability ratings (p = .78), but an interaction between entrenchment and disorders reached significance (F(1,131) = 6.62, p = .011, ηp2 = .05). There was no effect of order of presentation (p = .11). The midpoint of the scale was by far the most common response (36 percent of all responses), suggesting that experts may have withheld judgment about the syndromes’ treatability given the dearth of information.

Discussion

Laypeople in Experiments 1 and 2 found the uninformative explanations less than adequate, as evidenced by lower than midpoint average responses. Experts in Experiment 3, in contrast, found them highly unsatisfactory as evidenced by the near floor average ratings. Judgments remain low even after excluding those who do not believe in the utility of diagnostic labels, perhaps due to experts’ heightened awareness of the circularity of our labels.

The vast majority of experts gave the same response to both of the scenarios they read, possibly reflecting an explicit belief that the transparent manipulation should not affect their ratings. Nevertheless, a significant within-subjects entrenchment effect emerged due to responses to the first item. Looking only at the first scenario each participant rated, a between-subjects effect similar in size to the previous Experiments was found. The difference between conditions, however, diminished in the second scenario. Experts are susceptible to the entrenchment effect, but also may be more likely to actively monitor their evaluation criteria when attention is brought to the entrenchment manipulation.

General Discussion

Three experiments found that unfamiliar diagnostic labels used by a community of expert diagnosticians were judged as better answers to questions about symptoms than labels not “entrenched” in a community, even when the labels were demonstrably circular. This entrenchment effect was remarkably robust, occurring regardless of whether the labels carried concrete causal or symptomatic information, whether the diagnoses concerned psychiatric or non-psychiatric disorders, and whether participants were laypeople found on the internet or mental health professionals. We ruled out the recipient’s familiarity with the label or lack thereof, violations of conversational norms, and lack of reflectiveness or attentiveness as explanations for the results. We obtained a robust effect of entrenchment despite participants’ report that it did not affect them.

The findings have important implications for clinical practice. They show, for instance, that a clinician’s use of diagnostic jargon during a consultation, implied to involve commonly accepted terms, may have far-reaching and potentially unintended effects on the recipient’s perception of their condition. It can affect how they explain and consequently attempt to manage issues, and even how hopeful they are about the possibility of overcoming it. A clinician using the term schizophrenia to describe a patient’s symptoms, for instance, may use it as a shorthand and harbor no belief that all schizophrenic patients would share even one symptom, let alone the same etiology or prognosis. The patient’s intuitive understanding of such an entrenched diagnostic term, however, might be very different and--more insidiously--resistant to informational interventions. It is therefore of utmost importance to carefully articulate what clinical terms mean and what they do not.

Due diligence in clarifying the knowledge contained within diagnostic terms is not only in the interest of lay people subjected to them, but also important in guiding the intuitions of clinicians themselves. Diagnostic labels were considered valuable to the degree they were used by a community, despite a lack of informational content, even among mental health practitioners in our sample. Labels are likely to be frequently used if they fit the community’s shared narratives, which in the domain of diagnosis may include classic anecdotes from clinical practice. This means that the explanatory value of a category can depend more on such narratives than on evidential support. In that sense, our results show that narratives are important to determining the perceived value of a label, not just evidence. If an entrenched term like “Ganser syndrome” is supported by clinical narratives, then it may retain some of its explanatory value even after its validity is undermined by solid evidence. This may help explain the experts’ resistance to removal of long-standing disorders from APA’s influential diagnostic manual (Pull, 2013).

Was the effect due to the labels being entrenched in a community of experts or to the underlying diagnostic categories being entrenched? The experiments in this paper do not distinguish these accounts. However, our previous work (Hemmatian & Sloman, 2018) testing various category types offers a suggestion: There, we manipulated whether categories were labeled or not independent of entrenchment and found that the effect depends on the label rather than the category.

Whatever its source, label entrenchment appears to exert its influence partly by inducing a belief that a unifying causal mechanism underlies the diagnostic category, even when such a belief contradicts other known facts. Nonetheless, there is more to the entrenchment effect than implied causality: The effect did not distinguish between psychiatric and non-psychiatric categories even though degrees of belief in a common cause varied significantly between the two types of disorders. Furthermore, entrenchment contributed significantly to the ratings in Experiments 1 and 2 even after controlling for common cause beliefs.

Are these effects evidence of irrationality? On one hand, there is nothing wrong with relying on one’s community for knowledge when judging an explanation. Indeed, more often than not, we are forced to do exactly that (Sloman & Fernbach, 2017). Individuals cannot all know even how the appliances in our kitchens work, let alone complex issues of health. There is, therefore, nothing wrong with taking cues from the experts in one’s community. Experts should not require such cues to the same extent, and we found that mental health practitioners do rely less on their community when asked to make these judgments in Experiment 3. To some extent, they determined that the labels had little explanatory value, whether they were entrenched or not.

On the other hand, all of our samples showed a significant tendency to judge labels that carried no information because they were circular or internally incoherent as more explanatory if they were entrenched in a community. Common usage of a label hardly seems sufficient to infuse it with explanatory value; some informational content seems necessary. In that sense, we believe the groups we tested on average committed an error: They relied on social cues that should have been ignored. The sole justification that we can think of for such behavior, at least on the part of our expert participants, rests on the belief that entrenched categories carry more future inductive potential than unentrenched categories. Perhaps in the future some property of the diagnostic category will be discovered that gives the label value, and perhaps that is more likely if the community already uses the label than if it does not. But that is an unlikely prospect for the incoherent categories laypeople rated in our second experiment and hardly justifies elevated valuations of a label with no current informational value merely because others use it.

How can we heed Kant’s warning and not imbue diagnostic labels with undeserved utility? The current experiments provide some hints. The effect of entrenchment among laypeople was partially mediated by causal beliefs (see also Giffin et al., 2017). When causal information was provided to participants directly in Experiment 2, it eliminated the effect of entrenchment on treatability beliefs. It therefore seems that lay individuals would use causal information in their reasoning if available in ways that mitigate the impact of label entrenchment. Attempts to impart this knowledge, however, should consider the propensity we observed for developing causal beliefs that contradict other known information (see also Wilkenfeld et al., 2016).

More accurate causal information about diagnostic categories serves as one explanation for the smaller entrenchment effect among mental health professionals in Experiment 3. Cooper and Marsh (2015) provide one possible mechanism for this effect by showing that more expertise in psychodiagnosis comes with weaker causal assumptions. If there is no causal coherence to a category, it will be a worse container for knowledge regardless of the label’s common use.

Finally, expertise may come with heightened scrutiny of the sources of one’s judgments, as evidenced by the order effect observed in Experiment 3. This suggests that it is possible to actively suppress the effect of label entrenchment once attention is brought to it. Therefore, the mere knowledge of entrenchment’s impact may help us remember that knowing what to call a condition is far from understanding it.
