# Values and Consequences: Frames for Korean Reunification

## Abstract (from OpenAlex metadata)

Using the results of detailed surveys conducted by National Institute for Korean Unification in 2018 and 2019 with representative national samples of South Koreans, we argue that value-derived rules and consequences of policies are useful for conceptualizing different types of attitudes toward Korean unification.

Values and Consequences: Frames for Korean Reunification

Babak Hemmatian, Steven A. Sloman

Department of Cognitive, Linguistic and Psychological Sciences, Brown University

If you are a South Korean, chances are you have recently discussed Korean reunification with someone and have some opinion about it, one way or another. South Korean citizens, even if they are non-experts and have not spent too much time exploring the topic of reunification, are often called upon to make decisions that are informed by it, as they do when they vote for political parties with a stance on reunification. It is therefore imperative to think more carefully about where attitudes toward reunification come from.

We will use the results of surveys conducted by the Korean Institute for National Unification (KINU) to shed light on the most common reasons South Koreans cite for their attitudes. These are surveys administered to a representative sample of South Korean citizens in 2018 and 2019. We will discuss what underlies those attitudes, and how it may differ from what citizens believe led them to their stance. But first, we need to review which views in support of or against reunification are commonly expressed.

In the 2018 survey, about 70 percent of participants believed there was at least some motivation for Korean reunification, with the rest of the participants considering it unnecessary. In the 2019 survey, the percentage of those in favor of reunification dropped to 61 percent. We know from other recent surveys (e.g. the ASAN survey, Jiyoon et al., 2015, pp. 10-11,18) that younger South Koreans show declining interest in reunification. This may be partly to blame for the difference between the 2018 and 2019 KINU surveys as well, since only slightly more than a third of participants in the 2018 survey were younger than 40 years old, compared with about half of the participants in 2019. Moreover, support for reunification in both KINU surveys is lower than what the earlier ASAN survey found, suggesting an overall growing divide in public opinion. To better understand such changes in public attitude, it would be helpful to look at the reasons South Koreans cite for their stance on reunification. Figure 1 shows the most important reasons cited by the 1000 South Koreans who participated in KINU 2018.

Figure 1. The percentage of individuals in KINU 2018 survey who chose each option as the most important reason for their attitude toward reunification.

The three most common reasons offer quite different motivations for reunification. That the people of the two Koreas represent a single community is contrasted with fear of negative outcomes like war and beliefs in positive outcomes such as a more advanced South Korea. More specifically, the latter two reasons focus on concrete consequences of reunification policies, while the “Koreans as one people” reason highlights a sincerely held belief that is more removed from the direct outcomes of reunification on, for example, the South Korean economy. Could the differences in the types of arguments be related to the growing divide in reunification attitudes?

How Stances are Formed

Thinking About the Consequences: It’s Complicated

More than half of the people participating in KINU 2018 cited South Korea’s prosperity and the threat of war as the most important reasons for their stance. In other words, most believed that their attitudes are informed by knowledge of the expected consequences of reunification or lack thereof. The abstract argument in such cases would look like the following:

If reunification will or is likely to lead to a more advanced South Korea, then we must unify the two Koreas. Reunification will or is likely to lead to a more advanced South Korea. Therefore, we must unify the two Koreas.

However, the world is a complex place and so are the outcomes of reunification policies. Let us look more closely at “a more advanced South Korea”. It is unclear which type of advancement this refers to. Let us assume for the sake of argument that participants were thinking of economic growth and advancement. Take a moment to think: How much do you know about the impact of reunification on the South Korean economy? Would it result in slower economic growth because of subsidizing the North Korean economy, or faster growth because of corporations’ easier access to a cheaper and more convenient workforce and a larger market? Would it drive inflation up or down? What about bank interest rates? What would the combined effect of economic growth and inflation be on the interest rates and how would that translate to changes in everyday expenses? Would each possible effect be long-lasting or not?

As this example illustrates, many factors affect whether a policy will have a positive or negative outcome. Furthermore, positive outcomes in one domain may come with negative outcomes in others and vice versa. For instance, the political influence provided to North Korean parties in the event of full reunification may impact the Korean economy in ways that would not be uncovered by looking at only the market forces. While information exists out there about many of these associations, it has been known for decades that the average voter often knows very little about major policies (Somin, 2006, pp. 255-256). This point is not limited to political literacy: There is a growing realization that barring a small number of experts on a certain topic, most individuals are largely ignorant of the workings of even the objects they interact with daily, let alone broader societal trends (Sloman & Fernbach, 2017, pp. 19-36,171-193).

Furthermore, even if the barriers constraining our knowledge were conquered, some uncertainty in the consequences of reunification will remain. For instance, an unexpected natural disaster can upend the expectations borne of careful economic analysis. Even the experts among us may therefore not have all the answers. Similar considerations arise with the other stated reasons for attitudes. For instance, what effects would the unexpected death of the North Korean leader have on within-party politics and therefore the probability of a war between the two Koreas? There are therefore serious constraints on what one knows and can know about any of the effects of reunification policies.

The complexity of the problem is compounded by the fact that there is no consensus regarding what “unification” even means. Consider the UK’s debates about leaving the European Union. There is sharp disagreement in the UK about what it would mean to leave the EU. What it would mean to unify is an issue that is even more fraught with complexity. When participants in KINU 2019 were asked how much they agreed with alternative meanings of the term, about a third showed a clear preference for the following item:

In accordance with the Constitution of unified Korea, a single government and a single National Assembly have been established through elections, integrating the two regimes’ organizations and systems.

However, as can be seen in Figure 2, many others either clearly preferred an entirely different alternative, were indifferent between the various options, or showed a more complicated pattern of preference. This highlights the lack of agreement within the South Korean population about what reunification means.

Figure 2. The percentage of individuals in KINU 2019 survey who showed a clear preference for one of four meanings of reunification, had no preference, or showed a more complicated response pattern.

How did most South Koreans reach their firm beliefs about the consequences of reunification despite differing notions of unification, the complexity of their outcomes, and even though most participants (about three out of four people in KINU 2019) had not met a single North Korean in their lives? Is it possible that the source of South Koreans’ beliefs may not be accurate knowledge of reunification’s consequences but something else that South Koreans themselves are not aware of?

Values Instead of Consequences

One way in which voters simplify their decision-making process and reach their stances despite the overwhelming complexity of social issues is by relying on rules they derive from their values. Values are what people believe are important to the way they live. The rules derived from values apply to actions/decisions and cast entire classes of options as right or wrong, permissible or not, based on one or a few simple criteria (Baron & Spranca, 1997, 1-16). Take, for instance, the “Koreans are one people” argument that a third of South Koreans presented as the most important reason for their stance. The abstract form of this argument would look something like the following:

If Koreans are one people, then they must be united under one nation. The Koreans are one people. Therefore, we must unify the separated Korean states under one nation.

That “Koreans are one people” is a value that results in a rule to evaluate actions by. The last sentence provides the rule that greatly simplifies the decision-making in this case: If reunification is cast as a sacred duty to one’s nation, any option that does not include reunification to some extent is unacceptable and need not be examined. More important to our discussion, knowing the exact consequences of reunification, for instance on the South Korean economy, may not matter as much as the value of the action of reunification itself. This is because nowhere in the rule is the imperative for reunification conditioned on any outcome, whether negative or positive. By applying this rule one can get around one’s lack of concrete knowledge and yet have a principled way to take a position on this issue.

What are the values that such simplifying rules are derived from? Our identities and abstract ideas about what our ideal world would look like provides fertile ground for developing them. Holding the ethnic Korean identity close to one’s sense of self, for instance, can make it easier to accept the “Koreans are one people” argument for reunification no matter what. Fundamental beliefs about how the world should work provide further constraints on acceptable options. For instance, commitment to pacifism can rule out reunification methods that lead to significant loss of life. The sources of our identities and values are multifarious, adding to the richness of this resource for developing a stance: Religious beliefs, political ideology and abstract constructs like tradition or honor are other examples of sources from which value-derived rules can be drawn.

Do South Koreans rely on value-derived rules that focus on permissibility of options in the context of reunification? Figure 3 gives one example of the heterogeneity in values among KINU 2019 participants and how it relates to attitudes toward reunification. Twenty four percent of participants reported being politically conservative, 44 percent were centrist and the remaining 32 percent were progressives. Such differences in political values may then lead to disparities in the decision-making rules that are derived, consequently affecting attitudes toward reunification. As can be seen, while there is a majority of pro-reunification people in every group, the difference in favor of reunification is perceptibly greater among the centrists and the progressives.

Figure 3. The number of individuals in KINU 2019 survey (out of 1300 people) who were in favor of or against reunification, as a function of self-identified political ideology.

Political ideology reflects only one set of values. Are South Koreans driven in their stances to reunification by rules derived from values more broadly construed? When asked the following questions, very few participants in the KINU 2019 survey denied reliance on steadfast values, such as moral principles. In fact, a majority admitted being at least moderately driven in their reunification attitudes by such principles (adapted from Tanner et al., 2008, pp. 768-769):

This alternative is consistent with principles one has to follow.

I have a moral obligation to have this attitude.

Some attitudes are definitely right or wrong, irrespective of the consequence.

These statements reflect rules to follow in the sense that they suggest they should be followed without exception. They are not merely stating consequences that one hopes to achieve. We used Cronbach’s alpha to examine how close the three items are to one another in terms of patterns of responding across participants. We obtained a value of 0.71, providing acceptable confidence that the three items measure the same construct. We then used a statistical method called Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to extract one score for each participant along a singular dimension that best represented their responses to all three questions. That score, which explained 64 percent of the variability in answers to individual questions, represents each participant’s level of reliance on value-derived rules in determining attitudes toward reunification.

Based on the content of specific values, it is possible that the net effect of relying on them across the population would be either in favor of reunification or against it. For instance, focusing on a unified Korean identity would presumably promote pro-reunification attitudes, as it casts North Koreans as part of one’s own group whose integrity should be maintained. Alternatively, a focus on free market ideals may do the opposite, for instance encouraging more negative views of subsidizing the North Korean economy through government regulations that may follow reunification. To tease apart the effects of various values on reunification attitudes, we needed a more sophisticated attitude measure than yes/no answers to one question can afford. We therefore performed an exploratory PCA on responses to the following questions, where possible answers ranged from strongly disagree to strongly agree:

It is necessary that South Korea and North Korea form a single country

It is necessary that South Korea and North Korea maintain their status as two states while ensuring free movement and making one market

While they maintain their status as two states, South Korea and North Korea should make an alliance to establish joint government organizations.

It is my sincere hope that South Korea and North Korea will form a single country.

I feel good when I think about South Korea and North Korea becoming a single country.

It does not matter even if South Korea and North Korea maintain their status as two states unless it causes me significant harm.

If there is no risk of war, South Korea and North Korea do not need to form a single country.

South Koreans and North Koreans can live well in two different states.

As South Korea and North Korea are the same ethnic group, they should form a single country.

It will benefit South Korea if South Korea and North Korea form a single country.

It will benefit me and my family if South Korea and North Korea form a single country.

A Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.7 provided acceptable evidence that the items measure a singular concept, i.e. the desire for reunification. However, the PCA analysis suggested two prominent independent patterns of responding. Higher scores along one extracted dimension strongly predicted disagreement with items 6, 7 and 8 (average Pearson’s correlation coefficient, r, was -0.51) and strong agreement with all of the other statements (average r = 0.67). This score, accounting for 44 percent of the variability, represents a stance that we will henceforth call attached reunification attitude: The idea that a unified Korea is a desirable outcome and the current separated state is undesirable and unacceptable, or vice versa. More positive values on this dimension show stronger attachment to reunification, while more negative values suggest the opposite. The scores along the second dimension, accounting for about twenty percent more of the variability, identify what we will call detached reunification attitude. High scores along this dimension were not associated with the belief that either reunification or separation are necessary (unrelated to items 1, 4 and 9, average r = 0.06), but were associated with strong agreement that the status quo is acceptable (items 6, 7, and 8, r = 0.63), and so is a unified Korea (the remaining items, average r = 0.37). In other words, these are individuals who do not feel attached to the outcome of the reunification debate one way or another.

Is heavier reliance on value-derived rules related to these two types of reunification attitudes? Such reliance was a strong predictor of attached pro-reunification attitudes (r = 0.32, p < 0.01). Using a participant’s reliance on value-derived rules to predict their attached attitude in a linear regression explained ten percent of all variability in individual responses (p < 0.001), a significant amount given the breadth of the reunification topic and the various influences on attitudes. The same was not true of detached reunification attitudes (p = 0.64).

One potential reason for this result comes from our earlier discussion of ignorance regarding policies’ consequences: South Koreans know from decades of experience what separation looks like, but no reunification process has occurred in Korea in recent memory to help them form firm expectations about its outcomes. The unification of Western and Eastern Germany in the 1990s may provide one point of comparison. Nevertheless, as Niederhafner-- an expert on Korean reunification-- notes, there are as many differences between the circumstances of pre-unification Germany and pre-unification Korea as there are similarities (Niederhafner, 2013, pp. 270-280). For example, the harsh South Korean laws against associations with the North Korean regime may complicate the integration of the two Koreas in a way that post-unification Germany did not experience. As a result, there is greater uncertainty associated with the outcomes of reunification than that of the separation of the two Koreas. It is a well-known fact that uncertainty in the outcomes of decisions is aversive, especially if the outcomes can be negative (Kahneman & Tversky, 1986, p. 264). If the decision is to be based on concrete consequences of policies, one recourse from this aversive uncertainty would be relying on the default, the status quo, which in the case of reunification would mean supporting separation. However, adherence to pro-reunification values and deriving one’s decision-making rules from them can help people take the leap of faith and accept the uncertain outcomes of reunification. Value-based rules are about how people should act--not about how to achieve outcomes--thus they allow decisions to be made independently of outcomes, even unknown outcomes. Therefore, pro-reunification attitudes--especially those that are accompanied by negative views on the current situation--would be expected to rely more on values like the importance of Korean ethnicity.

Earlier we mentioned how political ideologies are related to overall attitudes toward reunification. Are there differences between political ideologies in terms of how they inform attached and detached attitudes? Figure 4 shows the percentage of self-identified conservatives, centrists or progressives in KINU 2019 who scored in top and bottom halves of the attached reunification attitude scale if we ignore the valence of their attitude. There is a slight tendency for the conservatives to be in the high attached attitude category. This difference is more pronounced among the progressives. The centrists, on the other hand, are more likely to be in the low attached attitude category. These trends suggest that both progressives and conservatives draw upon political ideology to determine the value-derived rules that inform attached attitudes more than centrists do.

Figure 4. Percentage of participants in KINU 2019 who scored in the top 50% of the sample with respect to attached reunification attitudes, separated by political ideology.

Figure 5 shows the same measure for detached attitudes across the political spectrum. Individuals across the political spectrum are more evenly distributed between the high and low detached attitude categories than we saw in Figure 4. This suggests that political ideology informs attached attitudes more strongly than detached attitudes.

Values Can Embolden Us to Make Less Informed but More Uncompromising Decisions

So far, we have discussed how South Koreans rely at least to some extent on value-derived rules to determine their attitudes toward reunification and that these rules can often be distinct from knowledge of the consequences of policies. Assuming all of this is true, a valid question would be if there is anything wrong with reliance on value-derived rules in decision-making. The overwhelming complexity of the world and consequently the options we need to choose from necessitates the use of such rules. Nevertheless, there are reasons to worry about overreliance on them, as they can lead to a less informed citizenry. This can happen in several ways that we now discuss in turn.

Value-derived rules can stifle interest in learning

Taking the perspective of people outside of our immediate communities can be helpful for thinking about complex issues from various angles and coming to a better understanding of them. Several questions in KINU 2019 asked participants about the degree to which they attempt to think about reunification from alternative perspectives:

When I watch the news about issues on the Korean Peninsula, I try to think from the perspective of North Korea.

I am interested in receiving information that contains the perspectives of European countries, which are somewhat different from South Korean views on inter-Korean conflict.

I am willing to watch movies or read books that reflect North Korea’s perspective on inter-Korean conflict.

I am willing to read the North Korean newspaper ‘Rodong Sinmun’ or watch the news on the North Korean broadcasting company ‘Korean Central News Agency’ to know how they cover the issue of denuclearization.

In a PCA analysis, one dimension was enough to capture 64 percent of the variance in responses to individual items and the resulting scale had good reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.81), suggesting that attempts to learn more about inter-Korea issues by assuming the other group’s perspective or channel is strongly associated with the willingness to consider other perspectives and media. Any of these sources of information would be invaluable in better understanding the probability and expected outcomes of separation versus reunification, regardless of one’s overall attitude toward reunification. However, reliance on values did not consistently encourage gathering information from alternative sources. While certain values encouraged these kinds of information gathering, other values did the exact opposite. We will discuss which specific values belong to each category later in the chapter. For now, it suffices to say that greater reliance on value-derived rules was a predictor of attempting to learn more about inter-Korea relations from any source among those in favor of reunification (attached: r = 0.45, p < 0.01, detached: r = 0.21, p < 0.01), but a similar tendency correlated with less interest in learning about the inter-Korean conflict among those against reunification (r = -0.13, p < 0.01). In other words, depending on which values one holds dear, greater reliance on value-derived rules may produce less interest in becoming informed about inter-Korean issues.

Values may lead to immature decisions through illusory simplicity and sense of understanding

Even when there is interest in understanding inter-Korea relations, reliance on value-derived rules can result in an illusory sense of understanding that hinders the true accumulation of knowledge. To see why, we need to first discuss what an illusion of understanding in the domain of policy outcomes entails. In one of several studies on this topic, Fernbach and colleagues (2013, pp. 939-946) asked participants in the United States to rate the degree to which they understood what the specific outcomes of various high-profile policies would be, for instance imposing unilateral sanctions on Iran for its nuclear program. They were then asked to write down, in as much detail as they could, what those outcomes may be and how they would come about. For instance, one may describe in detail their belief that imposing harsher sanctions would lead to financial hardships for Iranian families, who would then impose pressure on the Iranian government not to pursue its nuclear program. Alternatively, one may believe that harsher sanctions would induce more negative attitudes toward the United States among Iranians, giving hardliners more leeway to pursue the nuclear program despite the financial hardships that ensue. The actual content of the descriptions, however, was not the focus of Fernbach and colleagues’ study: They were interested in how participants would re-rate their knowledge after the attempt to write the detailed descriptions. Most participants gave themselves lower ratings after than before the attempt, admitting that they did not know as much about the topic as they thought they did. Not only that, but they became less extreme in their professed support for or against the policies.

It is not difficult to see how similar arguments may be provided with respect to the North Korean nuclear program and other important topics surrounding Korean reunification. It is natural, for instance, for people to have an intuitive sense that the repercussions of North Korea having nuclear weapons is straightforward. But when one thinks about it, and tries to explain it, one discovers that it is actually quite a complex issue and hence opaque. Therefore, while this effect is yet to be shown with policies in South Korea, it stands to reason that a similar illusion of understanding would be at work in determining what South Koreans expect the outcomes of reunification policies to be.

You may wonder what such illusions of understanding have to do with reliance on value-derived rules. By excluding many potentially relevant pieces of information from the decision-making process, such rules make the problem seem simpler: If one’s stance on reunification does not depend on the economic outcomes of it, one may be tempted to think that those outcomes do not matter and, given enough time, one might even forget that the outcomes exist as relevant pieces of information. Reliance on value-derived rules that are often detached from consequences can thus make us feel like we knew a greater proportion of what can be known about the policies even when we do not.

Does the KINU 2019 survey give us reasons to believe that such a process is at work in determining attitudes toward reunification? Are those whose attitudes are more based on values more likely to think that re-unification is simple and that they understand its implications? To find out, we constructed a measure of sense of simplicity based on a composite PCA measure of responses to the following statements (explaining 77% of the variance, Cronbach’s alpha=0.69):

To me, the meaning of unification policies toward North Korea, such as denuclearization and the peace process, and their influence on the Korean Peninsula…

are not that complicated.

are easy to understand.

How much people relied on rules derived from values was indeed a predictor of this measure of simplicity (r = 0.13, p < 0.01). There was a stronger association between the sense of simplicity and unwillingness to compromise those same values (r = 0.22, p < 0.01), where the unwillingness was determined based on a composite measure of responses to the following items (adapted from Tanner et al., 2008, pp. 768-769; explaining 64% of the variance, Cronbach’s alpha=0.72):

This issue is about something we should not sacrifice.

I would not change my opinion, no matter what the costs.

I would have problems making any concessions on this topic.

In other words, relying on values to determine attitudes made it more likely that the issue would be considered easy to understand, and this sense of simplicity in turn strengthened the commitment to those same values.

The same tendency to see the issue as simple was associated with a greater sense of understanding not only regarding reunification, but more broadly with respect to the politics of the Korean peninsula (r =0.41 , p < 0.01), gauged using responses to the following items (explaining 65% of the variance, Cronbach’s alpha=0.72):

I have a deep understanding of the meaning of unification policies toward North Korea, such as denuclearization and the peace process, and how they influence the Korean Peninsula.

I well understand the important political issues Korea is facing.

I know more about politics or administration than most other Koreans.

Exploratory PCA analysis showed that the sense of understanding and simplicity are dissociable (two distinct dimensions corresponding to them arose when performing PCA on all five items). Nevertheless, both of these variables were correlated with reliance on values and unwillingness to compromise them (both r = 0.17, p < 0.01).

The senses of simplicity and understanding in turn contributed to stronger attached pro-reunification attitudes (r = 0.20, p < 0.01). In other words, they may have made it easier to take the leap of faith that the uncertainty associated with reunification demands. To see if the sense of simplicity mediates the relationship between focus on values and the unwillingness to compromise them on one hand, and attitudes toward reunification on the other, we performed a hierarchical linear regression analysis, where simplicity and understanding were added first to predict attitudes, and value-based rules and unwillingness to compromise were included in the model afterward. Simplicity and sense of understanding were initially significant predictors of attached reunification attitudes (0.04 of variance explained). Accounting for value-derived rules and the unwillingness to compromise them in the second step added to the amount of explained variance (0.14 of variance explained), but also rendered the connection between simplicity and attitudes insignificant (p = 0.29). This suggests that reliance on value-derived rules induces beliefs that the reunification issue is easy to understand, which in turn hardens attached attitudes in favor of reunification. Values and the unwillingness to compromise, however, also contributed to attitudes in ways that were independent of the sense of understanding and simplicity, highlighting the multi-faceted nature of these influences.

Not only can an illusory sense of simplicity and understanding lead to more entrenched beliefs, it may encourage people to take bolder action in support of their attitudes. For instance, Fernbach and colleagues (2013) found that puncturing U.S. participants’ illusory sense of understanding engendered greater reluctance to contribute to lobby groups fighting for their professed beliefs. While conviction is not necessarily a bad thing, ill-informed convictions can prevent people who have the greatest need for information from seeking it out, creating a cycle of ill-advised belief trends with serious consequences (Fernbach et al., 2019, pp. 251-256).

The KINU 2019 survey did not directly measure participants’ knowledge about inter-Korea issues and therefore it is impossible to disentangle true from illusory knowledge in this case. Nevertheless, the results we have reported are in line with the broader psychological literature demonstrating shallow and illusory understanding (Sloman & Fernbach, 2017). They therefore highlight at the very least the possibility that many participants think they understand the issues surrounding reunification better than they do, with potentially important practical consequences.

Values can make attitudes resistant to conflicting evidence

Reliance on value-derived rules does not only affect the amount of information taken in and how quickly decisions are made based on that information, but also how one reacts to conflicting evidence. Such rules often reflect values that we are reluctant to give up, regardless of what evidence says about them. It may therefore come as no surprise that we found a strong association between dependence on value-derived rules and unwillingness to accept compromises in the KINU 2019 survey (r = 0.64, p < 0.01).

Why are rules for action resistant to the trade-offs that are inevitable when one compromises? One explanation is that the usefulness of rules for simplifying information gathering and decision-making depends on their simplicity: If rules are not broad enough or have too many addendums, one might as well do away with them and base one’s stance on the complicated consequences of policies. Another explanation revolves around the emotional charge often associated with one’s identity and values.

Whatever the reason, the reluctance to make any concessions on value-derived rules means that even information that is obtained and deemed relevant to an issue may end up having no impact on how someone behaves. Such reluctance would explain why among the quarter of participants who had encountered North Korean defectors in KINU 2019, there was no reliable impact of this experience on attitudes one way or another (attached and pro-reunification: r = 0.03, detached and pro-reunification: r = 0.01, attached and anti-reunification: r = 0.03, detached and anti-reunification: r = 0.01, all p > 0.05). Even though such encounters can serve as rich sources of information about North Korea and the inter-Korean conflict, it may be that those already in favor of reunification would not change their mind based on the encounter and neither would those who are already against it, as their stances are already guided by uncompromising values.

Values can promote wishful thinking about consequences

Finally, it is a general human tendency to expect that one’s values and the imperatives that result from them lead to better consequences, in ways that may not necessarily reflect the facts of the matter. The more participants in KINU 2019 relied on compromise-resistant, value-derived rules for their attitudes, the stronger were their beliefs that reunification would have either positive or negative consequences, where the valence of the expected outcomes was determined by their general feelings toward a unified Korea. The correlation between reliance on values and unwillingness to compromise with consequence beliefs was 0.47 and 0.60, respectively (both p < 0.01). Encounters with North Korean defectors, presumably a good source of information about the consequences of unification, had no impact on the strength of these associations (r = 0.44, 0.60, both p < 0.01).

What does this mean for the sources of attached attitudes? It means that value-derived rules, although often not rooted in concrete information about the state of the world, can engender concrete expectations about the consequences of policies. These expectations may then be used to justify one’s attitudes beyond what would be possible through reliance on the values themselves. One may even start to believe that these beliefs about the consequences were the principal reasons why one adopted a certain attitude in the first place, or mistakenly believe that one’s consequence beliefs came from concrete experiences and not from cherished values. In other words, those whose attitudes are grounded in value-derived rules may be more prone to a kind of confirmation bias regarding their expectations. They are more likely to think that the consequences of reunification would be good or bad, in a way that is consistent with whether they support or reject re-unification, respectively.

Why values can hinder the progress of national discourse around reunification

Overreliance on values in deriving attitudes does not only affect our individual decision-making. It can also make it less likely that we will listen to those with the opposite position on the issue, something that any controversial topic needs if it is to be resolved without hurting a society’s sense of community. This is partly because it is more difficult to have a constructive dialogue if the two sides lack a deep enough understanding of an issue, a potential outcome of overreliance on value-derived rules as discussed. However, divergence in the values themselves can pose extra hurdles to consensus-building regardless of one’s views on reunification.

Values can hinder communication among people with different views

As mentioned before, KINU 2018 and 2019 surveys both show a more divided South Korean public than earlier surveys have reported (e.g. Jiyoon et al., 2015, p. 18). One reason might be a focus in the discourse on values. If the two sides hold different but similarly uncompromising values, it is less likely that discussing the issue will lead to a convergence in opinions. There will be less common ground upon which arguments can be shared and resolution can be reached. Furthermore, as discussed earlier, different values can engender different expectations about concrete outcomes of policies. Such expectations would lead to a smaller set of shared beliefs to serve as a bedrock for constructive discourse, even if the discussion is to turn toward talking about the concrete consequences.

Furthermore, if South Koreans believe that North Koreans hold similarly inflexible values, they will develop even more entrenched attitudes toward reunification based on the perceived distance of these presumed values from their own. Jiyoon and colleagues (2015, p. 10-11) report that while interest in reunification is generally high among South Koreans, those in their 20s show declining interest in more recent years, a pattern that coincides with citing “values” as their most important perceived source of distance from North Koreans. At the same time, South Koreans above 60 showed the greatest interest in reunification and are the least likely age group to consider “values” a dividing factor between themselves and North Koreans. Note that this does not necessarily mean that this subpopulation is less focused on values, it might just be that they see their values as more aligned with those of North Koreans.

What could the set of values that inform pro- and anti-reunification attitudes be? Are there significant differences between the two? To understand which values may be associated with reunification attitudes, participants in KINU 2019 were asked to rate the personal significance of each of ten basic values proposed by Schwartz (2012, pp. 5-7). Schwartz’s theory of Universal Values contends that these values have been important to all major cultures in history and classifies them according to their focus on conservation, self-transcendence, openness to change, and self-enhancement. Of special interest to our discussion are the two categories of self-enhancement and conservation. Box 1 briefly describes the defining goals of values in each of these two categories.

Self-enhancement: Values that emphasize pursuit  of  one's  own  interests  and  relative  success  and  dominance  over  others.

Achievement:  Personal  success  through  demonstrating  competence  according  to  social standards, thereby  obtaining  social  approval.

Power: Social status and prestige, control or dominance over people and resources.

Hedonism: Pleasure or sensuous gratification for oneself.

Conservation: Values  that  emphasize  order,  self-restriction,  preservation  of  the  past,  and  resistance  to  change

Security: Safety, harmony, and stability of society, of relationships, and of self. Associated with health, moderacy and sense of belonging.

Conformity: Restraint of actions, inclinations, and impulses likely to upset or harm others and violate social expectations or norms, usually in  everyday  interaction  with  close others.   Associated with loyalty and responsibility.

Tradition: Respect, commitment, and acceptance of the customs and ideals that one's culture or religion provides. They often take the form of religious rites, beliefs, and norms of behavior.

A focus on value-derived rules in pro-reunification participants was most strongly associated with commitments to “tradition” (r = 0.27, p < 0.01), “conformity” (r = 0.20, p < 0.01), and “security” (r = 0.19, p < 0.01). Note that all of these are Conservation values (Schwartz, 2012, pp. 8). What might the ideals that these participants are trying to conserve be? Part of the answer lies in the stronger emphasis on ethnic Korean identities among pro-reunification individuals. Focus on ethnic identity was strongly associated with using value-derived rules to arrive at one’s stance (r = 0.35, p < 0.01), unwillingness to compromise those rules (r = 0.31, p < 0.01), and consequently attached pro-reunification attitudes (r = 0.36, p < 0.01). In other words, the values that support attached pro-reunification attitudes seem to stem from a desire to conserve the ethnic Korean identity.

The attached anti-reunification attitudes were less driven by a rule-based approach (r = -0.03, p > 0.05) and more by what they expected to be the negative consequences of reunification (r = -0.34, p < 0.01). However, those against reunification who did rely on value-derived rules were moved by somewhat different values: Ethnic identities had little to do with their rules for action (r = -0.01, p > 0.05), and they were most unwilling to compromise a less traditional, Self-enhancement-related value, “achievement” (r = 0.15, p < 0.01). These results suggest that the two sides of the Korean reunification debate may rely on different sets of values to make their cases for and against reunification. That the mentioned values are independent predictors of attitudes toward reunification highlights how differences in values may contribute to difficulties in reaching national consensus on reunification. It is well-known that negotiation and compromise are hardest between groups that do not share protected values (Atran, Axelrod, & Davis, 2007).

Values can hinder communication among people with similar views

Differing values are potential obstacles to successful communication and discussion even among those who have the same general attitude toward reunification. We distinguished between what we called attached pro-reunification attitude, where the valence associated with the separation of the two Koreas is negative, and detached pro-reunification attitude, in which separation and reunification are both seen as desirable outcomes. Reliance on value-derived rules was a strong predictor of attached, but not detached pro-reunification attitudes (attached: r = 0.64, p < 0.01; detached: r = - 0.01, p > 0.05). For instance, ethnic Korean identity that mattered a lot to attached South Koreans in KINU 2019 had a much weaker association with attitudes among the detached participants (r = 0.09, p < 0.01). Note that in this respect, the detached pro-reunification South Koreans have more in common with the anti-reunification portion of the population than the rest of those who share their positive attitude toward reunification. Such differing values may hinder fruitful discussion even among those in favor or against re-unification if the discourse is focused on rules for action.

The effects of values are resistant to demographic differences

There are well-documented gender and age differences in attitudes toward reunification (Christensen et al., 2012, p. 6; Jung et al., 2016, pp.34-39). Like previous studies, a greater percentage of men surveyed in KINU 2018 and 2019 were in support of reunification than women (34% of women opposed reunification in 2018, compared with 25% of men, while in 2019 about 33% of men and 44% of women were in opposition). However, both genders showed similar degrees of reliance on uncompromising, value-derived rules in decision-making. For instance, value-derived rules were strong predictors of attached reunification attitudes in both groups (male: r = 0.30, p < 0.01; female: r = 0.32, p < 0.01). This focus was similarly associated with unwillingness to compromise and stronger belief in positive consequences of one’s attitudes (male: r = 0.66, 0.47, p < 0.01; female: r = 0.62,0.45, p < 0.01). The sense of simplicity and the feeling of understanding associated with reliance on value-derived rules was somewhat weaker among women, but the association remained (male: r = 0.25,0.17, p < 0.01; female: r = 0.15,0.12, p < 0.01). The sense of understanding in turn similarly colored attitudes toward reunification (male: r = 0.17, p < 0.01; female: r = 0.16, p < 0.01).  The patterns were more or less the same regardless of general stance on reunification. The sometimes stronger associations among men are in line with their stronger support for reunification and its relation to reliance on values. Nevertheless, it seems that the processes we discussed affect people of various genders.

Similarly, separating participants based on age (above or below 40 years) had no impact on the overall trends, even though many of the associations were somewhat stronger among older South Koreans. value-derived rules were predictors of attached pro-reunification attitudes in both groups (younger: r = 0.26, p < 0.01; older: r = 0.34, p < 0.01). This focus was associated with unwillingness to compromise and stronger belief in positive consequences of one’s attitudes, more so among the older population (younger: r = 0.60, 0.37, p < 0.01; older: r = 0.68,0.54, p < 0.01). The sense of simplicity and the feeling of understanding associated with reliance on rules was comparable (younger: r = 0.17,0.14, p < 0.01; older: r = 0.24,0.13, p < 0.01). The sense of understanding in turn colored attitudes toward reunification in both age groups, although less strongly among the youth (younger: r = 0.12, p < 0.01; older: r = 0.22, p < 0.01).  Once again, the patterns were more or less the same regardless of one’s general stance on reunification. The stronger associations among older individuals are also in line with their stronger support for reunification and its relation to reliance on values. Nevertheless, the distinction between reliance on value-derived rules versus consequences of policies is fruitful for understanding attitudes in both groups.

These findings suggest that the effect of reliance on values to determine one’s attitude is resistant to at least some of the demographic indicators of stance on reunification. This generality highlights the relevance of this concept to understanding how stances toward important social issues like reunification are formed and change over time.

The main takeaways

In this chapter, we talked about the complexity of concrete consequences of policies and how we often use decision-making rules informed by our protected values about correct action to simplify the process by which we develop our stances despite our ignorance of policy consequences. We discussed how this process can shed light on the state of attitudes toward reunification among South Koreans. We also touched upon the various ways in which over-reliance on value-derived rules can warp understanding of reunification and hinder constructive societal discourse. We will now briefly highlight how an understanding of these processes may aid the facilitation of more constructive discourse around Korean reunification. More specifically, we will touch upon the importance of checking one’s understanding and being mindful of our own and other people’s values in discourse, lest the attempt to reach consensus on the important issue of Korean reunification end in failure.

Acknowledge ignorance and check your information regularly

Appreciating the inherent limits of our individual knowledge highlights the need for intellectual humility in discussions: For complex issues like Korean reunification, even experts often face more unknowns than knowns. It is therefore imperative to actively seek out information. What makes maintaining this humility and constant effort more difficult is the sense of understanding and simplicity that may follow reliance on values. Acknowledging the commonness of illusory understanding can encourage checking the sources of one’s information and updating one’s beliefs accordingly on a more regular basis. It is also a reminder to consult with experts, even if we feel more than capable of forming our own opinions.

Be mindful of how much your arguments rely on values rather than consequences

Acknowledging how important values and their associated rules are to decision-making about policies can help us better evaluate our knowledge of reunification and develop a more nuanced stance: It helps us notice when values masquerade as concrete evidence and allows us to choose when and how we want to rely on our cherished values in decision-making. It also encourages us to be on the lookout for when value-derived rules induce resistance to incompatible evidence, a source of inflexible policy attitudes.

Consider or at the very least acknowledge the opposition’s values in discussions

Value-derived rules serve important purposes and people will presumably continue relying on them in discussions at least to some extent. This fact about human thinking can be incorporated into one’s arguments to allow for more constructive discussions. Evidence alone will not decide the outcome of the discourse around reunification. The opposite side of the debate, and even other people in one’s own side of the debate, may hold values that are not fully compatible with one’s own. There is evidence that simply acknowledging such differing values can go a long way toward making policy debates more tractable (Atran et al., 2007, pp.1039-1040). Considering alternative values when compiling one’s arguments in favor of own position may get one even further in discussions.

Conclusion

Using representative surveys conducted by the Korean Institute for National Unification, we argued that South Koreans’ attitudes toward reunification can be categorized as attached or detached. Attached attitudes are associated with a strong commitment to reunification or separation. Detached attitudes reflect indifference toward the two options. The most important reasons cited by South Koreans for these attitudes were categorized as consequence- or value-based. We discussed how value-based thinking provides simplifying rules for decision-making that are especially conducive to attached pro-reunification attitudes. However, overreliance on values can decrease interest in learning more, may lead to an illusory sense of understanding, might harden attitudes, and can result in wishful thinking. It may also have a negative impact on societal discourse if values of different groups are incompatible. These arguments, derived from the psychological literature but corroborated by recent surveys in South Korea, highlight the importance of value- versus consequence-based framings to the Korean reunification debate.

Methodological notes

Once all the constructs were represented, potential associations predicted a priori according to the literature were examined using Pearson’s correlation coefficients.
