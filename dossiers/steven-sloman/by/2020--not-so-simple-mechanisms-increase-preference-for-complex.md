# Not so simple! Mechanisms increase preference for complex explanations

## Abstract (from OpenAlex metadata)

Mechanisms play a central role in how we think about causality, yet not all causal explanations describe mechanisms. Across four experiments, we find that people evaluate explanations differently depending on whether or not they include mechanisms. Despite common wisdom suggesting that explanations ought to be simple (appealing to as few causes as necessary to explain an effect), the previous literature is divided over whether people adhere to this simplicity principle. Our findings suggest that the presence of mechanisms in an explanation is one factor that moderates this preference for simplicity. Without mechanisms, people typically exhibit a preference for simple explanations, consistent with probabilistic accounts. This preference is significantly reduced or even reversed when explanations contain mechanisms, suggesting that mechanisms afford a different way of evaluating explanations. Rather than focusing on probability, complex explanations that contain mechanisms may be preferred because they provide a fuller account of the underlying causal network, promoting a greater sense of understanding.

Abstract
Mechanisms play a central role in how we think about causality, yet not all causal
explanations describe mechanisms. Across five experiments, we find that people
evaluate explanations differently depending on whether or not they include
mechanisms. Despite common wisdom suggesting that explanations ought to be simple
in the sense of appealing to as few causes as necessary to explain an effect, the
literature is divided over whether people adhere to this principle. Our findings suggest
that the presence of causal mechanisms in an explanation is one factor that reduces
adherence. While competing explanations are often judged based on their probability of
being correct, mechanisms afford a different way of evaluating explanations: They
describe the underlying nature of causal relations. Complex explanations (appealing to
multiple causes) contain more causal relations and thus allow for more mechanistic
information, providing a fuller account of the causal network and promoting a greater
sense of understanding.

1 Introduction
In January of 1986, the Space Shuttle Challenger exploded shortly after launch, killing
all seven of its crewmembers. In the months that followed, the Rogers Presidential
Commission was tasked with explaining what led to this catastrophic failure. The
commission sought to answer three questions (Rogers Commission, 1986, p. 40): What
faults contributed to the failure? What evidence supports that conclusion? And finally,
what was the mechanism of failure? The questions posed by the Rogers Commission
underscore the idea that explaining can achieve multiple goals. In particular, one goal is
to determine what the most likely causes of an event are (given the available evidence),
but another goal is to determine how a cause leads to an effect. This is achieved by
describing a causal mechanism, i.e., a causal process separated into its constituent
parts and their relation to each other (Glennan, 2010; Joo, Yousif, & Keil, 2021).
People rely on many factors when assessing the goodness of an explanation.
One important factor is the overall probability of an explanation (Pearl, 1988), but
people are also sensitive to how well an explanation satisfies explanatory virtues (e.g.,
Lipton, 2004; Lombrozo, 2016; Sloman, Zemla, Lagnado, Bechlivanidis, & Hemmatian,
2019)—such as simplicity, coherence, and breadth—that might carry some epistemic
value. An explanation may also be judged on more superficial characteristics, such as
how well it is articulated or whether it is easily visualized (Zemla, Sloman, Bechlivanidis,
& Lagnado, 2017). These factors may facilitate understanding, but do not necessarily
hold epistemic value in themselves. Lastly, people are swayed by explanatory vices
(Lombrozo, 2016) that do not hold epistemic value or facilitate understanding, but
nonetheless influence our perception of the explanation (see also Bechlivanidis,

Lagnado, Zemla & Sloman, 2017). For example, Weisberg et al. (2008) found that
explanations of psychological phenomena that include neuroscientific information may
be judged as better even if that information is not relevant to the logic of the explanation
(perhaps due to a preference for scientific reductionism).
Though many factors have been shown to affect how we judge explanations, we
ask whether preferences change depending on whether or not an explanation describes
causal mechanisms. While some studies have directly compared explanations with and
without mechanisms (e.g., Ahn & Bailenson, 1996; Vasilyeva & Lombrozo, 2015), most
have not. Here, we test whether explanations that describe mechanisms are evaluated
differently than explanations that do not contain mechanisms, with a focus on one
explanatory virtue: simplicity. In other words, we are interested in whether the presence
or absence of mechanistic information affects people’s preference for simple or complex
explanations.
Causal mechanisms re-frame the way people think about explanations. In
mechanism-free explanations, simple explanations are often (though not always) more
probable. In contrast, mechanisms provide additional ways to evaluate an explanation
that may be at odds with simplicity. Explanations with more mechanistic information
provide us with a richer understanding of the relations between cause and effect,
increasing the attractiveness of complex explanations because they more fully describe
those causal relations. Complex explanations make it possible to describe complex
mechanisms that involve multiple causes. Across five experiments, we find that the
presence of mechanisms in an explanation increases our preference for complex (multicause) explanations relative to simple (singular cause) explanations.

1.1 The role of mechanism in explanation
1.1.1 What versus how
Good explanations identify which of many possible causes led to an outcome.
Explanations should identify causes that are both true and explanatorily relevant, i.e.,
they should identify the causes that were present and made a difference to whether the
event occurred (Strevens, 2004; Woodward, 2005). In the case of the Challenger
shuttle, this was answered plainly: “[…] the loss of the Space Shuttle Challenger was
caused by a failure in the joint between the two lower segments of the right Solid Rocket
Motor,” and “[…] no other element of the Space Shuttle system contributed to this
failure.” (Rogers Commission, 1986, p. 40). The Rogers Commission found that a joint
had failed, and judged this to be the singular cause of failure that led to the explosion. In
doing so, the commission ruled out other potential causes such as intentional sabotage,
a genuine concern at the time (Rogers Commission, 1986).
This explanation specifies what caused the shuttle failure, but is somewhat
unsatisfying. How did a faulty joint lead to an explosion, and why did the joint fail despite
working on previous shuttle flights? A more complete account of the shuttle disaster
may include the chain of events and conditions that led to an explosion, describing a
mechanism that explains how the outcome occurred in addition to identifying what the
causes were. More broadly, a mechanistic explanation is one that describes a causal
process: the causes, preventers, disabling and enabling conditions (Cheng & Novick,
1991) and how they interact. In the case of the Challenger shuttle, the unusually cold
weather prevented an O-ring seal from expanding to seal the joint; as a result, exhaust

1.1.2 Previous Findings

Mechanisms affect our reasoning and understanding in many ways. They are
critically important for making predictions about the unknown (Johnson & Ahn, 2017),
helping to determine whether the same outcome will recur in other conditions (e.g.,
would the same faulty joint have caused an explosion if the temperature were warmer?).
Though covariation information can serve the same purpose, people generally seek out
mechanistic information over covariation information when forming explanations (Ahn,
Kalish, Medin, & Gelman, 1995). Johnson and Keil (2017) found this to be especially
common when evaluating token claims (e.g., smoking caused John’s lung cancer)
compared to type claims (e.g., smoking causes cancer).
Vasilyeva and Lombrozo (2015) found that explanations with mechanisms are
typically judged as better than their counterparts without mechanisms. In the same
study, participants rated their subjective understanding of a phenomenon as higher
when the explanations contained mechanisms. The finding that mechanisms promote
understanding is consistent with the illusion of explanatory depth (Alter, Oppenheimer,
& Zemla, 2010; Fernbach, Rogers, Fox, & Sloman, 2013; Rozenblit & Keil, 2002):
people often overestimate their own understanding of how a device works but lower
their self-reported understanding after failing to articulate its mechanisms.
Knowledge of causal mechanisms can also lead to apparent violations of
probabilistic reasoning. Park and Sloman (2013) found that mechanistic beliefs predict
whether participants will violate the Markov condition of Bayesian networks (screening
off). For instance, smoking causes both lung cancer and blood vessel damage.
However, participants will judge the likelihood of a patient having blood vessel damage
as higher when told that the patient is both a smoker and has lung cancer compared to

8

participants who are only told that the patient is a smoker, even though lung cancer and
blood vessel damage should be conditionally independent given that the patient is a
smoker. This finding can be explained if we assume participants bring latent knowledge
about mechanisms into play when making probability judgements. For example, one
might believe that smoking causes blood vessel damage in part because lung cancer
can invade the bloodstream. Johnson and Ahn (2015) found that judgements of
causality violate transitivity when the causal relations are not seen as belonging to a
single mechanism. For example, although sex causes pregnancy and pregnancy
causes nausea, participants did not judge sex and nausea to be causally related. This
contrasts with similar causal chains that encompass a single mechanism more
transparently: Not flossing causes plaque build up, plaque causes cavities, and not
flossing causes cavities were all judged as highly causal.
Collectively, these findings suggest that mechanisms play an important role in
explanation. Judgments of explanatory goodness and self-reported levels of
understanding are both affected by the presence of mechanisms. People seek out
mechanistic information when developing explanations and prefer mechanistic
information over covariation information. Knowledge of mechanisms influences the way
we reason about causality and probability.
1.2 The role of simplicity in explanation
1.2.1 Simplicity versus complexity
A guiding normative principle of explanatory reasoning is that explanations ought
to be simple (Lipton, 2004; Thagard, 1978). Though several measures of simplicity have
been proposed, one that has received attention in the psychological literature is the

9
NOT SO SIMPLE!
number of root causes that an explanation appeals to (Pacer & Lombrozo, 2017).
Explanations that appeal to fewer root causes are simpler than those that appeal to
more root causes.1

Figure 2. (a) In Experiments 1 and 2, a single effect is either caused by one cause or by two causes (common
effect). The complex explanation may include independent or conjunctive causes. (b) In Experiments 3-5, two
effects are either caused by a single common cause or by two independent causes.

One normative reason why simple explanations are preferred (and in particular,
common cause explanations) is rooted in probability. When the presence of a cause is
uncertain, we can infer how likely the cause is (via Bayes’ rule), taking into account both
the base rate probability of the cause and the conditional probability of an effect given
that cause. In the Challenger example from earlier, one needs to consider not only the
conditional probability that sabotage would lead to an explosion, but also the base rate
probability of sabotage. This offers an intuitive rationale for why we might prefer
explanations with a minimal number of causes: a conjunction of two causes has a lower
base rate than any one of those causes individually.

This justification does not apply equally to all causal structures. When comparing
a single cause-effect relation to a common effect scenario (Figure 2a), the base rate
and conditional probabilities of the explanations compete. For example, the base rate
probability of a faulty joint and sabotage in the Challenger is lower than one cause
alone–however if both causes were present, the conditional probability of failure is
higher than if only one cause were present. This contrasts with a common cause
scenario (Figure 2b), where positing additional causes will lower the base rate
probability of the causes but not necessarily affect the conditional probability of the
effects.
In addition to a probabilistic justification, simplicity is believed by some to be an
inherent quality of good explanations. However, this criterion does not necessarily align
with other explanatory virtues or goals. For example, a simple, single-cause explanation
may be incomplete, described in less detail (i.e., shorter), or may not give alternative
explanations equal consideration–all of which affect the perceived goodness of an
explanation (Zemla, Sloman, Bechlivanidis, & Lagnado, 2017). Explanations that do not
contain mechanisms are more difficult to judge on these criteria (e.g., a mechanism
cannot be judged as incomplete if no mechanism is provided). More generally, the
salience of different criteria that an explanation can be judged on shifts depending on
whether or not an explanation contains mechanisms.
1.2.2 Previous Findings
Several authors have argued persuasively in favor of a simplicity principle for
explanations. Thagard (1989) proposes simplicity as a core tenet of a good explanation,
and research has shown that people do commonly prefer simple explanations (Read &

11
NOT SO SIMPLE!
Marcus-Newhall, 1993; van Overwalle, 1998). For example, Read and Marcus-Newhall
(1993) asked participants to rate various explanations for why Cheryl has an upset
stomach, has gained weight, and has been feeling tired recently. Participants preferred
an explanation that appealed to a single common cause (Cheryl is pregnant) as
opposed to multiple separate causes (Cheryl has a stomach virus, has stopped
exercising, and has Mononucleosis). Similarly, Lombrozo (2007) and Bonawitz and
Lombrozo (2012) showed that both adults and children exhibit a preference for simple,
common cause explanations even when competing explanations are equally probable.
An extensive literature on discounting (Kelley, 1973) indicates that people
frequently discount the role of a cause in producing an effect given a belief that other
causes could have produced that effect (see Morris & Larrick, 1995, and Khemlani &
Oppenheimer, 2011, for reviews of the discounting literature), indicating a preference for
simpler explanations. For example, one might infer that Sally failed an exam because
she did not study enough. But when told that most students in the class failed the exam,
one might instead come to the conclusion that Sally did study, but the exam was unduly
hard—even though the two causes are not mutually exclusive. Because a plausible
sufficient cause will discount the role of other causes, people often neglect the
possibility of explanations that propose multiple sufficient causes. This tendency to
discount causes may be normatively justified (Morris & Larrick, 1995; Griffiths, 2001).
Though much of the literature suggests that simple explanations should be
preferred, there are some circumstances in which a conjunction of causes appears to
make an explanation better (Tversky & Kahneman, 1983; Leddo, Abelson, & Gross,
1984; Khemlani & Johnson-Laird, 2011). Ahn, Kalish, Medin and Gelman (1995) provide

12
NOT SO SIMPLE!
one account for this observation. They suggest that when explaining an event, people
seek to understand the mechanisms that relate cause and effect. For example, a good
explanation for why a car accident occurred might be that it was raining and the driver is
nearsighted, even though this explanation invokes two causes that might be
independently sufficient (nearsightedness and poor weather). In this case, the
conjunctive explanation is preferred because the two causes “cohere with respect to a
single mechanism” (Ahn & Bailenson, 1996)—i.e., the weather made visibility poor
enough so that a nearsighted driver would have difficulty driving. When two causes
appear to invoke separate mechanisms (e.g., the driver was not wearing his glasses
and the car’s brakes were worn), people may be less likely to endorse complex
explanations. Though reasons for the conjunction effect in explanation vary (see
McClure, 1998 for a review), it seems clear that explanations sometimes reflect a desire
to make sense of an effect in its broader context, even if that means positing additional
causes.
The addition of mechanisms to an explanation may mitigate the tendency to
discount causes. Fugelsang, Stein, Green, and Dunbar (2004) found that while
scientists often discount anomalous data when no plausible mechanism exists to
explain covariation, they are less likely to do so when made aware of a plausible
mechanism to explain that covariation. Other factors may also attenuate our preference
for simple explanations. For instance, Johnson, Valenti, and Keil (2019) replicated the
simplicity bias demonstrated by Lombrozo (2007), but found that when causes were
described as stochastic (rather than deterministic), preference for simple explanations
was reduced. Zemla, Sloman, Bechlivanidis, and Lagnado (2017) found that people

13

prefer explanations with multiple causes in a common effect scenario and proposed that
people like explanations that have enough causes to make the effect seem inevitable.
Lim and Oppenheimer (2020) also find instances where participants prefer complex
explanations, and suggest that people try to match the complexity of the explanation to
the complexity of the thing being explained: Simple phenomena require simple
explanations, and complex phenomena require complex explanations. Liefgreen and
Lagnado (2021) found that participants prefer complex legal explanations to simple
ones. However when participants are instructed to draw causal models of the
explanations, preferences shifted towards simple explanations. Those who drew causal
models were more likely to justify their preference by appeal to probability or fewer
causes, while those who did not were more likely to justify their preference by raising
questions about the mechanisms between cause and effect.
The prior literature demonstrates that although a preference for simple
explanations is common, it is not universal. Many factors can shift our preference
towards simple or complex explanations, including causal structure, stochasticity, and
complexity matching. We propose that one overlooked factor is that mechanisms afford
different ways of evaluating explanations. When explanations merely posit causes, they
are likely to be judged on probabilistic grounds. When explanations contain mechanistic
details, people may give less weight to probability and more to evaluating the
mechanisms themselves. Since mechanisms provide people with a sense of
understanding (Vasilyeva & Lombrozo, 2015) and improve our ability to make
predictions (Johnson & Ahn, 2017) it stands to reason that all else being equal, complex

explanations with more mechanistic information should be preferred over simple ones
with less mechanistic information. Though people may prefer simple explanations for
other reasons, such as probability, the addition of mechanisms should attenuate this
preference.
1.3 Present work
In five experiments, we tested the hypothesis that mechanisms increase a
preference for complex explanations. In Experiments 1 and 2, we compared a single
cause-effect relation (simple explanation) to a common effect scenario (complex
explanation; see Figure 2a). In Experiments 3-5, we compared a common cause
scenario (simple explanation) to multiple, independent cause-effect relations (complex
explanation; see Figure 2b). For probabilistic reasons described earlier, we expected
the baseline preference for simplicity to differ in these designs.
Our motivation is that people tend to prefer explanations that are perceived as
more probable (Experiments 1 and 5), and simple explanations are commonly
perceived as more probable than complex ones (Experiment 5). However, explanations
are also appealing for non-probabilistic reasons and people report this when reflecting
on their own preferences (Experiments 2 and 3). In particular, people prefer
explanations that contain mechanistic information over those that do not (Experiments
2, 4, and 5). We hypothesized that the inclusion of mechanisms in explanations should
lead people to think less about the explanations’ probabilities and more about the
mechanisms themselves. This shift in focus should coincide with a preference shift
towards complex explanations (Experiments 1-5) because complex explanations

contain more mechanistic information and should be judged as more informative
(Experiment 5).

In Experiment 1, we found that most participants had no preference between the
two explanations. However the inclusion of mechanistic information had a noticeable
effect on the relative preference for simple or complex explanations, which we call a

mechanism effect. When explanations were presented without mechanisms,

However, Experiment 1 has several potential limitations. Participants may be
familiar with the causes used in the stimuli (alcohol, iron deficiency, family history), and

Consistent with Experiment 1, Experiment 2 revealed a mechanism effect in

4.3 Discussion
The results from Experiment 3 corroborated a key finding in Experiment 1 and 2
that participants display a mechanism effect, preferring complex explanations more
relative to simple ones when those explanations contained mechanisms. As in
Experiment 2, participants in the mechanism condition were more likely to justify their
ratings by appeal to the quality of mechanisms, including the overall informativeness
and ease of understanding of the explanations. Participants who referenced
mechanisms or the quality of those mechanisms had stronger preferences for complex
explanations than those who did not. This finding provides more support for our theory
that mechanisms afford additional criteria for people to evaluate an explanation, and
that these criteria tend to favor complex explanations.
The results of Experiment 3 contrast most with Experiment 2 in that participants
showed a general preference for simple explanations, rather than complex ones.

This main effect of a simplicity preference for common cause structures is not
surprising, as it mirrors previous findings in the literature (Lombrozo, 2007; Read &

may result from consideration of the base rate probabilities: all else being equal, one
cause is more probable than two causes. This suggests a possible way to attenuate a
preference for simple explanations, which we test in Experiment 4. If participants
preferred a simple explanation in part because they believe it is less likely that Sally
consumed two nutrients (biotin and thiamine) rather than one (niacin), this preference
should be reduced if it is known that Sally has consumed all three nutrients. This
effectively negates any simplicity bias that is due to base rates, but should not influence
the mechanism effect.

7 Power and Sensitivity Analyses
Our results provide evidence for the proposed mechanism effect, however valid concerns
might be raised over our claim that a marginally significant effect provides evidence of
this (Experiment 4). Recent work in behavioral sciences has also called for larger sample
sizes and a stricter alpha criterion (e.g., p < .005; Benjamin et al., 2018). After the data
analyses were performed, we conducted post-hoc power analyses and sensitivity
analyses for added transparency. We limit these analyses to Experiment 2-5, which share
a common design (2 x 2 mixed ANOVA) and focus on the interaction term (i.e., the
mechanism effect).

8 General Discussion

47
NOT SO SIMPLE!
Across five experiments, we find that the presence of mechanisms affects one’s
preference for simple or complex explanations. Overall, participants preferred
explanations that included mechanisms (Experiments 2, 4, and 5).13
When explanations did not contain mechanisms, preference depended on the
causal structure invoked. In multiple-effect scenarios, participants preferred simple
explanations that appeal to a single, common cause (Experiments 3, 4, and 5). This
preference is predicted by probabilistic accounts of explanation (e.g., Jefferys & Berger,
1992) as well as non-probabilistic accounts that appeal to explanatory virtues
(Lombrozo, 2007). In single-effect scenarios, results were mixed: participants showed a
preference for simple explanations in Experiment 1, but no significant preference for
simple or complex explanations in Experiment 2.
Critically, when explanations did contain mechanisms, we found strong evidence
in favor of a shift of preferences (a mechanism effect). Mechanistic explanations
reduced (Experiments 2, 3, and 5) or reversed (Experiment 1) the preference for simple
explanations relative to complex ones. Mechanistic explanations also reduced the
preference for simple explanations relative to complex ones in Experiment 4, though not
significantly (p = .059). This effect appears to be driven largely by an increase in ratings
for complex explanations when the explanations contained mechanisms (Experiments
2, 3, 4, and 5), whereas mechanisms increased ratings for simple explanations only in
Experiment 2. The experimental design of Experiment 1 precludes evaluation of this
effect. These findings are summarized in Table 1.

We propose that the mechanism effect occurs in part because mechanisms
afford an additional way to evaluate explanations. When explanations do not contain
mechanisms, people rely predominately on criteria such as subjective probability that
allow them to determine ‘what’ the causes of an effect are (i.e., causal inference). This
criterion often favors simple explanations, as simple explanations are often seen as
more probable. When explanations do contain mechanisms, people can also evaluate
the mechanisms themselves (i.e., ‘how’ a cause produces an effect). Mechanistic
information increases subjective understanding (Vasilyeva & Lombrozo, 2015) and
allows for improved predictions about the unknown (Johnson & Ahn, 2017). This

49
NOT SO SIMPLE!
criterion favors complex, multi-mechanism explanations that provide a fuller description
of the causal relations.
In support of this theory, participants in the mechanism condition reported using
qualitatively different cues to rate explanations (Experiments 2 and 3), incorporating
factors such as informativeness and ease of understanding that played little or no role in
rating explanations for participants in the no mechanism condition. Mediation analyses
reveal that the presence of mechanisms shifted the criteria that participants report using
to evaluate explanations, and that participants who referred to these criteria (such as
informativeness) were more likely to exhibit a shift in preference towards complex
explanations (Experiments 2 and 3). Ratings of mechanism informativeness collected in
Experiment 5 corroborate that complex mechanisms are rated as more informative, and
these ratings are correlated with explanation ratings for participants in the mechanism
condition. We have argued that participants value mechanisms in part because they
increase subjective understanding and improve the ability to make predictions
(Vasilyeva & Lombrozo, 2015; Johnson & Ahn, 2017). However we cannot say for sure
that this is true in our data: participants in our experiments did not rate their own
understanding and were not tested on their ability to make inductive inferences.
Our theory could benefit from a more refined definition of informativeness. We
suggested that explanations that contain more mechanisms are more informative
because they provide a fuller description of how the effects are produced (as opposed
to what the causes are). In the present experiments, informativeness is measured with
subjective ratings (Experiment 5) and by coding open-ended responses that often
(though not always) referenced an explanation’s “information” or “detail” explicitly

50
NOT SO SIMPLE!
(Experiments 2 and 3). This differs from prior work that has measured informativeness
by asking participants “the extent to which the information tells you something new”
(Hilton, 1996), or by contrasting explanations that vary in ambiguity (e.g., disjunctions
versus conjunctions; Li et al., 2020). Future work might evaluate formal definitions of
informativeness such as minimum description length of a causal model (Mian, Marx, &
Vreeken, 2021), or by relating it to other information-theoretic constructs like information
gain between two causal models (Steyvers, Tenenbaum, Wagenmakers, & Blum
2003).Future work should disentangle this hypothesis from other possible explanations
of the mechanism effect. For example, mechanisms may change the way people think
about how and whether two causes interact (i.e., two causes might be perceived as
independent without mechanisms, but conjunctive when mechanisms are present).
Mechanisms may also alter which probabilities guide explanation preference. Johnson,
Valenti, and Keil (2019) found that people rate simple explanations as having a higher
prior probability, but that complex explanations are rated as more likely to explain the
data. The causal structures used in their experiment mirror those of Experiments 3-5,
contrasting a common cause (simple) versus separate causes (complex) in explaining
two effects. It is possible that because mechanisms describe the process by which the
effects are produced, participants shift their focus from the prior probability to the
likelihood of explaining the data, increasing the preference for complex explanations.
Related work has found that simplicity can act as a guide to estimating prior
probabilities, both in explanation and in curve fitting (Johnson, Jin, & Keil, 2014;
Vrantsidis & Lombrozo, 2022).

51
NOT SO SIMPLE!
This alternative hypothesis, like ours, assumes that mechanisms affect the cues
that people rely on to guide explanation preference. Unlike our hypothesis, it does not
imply that there is a qualitative difference in how people perceive the task of choosing
an explanation. That is, it does not imply that people shift from evaluating “what”
happened to “how” it happened. Instead, it suggests that mechanisms change the way
people use probability to evaluate explanations. The two hypotheses are not mutually
exclusive, and future work could explore their relative contribution to the mechanism
effect.
Our results are consistent with previous findings that people have an intrinsic
bias for simple, common cause explanations (Lombrozo, 2007; Read & MarcusNewhall, 1993) and that complex, common effect explanations are preferred when
mechanisms are present (Zemla, Sloman, Bechlivanidis, & Lagnado, 2017). However,
mechanistic detail is clearly not the only factor that influences one’s preference for
simple or complex explanations. Other factors such as the content domain and
stochasticity affect preferences for simple explanations (Johnson, Valenti, and Keil,
2019), and people seek explanations that match the complexity of causes and effects
(Lim & Oppenheimer, 2020). Our results are the first to implicate mechanistic detail as a
moderating factor. This provides further insight into how people evaluate explanations
and reconcile some previous findings for when complex explanations triumph over
simple ones.
Our experiments contain several limitations. In all five experiments, participants
judged which explanation was “the best,” and not which explanation was “most likely” or
“most satisfying” (a phrasing often used, e.g., Lombrozo, 2007; Weisberg et al., 2008).

52
NOT SO SIMPLE!
These descriptors may differentially affect explanatory preferences, but have not been
systematically compared. In particular, descriptors that invoke probabilistic language
(such as “most likely” or “most probable”) may dissuade people from considering nonprobabilistic criteria or explanatory virtues in their judgments. Similarly, the presence of
probabilistic language (such as in Experiment 1) may influence the criteria people use to
evaluate explanations. It is also important to consider that we define complexity in a
narrow sense: the number of root causes in an explanation (Pacer & Lombrozo, 2017),
with each cause having a unique mechanism. There are many other possible forms of
complexity (e.g., explanation length, causal chain length, causal interactions). These
other forms of complexity do not lend themselves to the same justification for a
mechanism effect, so we do not expect our results to necessarily generalize across
these fundamentally different forms of complexity. Moreover, we only used explanations
with one cause (simple) or two (complex), but not more than two causes. Whether the
mechanism effect generalizes to more than two causes is unknown. Though the exact
boundaries of the mechanism effect are yet to be determined, our findings show that
mechanisms are crucial to how we think about and evaluate explanations.
