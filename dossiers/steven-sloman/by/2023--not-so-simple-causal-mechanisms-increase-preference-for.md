# Not so simple! Causal mechanisms increase preference for complex explanations

## Full text

## Abstract

Mechanisms play a central role in how we think about causality, yet not all causal explanations describe mechanisms.Across five experiments, we find that people evaluate explanations differently depending on whether or not they include mechanisms.Despite common wisdom suggesting that explanations ought to be simple in the sense of appealing to as few causes as necessary to explain an effect, the literature is divided over whether people adhere to this principle.Our findings suggest that the presence of causal mechanisms in an explanation is one factor that reduces adherence.While competing explanations are often judged based on their probability of being correct, mechanisms afford a different way of evaluating explanations: They describe the underlying nature of causal relations.Complex explanations (appealing to multiple causes) contain more causal relations and thus allow for more mechanistic information, providing a fuller account of the causal network and promoting a greater sense of understanding.

### Introduction

In January of 1986, the Space Shuttle Challenger exploded shortly after launch, killing all seven of its crewmembers.In the months that followed, the Rogers Presidential Commission was tasked with explaining what led to this catastrophic failure.The commission sought to answer three questions (Rogers Commission, 1986, p. 40): What faults contributed to the failure?What evidence supports that conclusion?And finally, what was the mechanism of failure?The questions posed by the Rogers Commission underscore the idea that explaining can achieve multiple goals.In particular, one goal is to determine what the most likely causes of an event are (given the available evidence), but another goal is to determine how a cause leads to an effect.This is achieved by describing a causal mechanism, i.e., a causal process separated into its constituent parts and their relation to each other (Glennan, 2010;Joo, Yousif, & Keil, 2021).

People rely on many factors when assessing the goodness of an explanation.

One important factor is the overall probability of an explanation (Pearl, 1988), but people are also sensitive to how well an explanation satisfies explanatory virtues (e.g., Lipton, 2004;Lombrozo, 2016;Sloman, Zemla, Lagnado, Bechlivanidis, & Hemmatian, 2019)-such as simplicity, coherence, and breadth-that might carry some epistemic value.An explanation may also be judged on more superficial characteristics, such as how well it is articulated or whether it is easily visualized (Zemla, Sloman, Bechlivanidis, & Lagnado, 2017).These factors may facilitate understanding, but do not necessarily hold epistemic value in themselves.Lastly, people are swayed by explanatory vices (Lombrozo, 2016) that do not hold epistemic value or facilitate understanding, but nonetheless influence our perception of the explanation (see also Bechlivanidis, NOT SO SIMPLE!Lagnado, Zemla & Sloman, 2017).For example, Weisberg et al. (2008) found that explanations of psychological phenomena that include neuroscientific information may be judged as better even if that information is not relevant to the logic of the explanation (perhaps due to a preference for scientific reductionism).

Though many factors have been shown to affect how we judge explanations, we ask whether preferences change depending on whether or not an explanation describes causal mechanisms.While some studies have directly compared explanations with and without mechanisms (e.g., Ahn & Bailenson, 1996;Vasilyeva & Lombrozo, 2015), most have not.Here, we test whether explanations that describe mechanisms are evaluated differently than explanations that do not contain mechanisms, with a focus on one explanatory virtue: simplicity.In other words, we are interested in whether the presence or absence of mechanistic information affects people's preference for simple or complex explanations.

Causal mechanisms re-frame the way people think about explanations.In mechanism-free explanations, simple explanations are often (though not always) more probable.In contrast, mechanisms provide additional ways to evaluate an explanation that may be at odds with simplicity.Explanations with more mechanistic information provide us with a richer understanding of the relations between cause and effect, increasing the attractiveness of complex explanations because they more fully describe those causal relations.Complex explanations make it possible to describe complex mechanisms that involve multiple causes.Across five experiments, we find that the presence of mechanisms in an explanation increases our preference for complex (multicause) explanations relative to simple (singular cause) explanations.NOT SO SIMPLE!

### The role of mechanism in explanation

### What versus how

Good explanations identify which of many possible causes led to an outcome.

Explanations should identify causes that are both true and explanatorily relevant, i.e., they should identify the causes that were present and made a difference to whether the event occurred (Strevens, 2004;Woodward, 2005).In the case of the Challenger shuttle, this was answered plainly: "[…] the loss of the Space Shuttle Challenger was caused by a failure in the joint between the two lower segments of the right Solid Rocket Motor," and " […] no other element of the Space Shuttle system contributed to this failure."(Rogers Commission, 1986, p. 40).The Rogers Commission found that a joint had failed, and judged this to be the singular cause of failure that led to the explosion.In doing so, the commission ruled out other potential causes such as intentional sabotage, a genuine concern at the time (Rogers Commission, 1986).

This explanation specifies what caused the shuttle failure, but is somewhat unsatisfying.How did a faulty joint lead to an explosion, and why did the joint fail despite working on previous shuttle flights?A more complete account of the shuttle disaster may include the chain of events and conditions that led to an explosion, describing a mechanism that explains how the outcome occurred in addition to identifying what the causes were.More broadly, a mechanistic explanation is one that describes a causal process: the causes, preventers, disabling and enabling conditions (Cheng & Novick, 1991) and how they interact.In the case of the Challenger shuttle, the unusually cold weather prevented an O-ring seal from expanding to seal the joint; as a result, exhaust NOT SO SIMPLE! leaked from the motor which heated the external fuel tank, causing it to rupture and explode.However, a faulty joint in and of itself does not cause an explosion.Figure 1b depicts a causal model which expands on the causal connection between the faulty joint and shuttle failure to describe the mechanism of failure-the enabling conditions and sequence of events that began with a faulty joint and resulted in an explosion.

### Previous Findings NOT SO SIMPLE!

Mechanisms affect our reasoning and understanding in many ways.They are critically important for making predictions about the unknown (Johnson & Ahn, 2017), helping to determine whether the same outcome will recur in other conditions (e.g., would the same faulty joint have caused an explosion if the temperature were warmer?).

Though covariation information can serve the same purpose, people generally seek out mechanistic information over covariation information when forming explanations (Ahn, Kalish, Medin, & Gelman, 1995).Johnson and Keil (2017) found this to be especially common when evaluating token claims (e.g., smoking caused John's lung cancer) compared to type claims (e.g., smoking causes cancer).Vasilyeva and Lombrozo (2015) found that explanations with mechanisms are typically judged as better than their counterparts without mechanisms.In the same study, participants rated their subjective understanding of a phenomenon as higher when the explanations contained mechanisms.The finding that mechanisms promote understanding is consistent with the illusion of explanatory depth (Alter, Oppenheimer, & Zemla, 2010;Fernbach, Rogers, Fox, & Sloman, 2013;Rozenblit & Keil, 2002): people often overestimate their own understanding of how a device works but lower their self-reported understanding after failing to articulate its mechanisms.

Knowledge of causal mechanisms can also lead to apparent violations of probabilistic reasoning.Park and Sloman (2013) found that mechanistic beliefs predict whether participants will violate the Markov condition of Bayesian networks (screening off).For instance, smoking causes both lung cancer and blood vessel damage.

However, participants will judge the likelihood of a patient having blood vessel damage as higher when told that the patient is both a smoker and has lung cancer compared to NOT SO SIMPLE! participants who are only told that the patient is a smoker, even though lung cancer and blood vessel damage should be conditionally independent given that the patient is a smoker.This finding can be explained if we assume participants bring latent knowledge about mechanisms into play when making probability judgements.For example, one might believe that smoking causes blood vessel damage in part because lung cancer can invade the bloodstream.Johnson and Ahn (2015) found that judgements of causality violate transitivity when the causal relations are not seen as belonging to a single mechanism.For example, although sex causes pregnancy and pregnancy causes nausea, participants did not judge sex and nausea to be causally related.This contrasts with similar causal chains that encompass a single mechanism more transparently: Not flossing causes plaque build up, plaque causes cavities, and not flossing causes cavities were all judged as highly causal.

Collectively, these findings suggest that mechanisms play an important role in explanation.Judgments of explanatory goodness and self-reported levels of understanding are both affected by the presence of mechanisms.People seek out mechanistic information when developing explanations and prefer mechanistic information over covariation information.Knowledge of mechanisms influences the way we reason about causality and probability.

### The role of simplicity in explanation

### Simplicity versus complexity

A guiding normative principle of explanatory reasoning is that explanations ought to be simple (Lipton, 2004;Thagard, 1978).Though several measures of simplicity have been proposed, one that has received attention in the psychological literature is the NOT SO SIMPLE! number of root causes that an explanation appeals to (Pacer & Lombrozo, 2017).

Explanations that appeal to fewer root causes are simpler than those that appeal to more root causes.One normative reason why simple explanations are preferred (and in particular, common cause explanations) is rooted in probability.When the presence of a cause is uncertain, we can infer how likely the cause is (via Bayes' rule), taking into account both the base rate probability of the cause and the conditional probability of an effect given that cause.In the Challenger example from earlier, one needs to consider not only the conditional probability that sabotage would lead to an explosion, but also the base rate probability of sabotage.This offers an intuitive rationale for why we might prefer explanations with a minimal number of causes: a conjunction of two causes has a lower base rate than any one of those causes individually.

1 We use the terms simple and complex throughout this article as shorthand to refer to competing explanations that appeal to fewer or more causes relative to each other.NOT SO SIMPLE!This justification does not apply equally to all causal structures.When comparing a single cause-effect relation to a common effect scenario (Figure 2a), the base rate and conditional probabilities of the explanations compete.For example, the base rate probability of a faulty joint and sabotage in the Challenger is lower than one cause alone-however if both causes were present, the conditional probability of failure is higher than if only one cause were present.This contrasts with a common cause scenario (Figure 2b), where positing additional causes will lower the base rate probability of the causes but not necessarily affect the conditional probability of the effects.

In addition to a probabilistic justification, simplicity is believed by some to be an inherent quality of good explanations.However, this criterion does not necessarily align with other explanatory virtues or goals.For example, a simple, single-cause explanation may be incomplete, described in less detail (i.e., shorter), or may not give alternative explanations equal consideration-all of which affect the perceived goodness of an explanation (Zemla, Sloman, Bechlivanidis, & Lagnado, 2017).Explanations that do not contain mechanisms are more difficult to judge on these criteria (e.g., a mechanism cannot be judged as incomplete if no mechanism is provided).More generally, the salience of different criteria that an explanation can be judged on shifts depending on whether or not an explanation contains mechanisms.

### Previous Findings

Several authors have argued persuasively in favor of a simplicity principle for explanations.Thagard (1989) proposes simplicity as a core tenet of a good explanation, and research has shown that people do commonly prefer simple explanations (Read & NOT SO SIMPLE! Marcus-Newhall, 1993;van Overwalle, 1998).For example, Read and Marcus-Newhall (1993) asked participants to rate various explanations for why Cheryl has an upset stomach, has gained weight, and has been feeling tired recently.Participants preferred an explanation that appealed to a single common cause (Cheryl is pregnant) as opposed to multiple separate causes (Cheryl has a stomach virus, has stopped exercising, and has Mononucleosis).Similarly, Lombrozo (2007) and Bonawitz and Lombrozo (2012) showed that both adults and children exhibit a preference for simple, common cause explanations even when competing explanations are equally probable.

An extensive literature on discounting (Kelley, 1973) indicates that people frequently discount the role of a cause in producing an effect given a belief that other causes could have produced that effect (see Morris &Larrick, 1995, andKhemlani &Oppenheimer, 2011, for reviews of the discounting literature), indicating a preference for simpler explanations.For example, one might infer that Sally failed an exam because she did not study enough.But when told that most students in the class failed the exam, one might instead come to the conclusion that Sally did study, but the exam was unduly hard-even though the two causes are not mutually exclusive.Because a plausible sufficient cause will discount the role of other causes, people often neglect the possibility of explanations that propose multiple sufficient causes.This tendency to discount causes may be normatively justified (Morris & Larrick, 1995;Griffiths, 2001).

Though much of the literature suggests that simple explanations should be preferred, there are some circumstances in which a conjunction of causes appears to make an explanation better (Tversky & Kahneman, 1983;Leddo, Abelson, & Gross, 1984;Khemlani & Johnson-Laird, 2011).Ahn, Kalish, Medin and Gelman (1995) provide NOT SO SIMPLE! one account for this observation.They suggest that when explaining an event, people seek to understand the mechanisms that relate cause and effect.For example, a good explanation for why a car accident occurred might be that it was raining and the driver is nearsighted, even though this explanation invokes two causes that might be independently sufficient (nearsightedness and poor weather).In this case, the conjunctive explanation is preferred because the two causes "cohere with respect to a single mechanism" (Ahn & Bailenson, 1996)-i.e., the weather made visibility poor enough so that a nearsighted driver would have difficulty driving.When two causes appear to invoke separate mechanisms (e.g., the driver was not wearing his glasses and the car's brakes were worn), people may be less likely to endorse complex explanations.Though reasons for the conjunction effect in explanation vary (see McClure, 1998 for a review), it seems clear that explanations sometimes reflect a desire to make sense of an effect in its broader context, even if that means positing additional causes.

The addition of mechanisms to an explanation may mitigate the tendency to discount causes.Fugelsang, Stein, Green, and Dunbar (2004) found that while scientists often discount anomalous data when no plausible mechanism exists to explain covariation, they are less likely to do so when made aware of a plausible mechanism to explain that covariation.Other factors may also attenuate our preference for simple explanations.For instance, Johnson, Valenti, and Keil (2019) replicated the simplicity bias demonstrated by Lombrozo (2007), but found that when causes were described as stochastic (rather than deterministic), preference for simple explanations was reduced.Zemla, Sloman, Bechlivanidis, and Lagnado (2017) found that people NOT SO SIMPLE! prefer explanations with multiple causes in a common effect scenario and proposed that people like explanations that have enough causes to make the effect seem inevitable.Lim and Oppenheimer (2020) also find instances where participants prefer complex explanations, and suggest that people try to match the complexity of the explanation to the complexity of the thing being explained: Simple phenomena require simple explanations, and complex phenomena require complex explanations.Liefgreen and Lagnado (2021) found that participants prefer complex legal explanations to simple ones.However when participants are instructed to draw causal models of the explanations, preferences shifted towards simple explanations.Those who drew causal models were more likely to justify their preference by appeal to probability or fewer causes, while those who did not were more likely to justify their preference by raising questions about the mechanisms between cause and effect.

The prior literature demonstrates that although a preference for simple explanations is common, it is not universal.Many factors can shift our preference towards simple or complex explanations, including causal structure, stochasticity, and complexity matching.We propose that one overlooked factor is that mechanisms afford different ways of evaluating explanations.When explanations merely posit causes, they are likely to be judged on probabilistic grounds.When explanations contain mechanistic details, people may give less weight to probability and more to evaluating the mechanisms themselves.Since mechanisms provide people with a sense of understanding (Vasilyeva & Lombrozo, 2015) and improve our ability to make predictions (Johnson & Ahn, 2017) it stands to reason that all else being equal, complex NOT SO SIMPLE! explanations with more mechanistic information should be preferred over simple ones with less mechanistic information.Though people may prefer simple explanations for other reasons, such as probability, the addition of mechanisms should attenuate this preference.

### Present work

In five experiments, we tested the hypothesis that mechanisms increase a preference for complex explanations.In Experiments 1 and 2, we compared a single cause-effect relation (simple explanation) to a common effect scenario (complex explanation; see Figure 2a).In Experiments 3-5, we compared a common cause scenario (simple explanation) to multiple, independent cause-effect relations (complex explanation; see Figure 2b).For probabilistic reasons described earlier, we expected the baseline preference for simplicity to differ in these designs.

Our motivation is that people tend to prefer explanations that are perceived as more probable (Experiments 1 and 5), and simple explanations are commonly perceived as more probable than complex ones (Experiment 5).However, explanations are also appealing for non-probabilistic reasons and people report this when reflecting on their own preferences (Experiments 2 and 3).In particular, people prefer explanations that contain mechanistic information over those that do not (Experiments 2, 4, and 5).We hypothesized that the inclusion of mechanisms in explanations should lead people to think less about the explanations' probabilities and more about the mechanisms themselves.This shift in focus should coincide with a preference shift towards complex explanations (Experiments 1-5) because complex explanations NOT SO SIMPLE! contain more mechanistic information and should be judged as more informative (Experiment 5).

### Experiment 1

In Experiment 1, we tested the hypothesis that complex explanations would be preferred more with mechanisms than without them.

### Discussion

In Experiment 1, we found that most participants had no preference between the two explanations.However the inclusion of mechanistic information had a noticeable effect on the relative preference for simple or complex explanations, which we call a NOT SO SIMPLE! mechanism effect.When explanations were presented without mechanisms, participants favored simple explanations (i.e., those with fewer causes).When the explanations contained mechanisms, the overall proportion of participants preferring a complex explanation increased.

However, Experiment 1 has several potential limitations.Participants may be familiar with the causes used in the stimuli (alcohol, iron deficiency, family history), and this prior knowledge could have affected their preferences.For example, knowledge that susceptibility to disease is often linked to genetics may have resulted in a preference for explanations that appealed to family history.It is also possible that participants relied on a simple "explanation length" heuristic to decide which explanation was better, thus preferring explanations that were complicated in the general rather than the intended sense of multiple causes.Although the complex explanation was longer in both the no mechanism and mechanism conditions, this difference was larger (and perhaps more salient) in the mechanism condition which listed two causes and two mechanisms.A table of probabilistic information was presented in Experiment 1 in order to understand how risk and prevalence information guide explanation preference, though explanations are not typically accompanied by such tables.Though the table was identical for both mechanism and no mechanism conditions, we removed the table in subsequent experiments to ensure that the effect of mechanisms on explanation preference is not contingent on salient probabilistic information.The explanations in the two conditions also differed in their phrasing (i.e., whether the cause or the effect was mentioned first).The remaining experiments maintain identical phrasing between conditions.Lastly, the use of a ternary response variable resulted in a small sample size NOT SO SIMPLE! for analysis, as most participants expressed no preference between explanations.In subsequent experiments, we switched to a within-subjects rating scale where participants rated both simple and complex explanations on a continuous sliding scale.

This scale still allowed participants to express indifference by rating both explanations equally.

### Experiment 2

In Experiment 2, we sought to replicate the mechanism effect while addressing the limitations of Experiment 1. Specifically, we equate the explanation length in both conditions, use an explanation rating scale in place of a forced-choice design, use unfamiliar causes to avoid effects of prior knowledge, and provide no table of probabilistic information.

### Discussion NOT SO SIMPLE!

Consistent with Experiment 1, Experiment 2 revealed a mechanism effect in which the relative preference for complex explanations over simple ones increased when those explanations contained mechanisms.This result suggests that the mechanism effect is specifically a result of mechanistic detail, rather than general detail or explanation length.

In a slight departure from Experiment 1, participants expressed no preference between simple and complex explanations when explanations did not contain mechanisms (rather than a significant preference for simple explanations).Conversely, participants did show a significant preference for complex explanations when explanations did contain mechanisms-a result that was not significant in Experiment 1 (though in the same direction).This is consistent with a shift in the baseline preference for simple explanations without mechanisms.Properties of the explanations, such as the familiarity of the causes (Experiment 1) or whether the causes were initiated by an agent with intent (Experiment 2) may play a role in determining an individual's baseline

preference for an explanation without mechanisms.Despite this, the mechanism effect persisted across both experiments.

Participants used different criteria to justify their preferences depending on whether or not they saw explanations that contained mechanisms.Those in the no mechanism condition appealed more to probability, whereas those in the mechanism condition referenced the mechanisms themselves and appealed more to informativeness and ease of understanding.This is not surprising in itself, as mechanisms afford participants additional ways of evaluating an explanation.However this shift in judgment criteria may help explain the mechanism effect: probability has NOT SO SIMPLE! been used to normatively justify a simplicity bias, whereas criteria like informativeness or ease of understanding might favor complex explanations with more mechanistic information.Consistent with this theory, we found that participants who appealed to mechanistic information to justify their preference had a stronger preference for complex explanations than those who did not.

In Experiments 1 and 2, complex causes are related to their effect through a common effect structure.In Experiments 3-5, we test whether the mechanism effect is present in explanations with different causal structures.Specifically, we contrast a simple, common cause causal structure to a complex explanation that invokes multiple independent causes.

### Discussion

The results from Experiment 3 corroborated a key finding in Experiment 1 and 2 that participants display a mechanism effect, preferring complex explanations more relative to simple ones when those explanations contained mechanisms.As in Experiment 2, participants in the mechanism condition were more likely to justify their ratings by appeal to the quality of mechanisms, including the overall informativeness and ease of understanding of the explanations.Participants who referenced mechanisms or the quality of those mechanisms had stronger preferences for complex explanations than those who did not.This finding provides more support for our theory that mechanisms afford additional criteria for people to evaluate an explanation, and that these criteria tend to favor complex explanations.

The results of Experiment 3 contrast most with Experiment 2 in that participants showed a general preference for simple explanations, rather than complex ones.NOT SO SIMPLE!This main effect of a simplicity preference for common cause structures is not surprising, as it mirrors previous findings in the literature (Lombrozo, 2007;Read & Marcus-Newhall, 1993).This overall preference for simple, common cause explanations may result from consideration of the base rate probabilities: all else being equal, one cause is more probable than two causes.This suggests a possible way to attenuate a preference for simple explanations, which we test in Experiment 4. If participants preferred a simple explanation in part because they believe it is less likely that Sally consumed two nutrients (biotin and thiamine) rather than one (niacin), this preference should be reduced if it is known that Sally has consumed all three nutrients.This effectively negates any simplicity bias that is due to base rates, but should not influence the mechanism effect.

### Power and Sensitivity Analyses

Our results provide evidence for the proposed mechanism effect, however valid concerns might be raised over our claim that a marginally significant effect provides evidence of this (Experiment 4).Recent work in behavioral sciences has also called for larger sample sizes and a stricter alpha criterion (e.g., p < .005;Benjamin et al., 2018).After the data analyses were performed, we conducted post-hoc power analyses and sensitivity analyses for added transparency.We limit these analyses to Experiment 2-5, which share a common design (2 x 2 mixed ANOVA) and focus on the interaction term (i.e., the mechanism effect).

For the sensitivity analysis, we used 2 of .01,.06,and .14 as benchmarks for small, medium, and large effect sizes (corresponding to Cohen's f of .1,.25, and .4;NOT SO SIMPLE!Cohen, 1992).Using WebPower (Zhang, Mai, Yang, & Zhang, 2018), the suggested sample size to detect a within-between interaction effect with an alpha criterion of .05 and a power of .95 is N = 1288 for a small effect, N = 206 for a medium effect, and N = 82 for a large effect.This suggests that the sample sizes in Experiments 2-4 (N = 122, 120, and 120) were adequately powered to detect a medium-large effect, but underpowered to detect small or medium effects (we observed small-medium effects; 2 = .03,.045,and .033).Experiment 5 (N = 400) was adequately powered to detect a medium effect, but not a small effect (we observed a medium effect; 2 = .057).

We also conducted post-hoc power analyses and report the power for each experiment given the sample sizes and observed effect sizes.Experiments 2-5 achieved a power of .52,.65,.48,and .998respectively.This confirms that Experiments 2-4 were statistically underpowered to detect the observed effects with very high confidence (e.g., power of .95), while Experiment 5 was sufficiently powered to detect the effect.

Experiment 1 used a different statistical design (logistic regression as opposed to ANOVA).This proves more challenging to compute the achieved power, because the standard effect size measure for logistic regression (odds ratio) is not a proportion of variance explained like 2 or Cohen's f, which is required for most power calculators.

However the sample size for Experiment 1 (N = 150, or N = 68 for some restricted analyses) suggest that the analysis may be underpowered to detect small and possibly medium effects.

8 General Discussion NOT SO SIMPLE!

Across five experiments, we find that the presence of mechanisms affects one's preference for simple or complex explanations.Overall, participants preferred explanations that included mechanisms (Experiments 2, 4, and 5). 13hen explanations did not contain mechanisms, preference depended on the causal structure invoked.In multiple-effect scenarios, participants preferred simple explanations that appeal to a single, common cause (Experiments 3, 4, and 5).This preference is predicted by probabilistic accounts of explanation (e.g., Jefferys & Berger, 1992) as well as non-probabilistic accounts that appeal to explanatory virtues (Lombrozo, 2007).In single-effect scenarios, results were mixed: participants showed a preference for simple explanations in Experiment 1, but no significant preference for simple or complex explanations in Experiment 2.

Critically, when explanations did contain mechanisms, we found strong evidence in favor of a shift of preferences (a mechanism effect).Mechanistic explanations reduced (Experiments 2, 3, and 5) or reversed (Experiment 1) the preference for simple explanations relative to complex ones.Mechanistic explanations also reduced the preference for simple explanations relative to complex ones in Experiment 4, though not significantly (p = .059).This effect appears to be driven largely by an increase in ratings for complex explanations when the explanations contained mechanisms (Experiments We propose that the mechanism effect occurs in part because mechanisms afford an additional way to evaluate explanations.When explanations do not contain mechanisms, people rely predominately on criteria such as subjective probability that allow them to determine 'what' the causes of an effect are (i.e., causal inference).This criterion often favors simple explanations, as simple explanations are often seen as more probable.When explanations do contain mechanisms, people can also evaluate the mechanisms themselves (i.e., 'how' a cause produces an effect).Mechanistic information increases subjective understanding (Vasilyeva & Lombrozo, 2015) and allows for improved predictions about the unknown (Johnson & Ahn, 2017).This NOT SO SIMPLE! criterion favors complex, multi-mechanism explanations that provide a fuller description of the causal relations.

In support of this theory, participants in the mechanism condition reported using qualitatively different cues to rate explanations (Experiments 2 and 3), incorporating factors such as informativeness and ease of understanding that played little or no role in rating explanations for participants in the no mechanism condition.Mediation analyses reveal that the presence of mechanisms shifted the criteria that participants report using to evaluate explanations, and that participants who referred to these criteria (such as informativeness) were more likely to exhibit a shift in preference towards complex explanations (Experiments 2 and 3).Ratings of mechanism informativeness collected in Experiment 5 corroborate that complex mechanisms are rated as more informative, and these ratings are correlated with explanation ratings for participants in the mechanism condition.We have argued that participants value mechanisms in part because they increase subjective understanding and improve the ability to make predictions (Vasilyeva & Lombrozo, 2015;Johnson & Ahn, 2017).However we cannot say for sure that this is true in our data: participants in our experiments did not rate their own understanding and were not tested on their ability to make inductive inferences.

Our theory could benefit from a more refined definition of informativeness.We suggested that explanations that contain more mechanisms are more informative because they provide a fuller description of how the effects are produced (as opposed to what the causes are).In the present experiments, informativeness is measured with subjective ratings (Experiment 5) and by coding open-ended responses that often (though not always) referenced an explanation's "information" or "detail" explicitly NOT SO SIMPLE! (Experiments 2 and 3).This differs from prior work that has measured informativeness by asking participants "the extent to which the information tells you something new" Hilton, 1996), or by contrasting explanations that vary in ambiguity (e.g., disjunctions versus conjunctions; Li et al., 2020).Future work might evaluate formal definitions of informativeness such as minimum description length of a causal model (Mian, Marx, & Vreeken, 2021), or by relating it to other information-theoretic constructs like information gain between two causal models (Steyvers, Tenenbaum, Wagenmakers, & Blum 2003).Future work should disentangle this hypothesis from other possible explanations of the mechanism effect.For example, mechanisms may change the way people think about how and whether two causes interact (i.e., two causes might be perceived as independent without mechanisms, but conjunctive when mechanisms are present).

Mechanisms may also alter which probabilities guide explanation preference.Johnson, Valenti, and Keil (2019) found that people rate simple explanations as having a higher prior probability, but that complex explanations are rated as more likely to explain the data.The causal structures used in their experiment mirror those of Experiments 3-5, contrasting a common cause (simple) versus separate causes (complex) in explaining two effects.It is possible that because mechanisms describe the process by which the effects are produced, participants shift their focus from the prior probability to the likelihood of explaining the data, increasing the preference for complex explanations.

Related work has found that simplicity can act as a guide to estimating prior probabilities, both in explanation and in curve fitting (Johnson, Jin, & Keil, 2014;Vrantsidis & Lombrozo, 2022).NOT SO SIMPLE!This alternative hypothesis, like ours, assumes that mechanisms affect the cues that people rely on to guide explanation preference.Unlike our hypothesis, it does not imply that there is a qualitative difference in how people perceive the task of choosing an explanation.That is, it does not imply that people shift from evaluating "what" happened to "how" it happened.Instead, it suggests that mechanisms change the way people use probability to evaluate explanations.The two hypotheses are not mutually exclusive, and future work could explore their relative contribution to the mechanism effect.

Our results are consistent with previous findings that people have an intrinsic bias for simple, common cause explanations (Lombrozo, 2007;Read & Marcus-Newhall, 1993) and that complex, common effect explanations are preferred when mechanisms are present (Zemla, Sloman, Bechlivanidis, & Lagnado, 2017).However, mechanistic detail is clearly not the only factor that influences one's preference for simple or complex explanations.Other factors such as the content domain and stochasticity affect preferences for simple explanations (Johnson, Valenti, and Keil, 2019), and people seek explanations that match the complexity of causes and effects (Lim & Oppenheimer, 2020).Our results are the first to implicate mechanistic detail as a moderating factor.This provides further insight into how people evaluate explanations and reconcile some previous findings for when complex explanations triumph over simple ones.

Our experiments contain several limitations.In all five experiments, participants judged which explanation was "the best," and not which explanation was "most likely" or "most satisfying" (a phrasing often used, e.g., Lombrozo, 2007;Weisberg et al., 2008).
