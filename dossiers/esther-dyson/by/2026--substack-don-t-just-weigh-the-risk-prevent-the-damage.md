# Don’t just weigh the risk. Prevent the damage!

## Full text

*Author’s note: The subject of this Substack is too timely to wait a week for the regular bi-weekly publishing schedule, so I decided to post this now. The next post will be two weeks from today, on April 21!*

You probably noticed the [two amazing victories](https://www.npr.org/2026/03/25/nx-s1-5746125/meta-youtube-social-media-trial-verdict) against predatory business practices last week, when two juries in Los Angeles and New Mexico ruled against YouTube/Google and Facebook/Meta for harming and endangering children by design. Let’s acknowledge the irreversible devastation already wrought, bask in victory for a moment … and then figure out how to move forward, away from filing lawsuits and toward preventing the damage in the first place. Spoiler alert: The answer can be found in the most boring of market sectors: insurance. But first, let’s briefly explore what happened.

Instead of a vote by a broad public for a TV show, the juries each comprised 12 thoughtful adults committed to listening and learning in order to discover the truth. They read documents and listened to testimony from experts and company executives and whistleblowers, and probably considered their own experiences. They weren’t legal scholars grounded in [section 230 of the Communications Decency Act](https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R46751), which limits the liability of online platforms (i.e. passive websites) for content posted by users, and that disregards the role that underlying algorithms and affordances might play. They were normal people, with a sense of ethics.

Certainly, one of the most satisfying things about the recent verdicts was the use of appropriate analogies to describe what was going on. The companies tried to present their case as freedom of speech and consumer choice/autonomy vs. over-regulation and restriction of children’s freedom. Hard to argue with, right?

Maybe. But once you see behind the curtain, perhaps it’s more like cigarettes, opioids and - MAHA alert! - ultra-processed, metabolism-damaging food. Just as sugars (including artificial sweeteners), ultra-processed foods and additives mess with your metabolism and physical health, so does ultra-processed content mess with your mental metabolism, reducing your ability to process content while at the same time luring you to “consume” (not to interact with) more and more of it. Call that condition “information diabetes.” And now, we face ultra-processed AI companions and advisors - usually luring you into something that’s bad for you, and driven by a business model that wants you to be addicted rather than challenged. (They call it “customer retention.”)

Those analogies and metaphors won’t go away, precisely because they are so apt. Social media offerings and now chatbots and AI agents are not (free) speech; they are services and tools, run by algorithms and with personalization, akin to drugs or medical devices that mess with your mind.

So, we won a few lawsuits. Where do we go now? Is there a way to fight in advance, instead of waiting until the damage happens, when it’s too late to do anything other than sue to collect money and forced apologies? The judgments awarded were $6 million (LA) and $375 million (New Mexico) for what could - *should* - be just the first of many such trials - or settlements. But who would take money in exchange for the life of their child? That question is almost too horrifying to ask. Yet every day all sorts of companies are prioritizing money over the lives and well-being of children and adults around the world.

Let’s not even try to quantify the value of human lives … but now we can quantify the likely damages if you lose a lawsuit. The suits against Google and Meta follow well-worn paths established with Big Tobacco, and more recently with opioids and the Sacklers. When you bring in liability, suddenly the whole set-up changes. The Sacklers and their company Purdue Pharma faced their first lawsuit (for deceptive marketing) back in 2001; many lawsuits followed. In 2019, the company filed for bankruptcy to evade the liabilities. Late last year, the lawsuit was finally settled, for about $7 billion, to be paid off over the next 15 years. (Note: In that case, the Sackler family owned the company; in these new cases, the liability would be held by officers and directors of Meta et al.)

But here’s one big idea - a different analogy - that might help things move faster this time around: fire insurance, which doesn’t wait for the damage to be done.

Take what happened with fire protection of buildings in the early 20th century: Fire insurance companies and their inspectors led the way, ultimately joined by the government, in establishing building codes and formal regulations requiring building owners to buy fire insurance. The message was clear: If you have the capacity to harm, you have the obligation to avert the harm, or be ready to fix or pay for that harm. This led to a huge and sustainable business focused on safety-as-a-service (well before such terminology existed): Insurance companies diligently inspected properties to ensure that the building codes were being observed, that oily rags were disposed of, that emergency exits were accessible and opened from inside…and to notice things early rather than wait for an infraction. Following their own financial incentives, they went beyond formal fire codes in making their insurance projections and premium-pricing decisions.

In short, the best insurers of all kinds - fire, home, cybersecurity, general liability, etc. - reduce risks by inspecting the premises, digging deep into records and pricing coverage justifiably high…or simply refusing coverage. The question is not just how you fill in the forms, but how you actually behave. What are the tells, whether dirty rags or termites…or disaffected employees leaving at high rates (with non-disparagement agreements)?

In short, what we need is not a huge and quickly-out-of-date regulatory regime, but a few simple rules that mostly mimic what already exists. The industry itself should ask Congress to pass a law requiring social media/chatbot/AI agent companies (and their accountable senior officials and board members) to purchase liability insurance in order to offer their products to the public, even - or especially - when they are free. (That’s in the industry’s best interests overall, because it will keep out, or at least deter, the bad actors who might win without guardrails and who will tarnish the whole industry.)

Then the SEC should require any public social media or AI company to *disclose *their insurance payments - which private company investors will be savvy enough to ask about to protect themselves. Ironically, collecting that information is a good task for AI operated by the insurers: inspecting not just fire doors and oily rags, but searching through the kind of internal documents and operational tells that eventually got the Sacklers and Meta and many more on the hook. They can assess all that “sentiment analysis” valuing profits over customer lives, or discover an internal conversation (true story revealed in court!) where one Meta researcher wrote “IG [Instagram] is a drug,” and a colleague responded “LOL, I mean, all social media. We’re basically pushers.” That’s a better indication of risk than corporate mission statements.

So, if you know any insurance people, get them involved - or send them my way, please! They have a huge role to play here. The goal is not to win a lot of lawsuits, but to assess the risks that lead to them. Use the need for insurance not to spread the risk, but to *prevent* it. Good insurance companies are known by the risks they turn down, by the unworthy customers they refuse.
