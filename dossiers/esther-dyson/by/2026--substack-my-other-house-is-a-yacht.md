# "My other house is a yacht"

## Full text

*Author’s note: I’m a pretty cheerful person. I’d rather fix things than complain, and I’d rather talk about possibilities than disasters. But right now, I feel compelled to take note of the extraordinary, terrifying state of the world. I’m wondering where all the grown-ups have gone… (Well, it turns out they are just the same nuts that I - and maybe you - knew in kindergarten and high school: older, but not wiser.) Maybe it’s up to us, imperfect as we are, to fix. Back to our regular programming in two weeks!*

Long ago, on a summer Sunday, I was walking to my pool - the Downtown Athletic Club - and randomly took a path on the walkways beneath the towering World Trade Center.

A month later, I was in Bulgaria, at a conference about reconstructing the governance of Eastern Europe. The participants were wise people from the West - politicians, lawyers, activists and journalists - plus their counterparts from the East - would-be politicians, lawyers, activists and some existing journalists - eager to learn and to build a new world of transparent, freely elected governments; sensible laws; and fair courts and judges.

After hanging out a bit with the participants - what an amazing group of noble, altruistic people to be part of! - I went to a small, windowless office to check my email. My business partner in New York City had sent me a brief note: “We are all okay!” Weird, but whatever! There was more important stuff to read. Someone popped their head in to tell me that there had been an airplane crash in New York. Sad, I thought. But whatever! Then I checked the news: The World Trade Center I had walked through a month earlier was on fire and pouring out smoke.

All afternoon, both in that little office and back in the crowd, people came by to console me, to express concern and hope. It was about the best place in the world I could imagine being in at that moment: With people who had lived through dictatorships, corruption and censorship, and hoped to build a better world. People who believed in the power of the Internet and freedom to unite us all. People who were actively working for that better world - running for office, writing laws, working to uncover and document the past so it couldn’t happen again.

The next day one of the women attending and I started to talk. With a little encouragement, she said what others would not: “We are really so sorry for you guys. You mean so well; you have such good principles. But really, you have no clue what it’s like for the rest of the world. You’re like a rich kid living in an elegant mansion on the hill. You’re nice and generous. You invite us to your birthday parties. Your butler treats us with respect.

“But you have no curiosity about our lives. We live in a different world. We’ve had the Irish troubles; we had World War II and the Iron Curtain. Our countries were bombed and some of us were sent to prison camps. And of course many of us…were lost. We suffered under dictators like Stalin, Tito, Mussolini. So we wish you well, but we wish you just understood our reality!”

After the conference, I kept to my original itinerary, and stayed in Europe. There were long security lines at the airports, and the flights back were all canceled anyway, but everything else was in order. As for me, I felt odd. I was just glad I wasn’t in New York. Fortunately no one I knew was missing, and I wasn’t needed…the street where I lived, 12th Street, was within the residents-only restricted area authorities had established to keep crowds away from the wreckage of the Twin Towers; I would need some kind of pass to get home.

When I did come home, I returned to devastation. For the next few weeks, you needed a pass to cross the barricades blocking everything below 14th Street. I found a replacement for the Downtown Athletic Club at a smaller place nearby, and for six months I swam there, just blocks from the former WTC. The whole area smelled like a damp fireplace - which we now know probably caused lung cancer for many of the rescue workers and for others less directly involved except by location. Every morning, when I arrived at the pool for my daily swim (before the place fully opened up), I saw volunteer workers serving coffee to emergency workers in the lobby. One thing was clear: What you could destroy in minutes, you can fix only in years.

This all came flashing back to me when Iran started the war. (Just kidding!) I thought more about the wisdom that woman in Bulgaria had shared. I imagined the US as a rich, teen-age kid, welcoming, but oblivious to the lives of others outside his family’s mansion. The butler politely treats the visiting kids as if they belonged.

The gate in front of the mansion on the hill is high, with a guard house nearby. But things are starting to crumble. The foundation of his mansion is rotting, succumbing to mold and neglect. You can now see the beginnings of collapse. The mansion is crumbling and falling apart… The lights are on in some windows above, but there are weeds below. The foundation is rotting at the seams, succumbing to mold at the edges. To the side, you can see a stream of sewage from under the wall running down the hill, with a few broken syringes, some fancy torn clothing, a discarded Meta Quest 3 headset.

And it’s the kid’s fault. He lives alone with his staff. Somehow his family, who built the mansion, are gone.

From the outside, the kid is no longer seen as oblivious, but at times vicious and predatory, and at other times like a kid throwing his own toys at the wall. Once respected at least by some, he is still feared, even as his words are disregarded…the same as AI slop, but coming from a human. Perhaps that kid, obsessed with AI, thinks it’s easy to reprogram things, press a button, and start over. But society - along with buildings and other infrastructure - isn’t programmed. It has to grow, like any complex, living thing.

When will we wake up from this nightmare? And when we do, what can we do when we’re awake? Will the damage be beyond repair? Or can we learn enough to grow back better?
