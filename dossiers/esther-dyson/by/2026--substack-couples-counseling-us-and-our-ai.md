# Couples counseling: Us and our AI

## Full text

It is a truth universally acknowledged by couples counselors: Whatever’s bothering either person in the couple, it’s more about you – your reactions and your actions, the only things completely in your control – than about *them*. What is triggering you about the other person? Why does an innocent remark that you would brush off from someone else inspire so much angst when it comes from your partner? What are you demanding or expecting that - let’s face it - is unrealistic? Why do you let their words and actions so affect you, instead of understanding where they are coming from so that you can honorably and openly negotiate with, rather than manipulate, *them*? (Of course, if they won’t do the same work on their side, the options for your (re)actions may be rather limited.)

And what if the relationship in question is not between you and another person, but you and a *thing *- food or money or AI? It would help us all to heed this relationship truth in our interactions with things. Indeed, I’m writing a whole book about how to be human in the age of AI. While many people are writing books about AI from all sides, I’m more concerned with* people* and their *relationship* with AI….which may require some counseling.

The trick of having a good relationship with AI is understanding it - and its limitations. It is to avoid being seduced by it or misled by it…but also to avoid blaming it. For a healthy relationship, you need to understand what business models or other motivations lie behind the company and people that created it. And then you need to figure out how to get it to do what *you* want.

Before we get to AI, let’s explore couples counseling between people and “things” in other arenas. I like to half-joke that I have invested in several couples-counseling companies - one about money and the other about food. These apps help you recognize that food and money don’t need you or shame you; they are just inert, passive objects to consume or trade. But *you* react to *them*!

The first is [TheBeans.io](http://thebeans.io), as in “counting beans.” Its mission is not so much to help you manage your money, as to prompt you to consider how you *think *about your money. Do you see everything as a trade-off, or do you focus more on value for money? Do you buy things you don’t really need, when what you actually need is an expression of love from your partner or gratitude from your boss? For example, why do I blithely spend $300 on a hotel room but think twice about a time-saving Waymo ride for $20? Partly, it reflects my relationship with money through life-long training. I started paying for hotels long ago, so I don’t much notice spending that money. But spending money on a private car hits different somehow; I don’t drive and have always defaulted to subways and buses and lifts from friends whenever I can. A couples counselor might suggest that I value the time lost in transit too little!

The other is [EatMyWay.com](http://eatmyway.com), about you and your food. It helps you examine your feelings and habits around food, often based on early training: Do you automatically finish your vegetables first and save the steak as a reward? Do you eat so fast you can’t actually taste the food? Does certain food call to you? Does it shame you for wanting it (the way your mother used to)? As for me, I know I sleep better if I don’t eat dinner, but the value of time with friends (or business contacts) *at* dinner often outweighs the missed sleep. Relationships often require trade-offs - but ideally, I can make them thoughtfully.

Then there’s a very different example that shows that couples-counseling techniques can be used - usefully and carefully - to *create* a fake relationship where none existed. In this example, it is done in a way to make it explicit that it’s not a real relationship, even though some might find it creepy. As related by Stewart Brand in his recent book “[Maintenance: Of Everything](https://www.amazon.com/Maintenance-Everything-Part-One/dp/1953953492),” the US Army was trying to solve the problem that soldiers in the Vietnam War weren’t taking sufficient care of their poorly designed M16 rifles; those rifles were routinely getting gummed up with dirt, backfiring and otherwise harming the soldiers themselves instead of the enemy. So, the idea was to create a [comic book](https://archive.org/details/1968m16A1/mode/2up) that nudged the soldiers to treat their guns like lovers. A bit icky, but transparent and bold enough to be funny rather than manipulative. And it probably leaned in effectively to what was on soldiers’ minds anyway.

As Brand recounts: “It was a jokey 32-page comic book drawn by the revered comic artist Will Eisner. Its first three pages, titled ‘How To Strip Your Baby,’ illustrated the nineteen steps it took to disassemble the M16. The text began with flirty language: ‘You want to know her inside out, every contour and curve, every need and whim, what makes her tick’ ” …and outlines how to manage a relationship with that pesky, breakdown-prone device.

What we need now is a class of couples counselors mediating between us and AI; understanding their counterparts in these human-AI partnerships is vital both for individuals and for society as a whole. Such an AI counselor should start by helping you see clearly what it *itself* is: Not a companion or a participant with an agenda, but rather a metabot that looks out for you vis à vis other bots and AI affordances. How can we develop and sustainably fund such a genuinely useful and disinterested tool? A good therapist doesn’t automatically support the client’s worldview, unlike so many sycophantic, seductive chatbots. A good therapist starts by asking questions “as an observer,” not suggesting answers. And they focus on you and your needs, not on the “other” that you do not control. As in “real” life, you need to know who is friend and who is foe.

Your counselor should be a trustworthy AI tool that is genuinely on *your side* in a world riddled with tools and bots that claim to help you. It should remind you not to automatically believe what another AI or AI-based business tells you. It could help you ask important questions and tell you awkward truths about your interactions with each of the various AIs you deal with: Is the AI a tool you control? Or is it working for some person or company trying to evoke some behavior on your side: buying something you can’t afford on credit, watching an ad, renewing a subscription, among other possibilities? Such AI bots may be trying to manage your behavior to keep you paying; They are not predators; they are parasites. The couples counselor AI would remind you to avoid succumbing to the sycophancy of a bot that offers you short-term satisfaction by always taking your side - and lures you into long-term addiction to behavior that you will regret.

The whole point is for the person to manage their own behavior and reactions rather than to engage in yet another fraught relationship. There is nothing “there” on the other side. With people, yes, it’s about another person. But with AI - it’s only a tool. Who is using it? You…or someone else.

Somewhat surprisingly, the awkward reality is that having an AI bot (not a human) ask awkward questions may often be the best response to our current situation of stressed-out people and scarce human therapists. As Peterson Health Technology Institute concludes in a [discussion of this issue](https://phti.org/patients-use-ai-chatbots-for-mental-health-leaders-must-catch-up/): “Patients are counting on us to lead with guidelines that promote access and better clinical outcomes. Expanding access [through AI] without appropriate safeguards isn’t supporting innovation - it’s irresponsible. At the same time, failure to act isn’t caution - it’s a choice that serves no one.” Yes indeed…as long as these AI therapy bots are built to purpose, trained on vetted therapy techniques, and not generic bots like Claude or ChatGPT!

And FWIW, please consider this Substack post as coming from a bot (though I am a person!). In the end, I’m not sure whether this is a political/social problem created by lack of trust, or a mental-health problem created by the loss of human interaction, or both. Consider me your first counselor: All I have done is ask the questions. It’s up to you to find the answers!
