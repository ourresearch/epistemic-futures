# The Way to Wellville: How to Stop Global Sickening

## Full text

Two years ago I started a project called the Way to Wellville to help five small communities produce health – a demonstration to show what it would look like if we just did the basics of what we know we should do, in terms of early child care and education, nutrition, facilities for exercise, access to healthy food, and a variety of preventive interventions against obesity, diabetes, mental distress and so on.

I knew I didn't know much, so I hired a CEO who knows a lot more. We launched a call for applications from communities of under 100,000 people, self-contained (for greater self-reinforcing impact and easier community data collection), with some existing coalition focused on improving community health. Amazingly, 42 communities applied; we picked 10 visit and finally chose five: Clatsop County OR; Lake County CA; Muskegon MI; Niagara Falls NY; and Spartanburg, SC. Now we are a full-time team of five, each of us responsible for one of the communities. (Mine is lovely Muskegon, on the eastern shore of Lake Michigan.

I won't speak for all of us, but here's what I've learned so far.

The way I began was as an economist, looking at markets. There are three markets involving health: health care, multiple trillions of dollars covering hospitals, doctors, clinics, medicines, devices, procedures. This is what the arch now call "sick care." It's basically a repair function, trying to undo damage, much of which could have been averted.

Then there's the market for bad health which the arch call "modern life"; they are things that often damage health, especially when consumed in abundance: unhealthy food (sweetened, processed with chemicals); alcohol, drugs and tobacco; and all the distractions that get in the way of exercise, sleep and human connection and that often lead to a vicious downward cycle – mental distress, physical distress, joblessness, incarceration, homelessness, addiction and violence. Just one of these will open up a hole and draw a person down, but once a person's in the hole the challenges multiply. 

And finally, there's the pathetically small market for good health: gyms, good food, comfortable pillows... So many of the accoutrements of good health are almost free. And that's the problem. No one is incentivized to sell them. And they don't generally work without some complementary help from the individual. 

Yes, now we have Fitbits and Quantified Self and the like, which are sold primarily to mostly healthy and well-off people. They are useful, but they aren't going to solve the problem of global sickening.

**Global sickening** 

I call it global sickening because, like global warming, it threatens our collective future. The immediate threat is to our bodies and minds, eventually spreading to the society and economy those bodies and minds produce and embody...literally. And like global warming, it's everyone's problem even though its impact is uneven. Also like global warming, it will cost a lot less to address it now than to try, to undo the damage in the future.

Some of the problem is timing: Cultivating good health now does not seem urgent – especially health in the bodies of others. Indeed, some of the challenge is around who pays for the things that cultivate good health. But it's not just those who get sick who will lose out if global sickening prevails. It's third parties: taxpayers, employers, and all who suffer when those around them are sick and using costly remedial health care. 

Meanwhile, the products and services of the market for bad health are attractive and lucrative to the sellers, but they impose future costs on society. Fortunately, we're getting better at attributing and predicting those costs; just as we count and charge for carbon emissions (to an increasing extent), so could we count added sugar, the lack of pre-natal care in a health plan, the prioritization of cars over walkability, the use of painkillers over other forms of therapy from those who could benefit – in short, anything that broadly undermines health

Imagine a sugar or alcohol tax with the proceeds going to healthy school lunches or free health coaching. 

It would at least be easier to pass than a tax with the proceeds going to fund generic "government spending." And indeed, the Department of Health and Human Services has just recommended that Medicare pay for not just diabetes treatment, but diabetes prevention.

**The market for good health**

That's a huge step, and the obvious next step is for Medicaid to pay for diabetes prevention for the rest of us - people under 65. Our little team of five at Way to Wellville doesn't have much voice in that discussion, but we are trying to help five small communities provide an example of what it looks like when a community supports health rather than eroding it. To move from treating sickness to cultivating health, we believe we need to learn from the markets that have already scaled. We need smart innovation, and a market for it that assesses results and provides feedback, and ultimately picks winners and losers. We need investment that accelerates growth for the programs and products that truly cultivate health. And we need a change in the way people measure and value and invest in health that a market for the cultivation of health will foster. After all, health really is an asset, and when we create the mechanisms to produce and value it as such, we -- families, employers, institutions and society -- will all benefit over the long run. 

Some health-cultivating organizations already exist, but few of them have really scaled so far. This is why the industrial approach to scaling is critical. One of the oldest is Nurse-Family Partnership, a nonfprofit that has been training nurses to support at-risk pregnant mothers, taking them through the first two years after their first birth. The ovreall, validated return on investment – in terms of reduced pre-term births and neo-natal intense care units, as well as a reduction in behavioral and health problems for both mothers and (grown) babies 15 years later - is on the order of 500 percent, enough to impress former Director of the Office of Budget and Management Peter Orszag. And there's Parenting Journey, a program to help parents who may have been abused themselves reduce the risk of inflicting damage on their own kids. And the accredited Diabetes Prevention Program supported by HHS, being delivered by entities ranging from the YMCA to start-ups like Canary Health, Blue Mesa and Omada Health, now a 40,000-customer venture capital-backed company (in which I have an investment). There are others focusing on behavioral health, new and proven coaching techniques, and even products/services to address many of the necessities of daily life – transportation, food access, and safety – without which our most vulnerable citizens lose the capacity to sustain themselves. 

The model that seems to work best starts with subject matter expertise, developed and refined on the basis of sound evidence. It might be delivered through an app or a website; it might inform the workflow and ministrations of health coaches, whether in-person, voice-to-voice or on-line. Bots are scalable in ways coaches and other medical professionals aren't, but human-to-human interaction has benefits that can outweigh the economies of scale – especially in a world where so many other jobs are being replaced by bots. We're just at the beginning of learning what will work best, not just in general, but for specific people defined by body type, health conditions, personality and all the other factors that make each of us unique.

In the end, it's not just the model that counts. It's effective scaling *enabled* by the model. Right now, most health institutions – from weight-loss classes at the gym to early childhood education to self-help groups – are closer to Ma's Burger Joint than to McDonald's. Many are hyper-local and nonprofit, and they raise money based on niceness rather than effectiveness.

But accountability - and the resulting focus on delivery and ultimately scaling of what works - is needed. That's starting to happen now, especially with the Accountable Care Act, but not very broadly and not very fast. In our five communities, we hope to accelerate this trend: First by helping local leaders identify local challenges and define achievable success that elicits stakeholder buy-in and accountability.

And second by inviting health vendors to address these challenges and then holding them accountable for results.The health vendors who start with evidence-based programs and continue to improve them, who find coaches with empathy and train them on skills, who run their businesses effectively and focus on results will be successful, along with their investors, their clients and the communities they live in.

What's needed is the scale – the reach, the replicable models, the accountability – of a real industry and a market for good health, where the effective get more investment and can scale, and the ineffective release resources that can be better used elsewhere. As long as these entities profit based on producing health outcomes, the profit motive and the common good will be aligned...and global sickening will be averted.

*For more about the Way to Wellville, please see www.wellville.net. We're just at the beginning, and it's a long, complicated journey. We'll keep posting about our progress.*
