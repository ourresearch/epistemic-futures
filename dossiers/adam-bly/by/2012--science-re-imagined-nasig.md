# Science Re-Imagined

## Full text

### INTRODUCTION

At Seed Media Group, reimagining science includes changing how the public interacts with science, how scientists do science, and really the place of science within the world at large.For us the catalyst for reimagining science was not business-related, but was really rooted in the conviction that science has unique potential to improve the state of the world.As a mission-driven organization, what can we do to ensure that science reaches its full potential?I want to offer first a brief perspective on the forces changing science today and its place in society so that it is possible to understand better how we are trying to drive changes in science, how the public interacts with science, and also how scientists conduct their business.

In every era great enlightenments and great revolutions have come coincident with new ways of looking at the world.Today we have new ways of visualizing information, and new ways of seeing connections in the world.34

### Vision Sessions

Some of those are giving light to the kinds of revolutions Seed is trying to bring forth.Not only are we seeing new things; we now have sophisticated technologies and the ability to manipulate the world around us as never before.These incredible transformations will have far reaching implications on energy, on healthcare, and likely on domains that we have not even imagined yet.

The other consequence of these kinds of transformations, of our newfound ability to manipulate nature, is that they are creating a new dynamic between science and society, specifically, a new need for an ethical framework for where science fits within the world.Society will also need new sets of policies and regulations around the world, because our capacity to manipulate nature and change our environments will only continue to increase over time.How do we anticipate the changes of the future without inhibiting future research?How do we lay the ethical and moral framework in society and have the necessary conversation as a society to be able to guide science?

While science itself is changing, and new questions are giving birth to new disciplines, these questions are giving birth to new moral and ethical frameworks, and the world itself is changing around science.Science is by no means a closed system; it is very much a permeable system that both influences and is influenced by the world around it.Today as we see the rise of science elsewhere than in the United States, the West, and in the scientific superpowers that dominated twentieth-century science, we see cultures of science that, in some respects, superficially look very similar to our own.But here are also cultures of science profoundly distinct from that which we have here in the United States or that we recognize in Western societies.The approaches to investigation, hypothesis generation, understanding the natural world differs in China, the Arab world, and in other cultures that are now starting to reconnect with their profoundly rich scientific roots-cultures that have a stronger, deeper connection, in fact, to science, than we have as newer societies.As these cultures around the world start to recognize that science has the capacity to transform their economic development and to spearhead major changes in society this recognition, too, will have a consequence on the culture of science.Not only will new advances and technologies, the output of science, increase from countries that we previously did not associate with this kind of robust scientific output, but the very culture of science itself, the way we think scientifically, could very well be impacted by new approaches to scientific thinking around the world.

At the same time, we recognize today, in an unparalleled fashion, that this world we live in is more interconnected than ever before.To understand any single issue that is on the global agenda today requires that we see it in the context of a system.To understand disease in a particular environment, we need to understand climate.To understand climate, we need to understand energy.To understand energy, we need to think about economic growth and our demands on an economy.To think about economic growth, we need to think about population dynamics.To think about population dynamics, well, we need to think about disease trajectories.Every single thing is linked, and we recognize these interconnections perhaps more so than any time in history because we are, in fact, also more networked.

The other hallmark of this moment is the plethora of data at our disposal.We are now producing more data each year than the combined sum of all prior human history, and that statistic is profoundly mesmerizing and astonishing.It is coming from many different sources: our use of the Internet, creation of electronic health records, and massive scientific undertakings, like the modeling of the planet, climate systems, mass genomic sequencing projects, and so forth.Simply put, the amount of data that we are now producing is creating a moment of incredible opportunity.People can now get reports of their own genomes in the mail.Imagine the extraordinary potential that this one example provides: we now have not only the abundance of all of this data as a society and can use it to advance a new type of science, but also at a personal level we can take ownership of this kind of data.To navigate these data and make decisions responsibly, we will have to have the requisite information literacy.For information scientists and the library community in general, this is an extraordinary moment of opportunity to recognize that without the requisite science literacy and information literacy, we will not be able to manage, manipulate, or reap the benefits of it.And those who are most fluent with information, with an abundance of information, have the opportunity to educate society now in profound ways that we have never thought before.

Not only are we seeing this abundance in the societal context, the capacity for tremendous amounts of data gives rise to new types of science and permits work across a variety of different disciplines.Data abundance is now becoming also the basis for interdisciplinary science in a way that we could not have anticipated before.So, as scientists start to visualize information, and develop common visual languages to annex and integrate data from varieties of disciplines, they create a new framework to begin to catalyze the kind of holistic, interdisciplinary science that many have anticipated and hoped would come.Now we have a new opportunity to develop visual languages and interfaces that are rooted in a common pursuit of understanding, as opposed to one that might be rooted in any one particular discipline.

This new moment created by our ability to catalyze interdisciplinary and holistic thinking by visualizing huge amounts of data puts us now in the context of a revolution.Remember, Seed Media's mission has been to recognize the uniqueness of science to improve the state of the world, and we see science as having a potential unlike any other facet of society.Not just because science is a source of economic benefit or political change, but for other reasons as well that I think define a new way of looking at science in the twenty-first century.

### PRECONDITIONS FOR ACHIEVING SCIENCE'S POTENTIAL

Science's unique potential to change the world for the better hinges on and is ultimately determined by two major changes that need to occur in the way we think about science and the way we do science.These two preconditions for achieving science's potential follow.

First, we must change the way we think about science literacy.Since the days of Sputnik, we have equated science literacy to producing more scientists.We measured how we trained society for scientific times based on how many scientists we produced.Yet, in order to navigate today's new landscape of global science, we need to start thinking about science literacy not as the addition of another 10,000 scientists or even another 100,000 scientists, but as the pursuit of seven billion scientifically literate people.Certainly, laying out the goal of every single person on the planet being scientifically literate means we need to rethink what it means to be scientifically literate, and it also means that we then need to rethink how we educate people around the world to have this scientific literacy.

The second precondition is open science.We need to recognize the imperative to transform science from its closed structure of the twentieth century, dominated by a few companies, using structures and cultures that simply do not work anymore; we need to re-architect science for the twentyfirst century to meet the needs and changes that are already happening both in society and in science.So these two big ideas-seven billion scientifically literate people and open science-are the two driving forces of our work at Seed Media.And so now, let me break them up a little bit and share some thoughts on each.

### First: Changing the Way We Engage with Science

In order to educate seven billion scientifically literate people, we need both a new philosophy of scientific literacy and new modes of engaging the world in science.Science is clearly and increasingly recognized as a source of good in the world.Science creates medicines, and new technologies, and has positive economic impacts on the world.Science's output is important also because it has cultural implications.When I first started Seed, the tag line of the magazine was "science as culture," and saying that seven or eight years ago was bold.It was acknowledging that science's outputs in the world have cultural ramifications, and transform the way we look at culture itself, that science would become our culture.Today, that seems fairly self-evident.It is happening.The bigger idea, and the one that needs to drive a new philosophy of engagement and science literacy, is that science is not just about its output.Science is fundamentally a methodology.It is a philosophy, a way of looking at the world and understanding systems.Science is a way of looking for patterns, and a way of building arguments and subjecting them to empiricism.Science is a system of thought that can be turned against the biggest problems in the world, even those that we do not recognize as being scientific.

Over the last few years, we have started to see the birth of this notion that science is a lens through which we can solve the world's problems.We can look at conflict, financial markets, the growth of cities, development, peace, systems that are very far removed from topics that we normally associate with being scientific.As we start deploying this lens, thinking about the justice system, death row, or human rights, thinking about the kinds of things that affect our daily lives, and through which we reap the most significance in our world, we must realize that science has rarely, if ever, had a seat at the table of thinking about the systems that are not scientific.That is beginning to change.So to the extent that we can now start mobilizing science, and bringing it to where thinking is happening around these non-scientific systems, we start to recognize that thinking scientifically, having the capacity to change someone's mind with new evidence, having the capacity to turn scientific ideas into the basis for looking at systems that are not scientific, is really what we need of seven billion people.

The corollary of this goal of scientific literacy is to engage differently with science, because we need for people to see science very differently than the way they have seen it before.They need to learn science very differently.Accomplishing this goal will not be brought about by graduating more scientists.We need more scientists.Having them is a good thing, but it is nowhere near the big goal for now.Engaging people with science through culture and ideas is one of the goals that we have worked on for the last decade at Seed Media Group.We have tried, through the pages of Seed, through books, through all sorts of special periodicals, to take time to slow down and introduce several ideas: first, of science as culture, ten years ago, and also, over the last few years, of science as a lens.These are two big ideas that require the time to be checked, to be attacked, to be subject to peer review, and we felt that using this medium has been the right way of doing it and we will continue to have slow conversation about these changes happening in the world.

We also need to create a more robust culture of conversation around science.How can we create more robust tools to engage the world, and a substantial critical mass of people, in a conversation about science, about their fears, their anxieties and their hopes, about the things that make them angry, excited, or optimistic, so that we can have a truly frank and open conversation about science in the world?We have done this through platforms like Seed Salon and our science blogs that have now grown to reach 75 million people over the last five years.These venues create conversations that involve people who historically never would have engaged with science, people who come at it through education, arts, policy, or religion.This was not happening when we created science blogs five years ago.Journals would publish science and popular periodicals would then communicate that out into the public, and stop there.How can society continue that engagement?We think science blogs are one way to accomplish this.

The other major mode of engagement for us has been art and design.In 1883, Edwin Abbott wrote this now-famous book called Flatland, in which he described a two-dimensional universe that for the first time contemplated what it might be like to have that extra third dimension.1 He was not a scientist, and through this great work of literature, he introduced ideas that today are the cornerstone of a whole avenue of theoretical physics thinking about higher dimensional universes.And when great physicists today cite this English essayist Edwin Abbott as having best understood this notion of a higher dimensional universe, it starts to make me think about the role of art, and the arts generally, in advancing ideas that we later claim as being scientific.

A good example of this is a project that has been touring the world called "The Morning Line."The project begins with this idea of higherdimensional universes, so something fundamental to string theory, for example, to some of the big ideas in contemporary physics, requires that we think about a world that has, perhaps, eleven dimensions.The problem is we cannot understand an eleven-dimensional universe; we cannot visualize it.So this project was initiated by a great art catalyst in Europe, Francesca von Hapsburg, and a group of architects in New York, Aranda/Lasch, together with Paul Steinhardt, who is the Albert Einstein professor of physics at Princeton.What they have done is to take the idea of contemporary multidimensional universes and turn it over to architects and the artist Matthew Ritchie, who has long been fascinated with cosmology and physics, and start to develop an environment that best represents, best emulates, best catalyzes thinking about a higher dimensional universe.One of the top physicists in the world, some amazing young architects, and a brilliant artist are coming together to advance an idea that no single discipline could even begin to entertain on its own.

Another example comes from work by Neri Oxman, now at the Media Lab at MIT, bringing forth ideas of biology and introducing them in physical forms.She is taking algorithms or processes in nature, and exposing them through beautiful physical structures that might give us new insights into how we think about materials of the future, how we think about our environments, how we think about our bodies.She is really bringing together science and design in profoundly new ways.

Design is making us all scientists, again.One of the core convictions for us about rethinking science literacy has been that we were once all scientists.We did not grow up hating science; we became haters of science later in life.But we did not hate science at the beginning because we did not know that it was science.Then at some point, our teachers stupidly called it science, and associated that with an exam to learn about something and memorize something, and then that was it.Science becomes hard; it becomes inaccessible, when it is really very simple.We were once all scientists; we once all employed the methodologies of science.We once all had that innate curiosity about the world and built experiments, and tested things, and saw "this doesn't work; let's try it again."Now if we can buttress that initial enthusiasm with a real rationale, to make it fungible, and take it elsewhere in the world, knowingly-well, then, we have just catalyzed universal science literacy.The problem is that it is hard to do that through science, so one of the projects Seed Media has been working on has been with the Museum of Modern Art in New York, the MOMA, bringing together scientists and designers to expose how the coming together of these disciplines could catalyze that kind of process, and many others as well.

Through design, through projects like BioBricks, that began at MIT and is now a universal, global project, we can start taking genetics and developing them effectively into bricks, units, modules that could be brought together to create organisms.Suddenly genetics becomes a material of creation, something to work with, test, and develop, and now we see the next generation of science fairs of kids coming together and actually creating biological organisms using BioBricks.It is amazing stuff, and it comes a sort of design thinking, where builders can prototype, test things, be creative about problem-solving, but really what they are doing is science.So design is a way that we have tried to introduce a new portal into scientific thinking.

Second: Open Science This is the second big challenge: the future of science is open.Not because it should be, not because knowledge about the world that is produced by the world and funded by the world should belong to the world.Of course that is true, and if we ever forget that we delude ourselves into over-complicating the challenge of re-architecting science for the twenty-first century.This has to be a fundamental goal for those of us who care about information, about communicating and disseminating information, and providing people with the tools to be informed.We cannot over-complicate what I am about to say because at the end of the day this is too grand a goal, too important a challenge in this moment of information abundance.We see the significance of science and the need for making all scientific information available to anybody who desires it, because it needs to be.Because the changes that we are seeing in science and in society require that science be open for its progress.We are all in a moment right now in which science needs to be completely reimagined in terms of its architecture, its functioning, and its metrics, and this needs to happen in a bottom-up fashion.

The other big change was best described by a friend of mine, C.S. Kiang of Peking University, in Seed a few years ago.He said, "Complexity is the new science.Everything is complex; every problem in the world is a system.The disciplines are classified by people, but nature never recognizes them." 2 It is another seemingly very simple notion: that nature never recognized our subject disciplines.We created biology; we created physics; we created chemistry to understand the world.And it worked.But now the challenges in front of us require that we re-architect based on this prism of complexity.We can also look at the entire map of science, and look at 750,000 papers linked by common authors and clustered into scientific paradigms, 776 of them, and start to see where vectors are being drawn across the map of science.We see that the links between brain research and computer science are starting to form, and we start to see the links between ecology and neuroscience.We start to see the links between climate change and health.And we start to recognize that research itself is necessarily moving in this direction of interconnectedness, because this is where the edge of knowledge lives, and this is where society needs us to mobilize scientific inquiry.This is not just happening at a grand scale; this is also happening at an individual scale.Scientists, 65% of them, cite literature as having an influence on their science.I mentioned Edwin Abbott; he is by no means the only example.Proust was ahead of his time understanding neuroscience.Many other great thinkers in arts and literature are way ahead of the scientists in understanding these complex notions.And scientists care; there is that, too.We forget, every so often, that scientists both as individuals and as researchers care about the world that they live in, and can be mobilized to change their modes of inquiry, can be mobilized to address the challenges in the world that they, too, as citizens, care about.The problem is that the architecture of science, the Web that was created by scientists to organize information, networks, and people, is no longer ideal for science.Arguably it never was.And so today we sit at a moment where this entire community, publishers, librarians, and scientists, are all seemingly struggling with this notion, and I want to introduce some reasons why I think this is not working, and five core principles of how we might take it forward.

We need to move forward, because the research Web today is disorganized; it is fragmented and inefficient.Information is structured around artificial and outmoded disciplines.Innovations are driven to preserve a business model and not improve research.Innovations that we are seeing brought forth to modernize science, to modernize science publishing?We should never forget where they come from.Scientists deserve better and society needs better.This is a fundamental notion.We have to recognize that the inefficiency of the Web is a significant challenge that affects society at large.I just came from having this same conversation in Washington, and there is much greater recognition than there ever has been that government has a role to play here because the changes are starting to happen.Scientific research might just get regulated; it might just get legislated this way, but we should all start to think about the big ideas that need to drive those kinds of regulations and legislations.

The best part about this revolution is that scientists are not waiting.They never have.It is antithetical to the culture of science to wait when confronted with a challenge.When confronted with the pursuit of truth, and a dogmatic pursuit of it, scientists innovate.And yet, somehow we need to agree on common frameworks, some common ideas, in order to mobilize all of the players in science, even against some fervent resistance toward some first principles, and make open science scalable, sustainable and very simple because at the end of the day if what we are doing and changing does not reflect the endogenous behaviors of the research community, they will not get adopted.

### MAKING SCIENCE OPEN, SCALABLE, AND SUSTAINABLE

So to make open science scalable, sustainable, and simple, there are five first principles I have come to over the last couple of years.The first is a digital core.Today we are moving science more and more digitally.We are all moving more and more digitally, and that is great.That is a natural, important first step.But science still lives and breathes off fundamental units that themselves are analog.That unit of information is the paper.The amount of information contained within it, the way we use it, manipulate it, cite it, the way we throw it around, the data that we associate with it, and the funding sources that we associate with it; all of the various processes, publications, data, metadata, and everything that drives science and showcases an output of science needs not simply to live around these fundamental units that we just appropriate for a new science.We need to reimagine the very core of these units.The challenge here is that we should not be deluded into thinking there is no better way because the sources of the solutions today are the same players in an industry that has a vested interest in protecting this fundamental unit of the past, and we cannot simply innovate around it.We should not think that this is an intractable problem.We need a digital core that does not rely on the paper as the sole fundamental unit of science.

Second, we need mandated free flow of information.This, too, seems to me to be self-evident, and yet not a universally accepted principle.Scientific information that is funded by the public must be available to the public immediately.Government must regulate that, and information providers should not accept anything else.If we fund science, we should be able to disseminate it immediately.We should reinvent models of peer review, because the investment in peer review, in some respects, substantiates the non-free flow of information.There are costs involved in publishing information and so forth-we should subsidize that.We should make peer review an investment of the world and be done with it.That is an idea I have been introducing of late: let us just put peer review in the hands of society and regulate it by governments and nongovernmental organizations around the world.Let us just take it out of the hands of publishers, and then that is it: science gets funded; science gets published.

Third, we need open standards and interoperability.We are seeing some great innovation on open standards in terms of data, in terms of unique author IDs and Digital Object Identifiers (DOIs) and so forth, but the other problem is that we need to recognize that these standards often are borne by individual societies or by individual groupings of people.Rarely are they viewed systemically, and so these standards themselves do not link to one another and are not designed to be interoperable.So not only do we need the open standards, we need to think about how these standards can be interoperable.

Fourth, we need new ways of extracting knowledge from information, and fifth, we need modern metrics.We need to recognize that the current modes of tracking impact and influence, absolutely must change.They are currently a detriment to the advancement of science, and they have dire economic consequences on the very architecture of science.The people in this room have a huge role to play in changing these metrics.

Seed Media is working to advance modern metrics.One project we have is called Research Blogging where what we are trying to do is advance post-publication peer review around papers that are published, and then use these conversations to be able to assess immediately the direction and momentum of ideas and science.We can use conversations that happen immediately post-publication that are now happening in blogs and happening around the Web.How can we start to track those conversations, and then serve that back as new ways of understanding what today has momentum?What are scientists in physics reading about climate change, what are scientists in Russia talking about different than scientists in South Africa?

Research Blogging allows us to syndicate back to journals the conversations that are happening off-platform.In a very distributed, organic way, these are conversations that are happening.We are providing tools to allow scientists to tag those conversations, assign DOIs to them, and then feed that back, with metadata, into our system so that we can do a few things with that information.One is that we can serve it back to the scientists who are the sources of the original material so that we can respond to their natural conversational behaviors.So for scientists interested in a particular kinase inhibitor or a particular institute or another particular scientist, we should be able-and now we are beginning to be able-to feed back to them the conversations that are naturally happening by their global journal club that they can now tap into.This has been growing over the last couple of years.

We are also now beginning to enlist experts in bibliometrics and scientometrics to study how we could use these data to understand the direction and momentum of scientific ideas, of articles, of researchers, and start to see in a much more dynamic way, on a daily, weekly, or monthly basis.What is moving?How is it changing?How is it being used?And be able to feed that information back to people.

The other area of research for us has been around taking knowledge from information.As we look at the issues of complexity, interdependence, and volume of information mentioned earlier, we need new ways of understanding and visualizing that information.The concomitant rise of major open data sources recently burst in design, bringing design and computer science together to introduce this blossoming field of data visualization.This is an area that we have been very excited about for the last few years.

Six months ago we launched a new project called Visualizing.org, the first open platform for data visualization.3 Our intention is to connect data that are being made available by research organizations, universities, and all sorts of sources around the world, with the design community and then with media and the public, so we can close the loop.Visualizing allows designers to upload work, all under a Creative Commons non-commercial license, so that users interested in understanding how particular data sets might be visualized.Users can find out how particular issues like water, climate change, war, or bin Laden are visualized and could be better understood.They can find visualizations being produced by our community, grab them, use them, create derivative works, and really start to advance a new visual language for how to look at these complex issues.We have also created tools to allow users to search for data that we are bringing together from a whole variety of open data sources including the United Nations, the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), the Department of Health and Human Services, and about thirty-five universities now around the world, to engage their students and their faculties in using data and design to advance our understanding of complex issues in the world.

The big vision for us is to connect public data with designers and to bring that link together with blogs and social media, mainstream media and conferences.How can we bring these visualizations and ideas into the public?Of course, through social media.We should encourage more open data, we should push for more open data legislation, and we should require it.So we are trying to complete the loop as well and expose those projects and educate government in the positive consequences of their decisions.

I am going to end with this: how will we know that this new way of thinking about science literacy, that these new cultures of open science are really taking hold in society?A few years ago Walt Mossberg of the Wall Street Journal was asked when the Web will reach its full potential.He said, "When we start to talk about it like electricity, like energy.Where we don't talk about energy."(Admittedly this was a few years ago before we now all talk about energy.)But fundamentally, we do not talk about it, right?It is an invisible system.This is the same thing.We will stop talking about open access; we will stop talking about open data; we will stop talking about open science; because it will simply become the norm.It will simply become the way that science progresses.And we will start to tag things as being closed-this is a closed data source, this is a closed publication, this is a closed society-and not open being the novelty.

We solve society's problems with science not only as a tool, but also as a lens that we spoke about before.And this change will bring about nothing less than a twenty-first-century scientific renaissance.Science has this unique potential to improve the state of the world, but only if we integrate it into society, only if it becomes a source of decision making, if its knowledge is shared, if its architecture is open and we do not stand in opposition to it.I think that the information community in general has a great role and responsibility in taking this information that is so precious and helping ensure that we advance ideas, that we reach this moment of great change as we move digital, as we start thinking about new approaches, new systems, new infrastructure, that we have some common first principles.

### SELECTED QUESTIONS FROM THE AUDIENCE

At the conclusion of Bly's prepared remarks, the floor was opened for questions.Two audience members asked questions concerning peer review and its role in quality control for scholarly communication.One attendee noted that one of the important pieces of traditional peer review is that the editors of the journals choose the peer reviewers to make sure that the right person reviews the paper.With post-publication peer review, first, how do editors get the right people to look at it, and secondly, what is the danger of having bad science published?

I am not, Bly began his answer, advocating post-publication peer review, and I am not advocating that we disassemble pre-publication peer review at all.I think that peer review is absolutely critical, that it is actually one of the systems within science that is essential and must be protected.But it has been used as a crutch for preserving a business model and preserving a structure of science that gives far too much power to commercial publishers.We should take that process in a nearly identical fashion and move it out of the hands of industry.Peer review should not be done for commercial gain, and we should take a small percentage of the trillion dollars that the world spends on research and development every year and allocate it collectively to peer review.

The value of post-publication peer review is that it extends conversation.If we have a digital core, then we can link those post-publication conversations to the pre-publication conversations.We can have full transparency, and advance science that way.If someone misunderstands research it is ultimately the responsibility of that person's research community to provide further peer review, leading ultimately to further transparency into how things are being misinterpreted or misused.My belief is that science would progress faster and we could ultimately arrive at rejection of an idea and move forward if these research conversations were out of the hands of scientific publishers and in the hands of an open Web.

Another audience member reminded fellow attendees that even though we may believe that open science may generate a host of errors and may remove certain quality control systems that are in place, under current practices these problems are already happening.Also there have been several studies in the health sciences showing that when research results have been retracted by an author or by the peer review board, very seldom is that retraction actually communicated to the people who have cited the article.The article continues to be cited, multiplying the error.

Bly's answer was simply, I agree.Two other audience members asked questions related to Bly's stance on open science.One person asked how he might address critics of open science who are worried about the "bad guys" using research with ill intent.Another audience member expressed concern that universities see profitability and patentability from scientific research, and refuse to open up their research because of the revenue potential.

Bly gave a two-part answer to the first question: First, he said, information that a particular, private funder of research chooses to keep private is absolutely at their discretion.For example, I do not advocate that every pharmaceutical company should put all of their research out on the Web.To do so would be unrealistic, and ultimately not sustainable.The three pillars of whether this will work are if it is sustainable, scalable, and simple, and making everything public is not sustainable.It simply is not.We need industry to buy into this; it is very, very important.

The second part, though, is that we simply need the collective scientific literacy around the table of the people who are going to be interfacing with those kinds of issues so that they recognize the potential of a particular direction of research.So, if the scientists are the only ones who are regulating it, and we have had great success in history of scientists self-regulating before government could even begin to contemplate the consequences.But I would not leave this all to the scientists.It is also important to have better integration of science advisors into all areas of government, including now a science advisory infrastructure at the State Department, for example, that is far richer and far more robust than it has ever been before.So integrating science literacy, scientific thinking, throughout the parts of government provides an additional check and balance on things like that.

In response to the question about universities trying to protect potential revenue streams resulting from scientific research, the speaker said this: I have no real issues with intellectual property (IP) protection.There are issues that we need to address with the patent system in general, and there are parts of IP that need to be modernized, but I accept that that is a little bit of an orthogonal challenge.I do not see it as fundamental to this, or put it as a top core principle.So, if a university wants not to publish something because they see the potential of turning it into something that will generate an income stream, I cannot go to the level of providing them that income or replacing that income with this model.I can replace the income, I believe, the way publishing is working, but if a university or a company wants to patent something and turn it into a source of jobs and economic growth, I am fine with that.I think we need to make sure that the information that can be made available is available.And the part of it that I am most interested in is how to create vehicles for institutions, for profit and nonfor profit, to release their intellectual property back into the public domain with certain conditions attached to it that allow for the application of that technology to the developing world, to non-commercial purposes.So I have been working on a project called a Global Responsibility License that would allow companies and institutions to release their patent pools under specific IP provisions so that a research organization in a developing country of a certain Gross Domestic Product (GDP) could then use some of those patents for non-commercial purposes, and any advancement that they made on the patent, on the technology, would inure to the benefit of the patent-holder.Some huge companies have expressed great interest in this idea.And even if it just looks good for them to do it, frankly that is fine, too, because it is good for the world.
