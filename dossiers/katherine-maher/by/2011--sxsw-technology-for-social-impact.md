# SXSW festival takes on board use of technology for social impact

## Full text

I'm home in Washington, finding my voice and recovering from a mild case of SXSW Sars – that general state of disrepair following a week of debate and carousing at the massive South by Southwest (SXSW) festival held each March in Austin, Texas.

SXSW, which started as a scrappy little music festival back in 1987, is now a media juggernaut, with dedicated interactive, film and music tracks. The event lasts a "Texas week", and brings more than 35,000 media people to Austin every spring in search of local beer and the next big thing.

With development people moving rapidly into technology, the industry has been trying to catch up. Not long ago, SXSW's approach to ICT4D amounted to putting a mobile money, mobile health and mobile education person on a panel and calling it a success. Three years ago, at my first SXSW, I was the token representative of developing markets in a discussion on mobile banking. Back then, I counted perhaps 10 panels addressing any sort of technology for social impact. One of these was "Appfrica: How Web Applications Are Helping Emerging Markets Grow" – and although it included two of the founders of Ushahidi (then still a scrappy startup), the panelists seemed to spend most of their time convincing attendees that Africa is not a country.

These days, things are different. The Silicon Valley technology community will always include people focused on making money and building consumer products – that's how the industry works. But events in recent years have caused nerds and suits alike to sit up and take notice of the use of technology for social impact. Last year, the scale of devastation and suffering from the Haitian earthquake left people casting about for ways to help. When technologists jumped into action, they spun off CrisisCommons, Random Hacks of Kindness and Ushahidi Haiti. SXSW responded, featuring tracks on nonprofits, fundraising and technology, and the role of tech in humanitarian relief.

This year, the field grew again. Events in the Middle East reignited the simmering debate over how technology can be used for revolution, and fuelled discussion about its role in the context of human rights and democracy. The Middle East was the theme of no fewer than five events – from hardware hacking, to social media and journalism, to activist digital security (full disclosure, I was a panelist on one of these). All of these panels were in addition to the burgeoning track of panels on the more established aspects of ICT for development.

On ICTs and human rights, it seemed like almost everyone had a say – from social scientist to journalist to regional expert. Clay Shirky discussed the importance of technology in facilitating synchronised action, while researchers Jillian York, Rebecca Mackinnon and Danny O'Brien discussed the moral imperative of technology companies in the protection of human rights. Jennifer Preston and Brian Stelter of the New York Times led a discussion on technology and journalistic responsibility, while Habib Haddad, an entrepreneur in the region, emphasised the transition of tools from early adopters to the general public. And while Ayah Bdeir, an artist and hardware hacker from Lebanon, discussed the importance of the building of tangible objects as a means of fabricating identity, I was disappointed not to hear any Egyptian or Tunisian voices.

Meanwhile, in quieter ballrooms across town, it seemed the conversation on ICTs for development and humanitarian response was maturing. While there were many familiar faces, the generic panels of the past – overloaded with multiple representatives from one organisation – were harder to find. Some of the better discussions were concentrated on subsectors of ICT4D, including a panel on mobile phones and the future of health in Africa, with Josh Nesbit, founder of Medic Mobile, and Patricia Mechael of Columbia University, and one with Robert Kirkpatrick, the director of the high-profile UN Global Pulse initiative, joining Patrick Meier of Ushahidi and Chris Blow of Swift River, in a discussion on crowdsourcing and mapping in crisis environments.

There's a question as to whether it is fair to criticise the depth of ICT4D programming at what is still primarily a consumer technology conference. After all, the ICT4D field has its own speaking circuit, and the audience at SXSW is not one of development professionals. However, this is one of the few times of the year that the development tech community has the chance to get in front of some important stakeholders – the average engineer and product manager.

For some time now, enterprising development groups have worked to partner with big names in technology, with varying success. Often this approach is top-down, the result of a conversation on the margins of a crisis or an exclusive event. But most innovative technology companies pride themselves on being engineer-driven cultures, powered by the priorities of their top performers. If the ICT4D community wants to impact change at scale, why not start in Austin?
