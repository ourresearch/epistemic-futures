# From Blogs to Riches

## Full text

Clay Shirky's work on [power law distribution](http://www.shirky.com/writings/powerlaw_weblog.html) in blogs hits the mainstream with [this New York Magazine article](http://newyorkmetro.com/news/media/15967/index.html#) about how there's a glass ceiling in the blog world, and a sharp divide in potential revenue between the A-List and would-be aspirants to that status. In particular, the article suggests that old is gold, since long time blogs have the advantage in building up links and thus staying at the top of the heap. Dave Sifry offers some hope at the end of the article: "'You think the A-list is the A-list is the A-list,' says David Sifry, the CEO of Technorati. 'But I'm telling you, boy, does it shift--and does it shift fast.' Cultural winds can drive blogs in and out of favor."

That's certainly true. Looking at [Technorati's Top 100 list](http://www.technorati.com/pop/blogs/), there are quite a few blogs that weren't there a year ago, including our own Phil Torrone's [Make: blog]() at #72 after only about eight months. However, a recent presentation by Phil to our marketing staff, which might as well have been called "tech secrets of an alpha blogger," was an amazing rundown of the tools and techniques that Phil uses to keep on top of his subject area, which allowed him to make more than 3000 posts in those eight months. 

Rapid and repeated posting is one of the techniques called out in the article, which describes how top bloggers work from dawn to dusk keeping their site live. Given the sporadic posting here on Radar (#237 on the Technorati list), we must have something else going for us :-)

All in all, a nicely done look at some of the back story in the blog industry ... and one that plays to my own prejudices, perhaps, since I've always been a bit bemused by early blog advocates' claims of a new golden age of egalitarian publishing, where everyone had an equal chance. I smiled, because I remembered the same rhetoric on the early web -- which, after only ten years was dominated by multi-billion dollar media giants. New giants to be sure, rather than the old ones, but no longer a flat playing field.

I wrote about this back in 1995, in an essay called [Publishing Models for Internet Commerce](http://tim.oreilly.com/articles/pubmod.html). I just re-read it, and was pleased to see how well it has held up. Here's a selection:  

> Everyone's initial thought is that the net does away with the need for a distribution layer. After all, any site is accessible from anywhere else. This is clearly far from the case. First of all, net bandwidth isn't evenly distributed. Hosting of mirror sites and data warehousing are enormous opportunities. But perhaps more important than the physical distribution layer are the mechanisms for "attention distribution."   
> 
> 
> You can see this very clearly in the development of Web-based advertising. I believe that GNN was the first Web site to use advertising sponsors; in the two years since, we've seen the market go from incredulity ("You can't do that!") to arrogance ("Anyone can do that!") to a more mature realization that while anyone can put up a Web site, not every Web site will get an equal number of hits. 
> 
> When there were only a few hundred or even a few thousand sites on the Web, all one had to do was to set up shop and let the visitors come. With tens of thousands, and soon hundreds of thousands of sites, it becomes clear that setting up a Web site is something like setting up a shop on the streetcorner outside your factory. You may get some visits from passers-by, and because distance is no object, you can easily invite in your existing customers, but the rest of the world may never know you exist. Companies try to get "word of mouth" by creating more and more innovative or controversial come-ons, but that will only go so far. As the market matures, you'll see a topography emerge in which certain sites stabilize as the focus or starting point for a certain type of user.  
> 

Boy, did that prediction come true with a vengeance! Google, Yahoo!, myspace.com... How retro, though, to look back on a world where there were only tens of thousands of web sites, and the prediction of hundreds of thousands could be bold! (We started work on [GNN](http://www.littlechair.com/web/gnn/), the first online portal and the first ad-supported site on the net, in late 1992, when there were only a few hundred web sites.)
