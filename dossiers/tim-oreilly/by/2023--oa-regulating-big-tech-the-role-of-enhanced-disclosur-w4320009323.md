# Regulating Big Tech: the role of enhanced disclosures

## Full text

Attempts by governments to curb the market power of 'Big Tech' (Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, Meta Platforms, and Microsoft) are impeded by limited public information on their diversified digital platform ecosystems.Big Tech's annual 10-K financial reports disclose little about their globally dominant 'free' services, platform user numbers, and monetization practices, and suites of products.To support antitrust and regulatory oversight, we propose mandatory 10-K type disclosures covering Big Tech's: (i) internally used operating metrics (e.g.monthly active users), which underpin platform market share and monetization; (ii) ubiquitous 'free' products which escape traditional 'profit and loss' reporting; (iii) 'monetization' processes detailing how platforms make money from user data and attention; and (iv) product-by-product reporting through updating segment reporting rules.Disclosures should be mandatory for digital 'gatekeepers' and eventually integrated into reporting standards for all digital platforms.

# Introduction

Economic activity increasingly resides online.Around 12 per cent of global goods trade was conducted via international e-commerce platforms in 2015, according to McKinsey Global Institute (2016), while digital cross-border flows of data and information now far exceed trade and finance (ibid.).By late August 2022, technology shares accounted for 26.3 per cent of the total US market capitalization-more than energy, utilities, consumer staples, and industrials together. 2 Underpinning the global digital economy lie a handful of (largely) US-based internet platform companies, with user bases exceeding the most populous countries. 3Just Apple and Microsoft together account for 13.1 per cent of the S&P 500's market capitalization. 4The 'Big Tech' platform giants of Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, Meta Platforms, and Microsoft are today pivotal gatekeepers (Khan, 2017;Cabral et al., 2021) for firms and consumers wanting to participate in the global digital economy.Big Tech's considerable market power has so far escaped regulation (European Commission, 2022a).

A key reason for this, we argue, is the lack of publicly available information on Big Tech's individual products and their integrated product ecosystems (Strauss et al., 2021;Haugen, 2021a)-stemming in part from their annual 10-K financial reports to public shareholders and regulators being uninformative.These financial reports were designed for the industrial economy of in-person consumption and physical assets, even though much production and consumption today is done through remote digital services.Big Tech's platform business model is not captured by present disclosure requirements for publicly listed firms.Today's multi-sided digital platforms prioritize user growth to achieve network effects (Eisenmann et al., 2006).This leads to the selling of 'free' (or subsidized) goods and services to users, which are monetized indirectly (through advertising), or directly through subscriptions or sales-but only once sufficient market share has been achieved.All these indirectly or weakly monetized 'free' products are not subject to today's 'profit and loss' disclosures.Reporting requirements only cover the 'moneyed' side of the platform (e.g.advertising revenue)-and even then only in the aggregate rather than by product.There are no obligations to disclose the user numbers and monetization process that underpin a platform's financial prospects.In addition, even major products like Apple's App Store are not disclosed to shareholders since segment reporting requirements-which regulates product-by-product reporting-is subject to high managerial discretion.

As a result, vast knowledge gaps on the sources, uses, and extent of Big Tech's market power has arisen.This has left regulators reliant on whistle-blowers and protracted litigation to extract the information needed to understand which business practices and product markets to regulate.Given the large information asymmetries between regulators and digital platforms, effective oversight and enforcement of forthcoming European Commission (EC) regulations-the Digital Services Act and the Digital Markets Act-will be difficult.

To fill these gaps we advance mandatory public disclosures for digital platforms as an essential, yet overlooked, policy in the competition and regulatory toolkit.Such disclosure requirements should initially be required for all 'gatekeeper' digital platforms.But over time can quite easily be incorporated into existing national accounting and securities reporting frameworks, since our recommendations are designed with these frameworks in mind (Williams, 1998) and do not require reinventing the wheel (Hacker, 2017;Benoliel, 2021).

We construct case studies and use Compustat firm-level and segment data to show how present 10-K US disclosure requirements are intentionally exploited by Big Tech to entrench and expand their market power.The ability to conceal material information from the public allows Big Tech to hide profitable business opportunities from potential competitors and antitrust enforcers; extract rents from firms and users within their ecosystems; and prevent investors from allocating capital efficiently-as companies' true business prospects cannot properly be assessed.The paper's core findings are as follows.

• Big Tech's business models have all become increasingly diversified, providing integrated multiproduct offerings-or 'digital ecosystems'.This has driven more indirect monetization strategies, using user data or engagement in one product to increase the monetizable value of another product.• Free products make money for Big Tech yet are subject to no operating (or financial) disclosure requirements.Alphabet, for example, owns at least nine products-including Google Maps, Chrome, Android, and YouTube-each with more than one billion active monthly users and dominant global market shares, but with minimal disclosure requirements since these products are provided 'free' to the consumer.

• Present disclosure rules allow for Big Tech's product-level market power to be concealed.Segment reporting rules are supposed to ensure that large diversified companies release any '"hidden data" from consolidated financial information' (Flood, 2020), owing to the company operating many different product segments, each with different levels of profitability, risks, and growth potential.But in practice, segment reporting has failed to ensure Big Tech disclose anything on their major products.We provide several examples of this.To extend its lead in cloud computing, Amazon blindsided competitors by withholding the release of Amazon Web Service's (AWS) financials from its public 10-K report for longer than permitted by segment disclosure rules.Similarly, for years Alphabet declined to provide any financial disclosures for YouTube (or its other products) to the SEC and investing public, despite YouTube being the dominant video platform on the internet.Even today, YouTube's disclosures are minimal.Similarly, Apple relied on segment disclosure rules in its trial against Epic Games, claiming that the profit margin of its App Store did not exist, potentially withholding a key piece of evidence on its 'extraordinarily large' profit margins (Gonzalez Rogers, 2021).

We make five substantive disclosure recommendations for digital gatekeeper firms and their products.The aim is to ensure that all sides of a digital platform are subject to disclosure requirements:

(i) Detailed product-by-product financial and operating metric disclosures on any product segment with $5 billion or more in annual revenues or profits/losses.Mandatory reporting requirements for products which meet a clear, objective, monetary threshold ensure Big Tech cannot use subjective managerial discretion afforded to it by present (widely criticized) segment reporting rules to conceal major product lines (FASB, 2019).

# (ii)

Disclosures on 'gatekeeper' products that serve as major 'arteries of commerce' (Khan, 2017)-even if their direct financial earnings are relatively small.Mandatory reporting of operating metrics and financials for 'gatekeeper' products-those with entrenched and durable market positions and a minimum number of monthly active end users and business users.foot_0(iii)

Disclosure of detailed and consistent financials by segment.At present, wide discretion exists as to which financials companies disclose by product segment and how the financials are defined.Changes should draw on existing recommendations by leading accounting practitioners and investors (CFA, 2018).(iv)

External reporting of internally used operating metrics.The operating metrics used internally by managers to assess product and platform performance should be disclosed externally, by reportable segment and gatekeeper platform.If operating metrics are assessed by managers across geographical region-as they often are-then this should be included in the disclosure.(v)

A 'monetization narrative' to disclose how platforms make money from their users' attention and data.This should include details on direct and indirect monetization, whereby user data or engagement in one product is used to increase the monetizable value of another product within the ecosystem (or outside of it).Monetization disclosures should become more detailed with time, covering the characteristics of both users, their data, advertisers, and producers on the platform.

Our approach views a platform's 'non-financial' disclosures-its user operating metrics and monetization details-as closer to non-price financial disclosures.This extends the New Brandeis School's emphasis on non-price harms and sources of market power (Khan, 2016(Khan, , 2017) ) to multisided digital platform disclosures, with users on one-side and firms on the other co-creating value together through reinforcing network effects (Rochet and Tirole, 2006;Martens, 2016). 6This contrasts with the existing disclosure literature, which sees financial and non-financial disclosures as two sets of unrelated information (Farvaque et al., 2011).Damodaran (2021) argues that operating disclosures should be 'triggered' only if used by the company for its public narrative to shareholders.This, however, encourages fewer relevant public disclosures not more.Instead, external disclosures should be structured to reflect internal business reality, as we recommend.

Our emphasis though remains on the real economic harms which result from a lack of mandatory disclosures covering gatekeeper platforms.This contrasts with the privacy paradigm which predominates in much of the digital platform disclosure literature, with its emphasis on data ownership (Duch-Brown et al., 2017), personal rights, and user consent (Alexander et al., 2021;Zuboff, 2019).We draw on Mazzucato (2018) and O'Reilly (2017) in understanding disclosures and access to data as a governance mechanism that can help regulators and the public advance social goals-just as enhanced environmental, social, and governance (ESG) reporting helps society work towards these goals.Our approach to regulating Big Tech emphasizes the importance of platform disclosures empowering a wide range of constituents: including investors, competitors, employees, researchers, national regulators, and global governments.Disclosures should not just be for antitrust authorities or bureaucratic committees (Williams, 1998;Schweitzer and Welker, 2019).

We focus on the US 10-K disclosures framework because most of the largest digital platforms are US listed (UNCTAD, 2022).Our recommendations, however, can be applied across jurisdictions, since US Generally Accepted Accountancy Principles (GAAP) and International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS) accounting standards are very similar.Moreover, we recommend first targeting digital gatekeepers, which can be done through introducing new legislation, after which all publicly listed platforms can be included through updating existing public disclosure requirements.

The structure of our paper is as follows.Section II provides an overview of present disclosure requirements for digital platforms and resulting gaps.We discuss US SEC disclosure requirements and segment reporting rules, forthcoming platform regulations in the EU, and proposed platform regulations in the US.Section III uses Compustat segment data to detail the disclosures made by Big Tech on their growing ecosystems and compares this to their actual business evolution, driven by diversification and increasingly indirect monetization of users.Section IV uses case studies to detail harms to consumers, producers, and government oversight resulting from the present 10-K disclosures regime which allows Big Tech to conceal its market power and expand its platform dominance.Section V provides disclosure recommendations based on the preceding analysis.

Section VI concludes briefly.The online Appendices contains further details on segment reporting and Big Tech's actual disclosures and diversification.

# II. Disclosures and the digital platform business model

The section details the current state of digital platform disclosures in the US, EU, and China.We first explain the present regulatory disclosures framework covering the US Big Tech firms, which is the US 10-K annual report filed with the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) by all large publicly listed firms.This is based on US GAAP accounting but is similar to the IFRS-based filings requirements by firms listed in the EU.The key pieces of information which underpin digital platform operations and financial performance-namely their monthly active users and the free or subsidized products which drive platform adoption-face few reporting requirements.In addition, Big Tech's evolution into diversified entities, running multi-product digital ecosystems, remains poorly regulated by existing diversified 'segment disclosure' regulations.Next, we cover proposed digital platform legislation in the US (briefly) and the agreed upon legislation in the European Union (EU) (European Commission, 2022a,b).We focus on the disclosure requirements in the EU legislation, which are largely based on users and firms requesting data on a case-by-case basis, or the European Commission having privileged data access when requested and justified.China's regulatory model of digital platforms is even more heavily centralized.It relies on partial government ownership of technology companies and complete access to firms' source code.Even then effective regulation has been elusive.

# (i) Disclosure rules

The primary framework governing firms' disclosures are the listing requirements for public firms.These financial disclosures are required to be undertaken regularly (quarterly and annually) and usually include a qualitative description (or 'narrative') about the broader risks and business prospects facing the company.These disclosures are informed by that jurisdiction's accounting regulations-in the US GAAP accounting and IFRS in most other places.The US is home to the world's largest and most influential tech companies and is our focus, although the discussion which follows is broadly applicable to other jurisdictions and legislative contexts, including those emerging outside of securities law in the EU.

In the US, the SEC is the primary regulator overseeing disclosures made by publicly listed firms through their annual 10-K report (and quarterly 10-Q report).In theory, the 10-K and related filings in other countries should provide shareholders with an accurate and complete view of the financial performance of a publicly traded company, along with an assessment of the risks and prospects facing it.The 10-K's qualitative disclosures include business description (Item 1) and risks disclosed (Item 1A).While Part 2, Item 8 contains the consolidated and audited financial statement of the firm. 7In addition, unconsolidated (disaggregated) financials should be included in the 10-K (with very similar requirements for other jurisdictions, too).The requirement to disaggregate financials by operating segment is informed largely by accounting rules called 'segment reporting'-see online Appendices for further details.We show later that segment reporting rules-designed to ensure that disaggregated financial (and in theory non-financial) disclosures are provided by diversified conglomerates-no longer serve their stated purpose of releasing 'hidden data' from consolidated financial statements (Flood, 2020).This is a problem both with the rules of segment reporting (which require revising) and the lack of enforcement of existing rules-thereby rendering the provisions meaningless. 8

# Segment reporting

Segment reporting, for example, has its origins in the Senate antitrust hearings of 1965, when Professor Joel Dirlam proposed that because firms were becoming larger and more diversified, 'the relative profitability of different divisions and product lines' should be reported separately and that this information was of fundamental importance to investors and regulators (Pacter, 1993).This occurred in the context of the 1960s where, similar to today, there was a large increase in mergers and acquisitions activity in the US.This led to increasing corporate diversification as mergers were in unrelated industries.Internal diversification efforts were also taking place within firms.Starting in the mid-1960s, American companies began to voluntarily disclose separate sales revenues by major business line.By 1969 the SEC passed its own regulations to oversee these diversified disclosures.The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) passed its own line-of-business reporting requirements which ran from 1974 to 1977 and covered most of the 500 largest manufacturing firms (Hunt, 1975;FTC, 1977).Today, dissatisfaction with segment reporting rules is widespread among accounting practitioners and investors (CFA, 2018).This has led the Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB) in the US to explore changing them (FASB, 2019).Segment reporting should, in theory, compel disclosure of large products-those segments worth 10 per cent or more of combined segment profit/loss, revenue, or assets.

There are two specific shortcomings of segment reporting that severely hinder its ability to achieve its purpose of supporting investor and public oversight of diversified conglomerates.The first is that segment reporting disclosure requirements are triggered at a specific percentage of each company's financials.And all companies with annual revenues of $100 million or more are subject to the same segment reporting requirements.In today's market, where the largest companies have hundreds of billions of dollars in revenue, a single product or service line that represents only a small percentage of a company's total financials can still dwarf their competition and dominate global markets-but not require disclosure.For example, even if books were to represent only 1 per cent of Amazon's  (Lippman, 1934;Patterson, 2005).Congress ensured that the Securities and Exchange Act contained mandatory disclosures to force companies to disclose information that investors would find relevant to making investment decisions. 10For market-based disclosures, see Avedian et al. # (ii) Disclosures on digital platforms

Historically, the emphasis in public disclosures of US and global companies has been on price-based disclosures, in the form of financial measures of performance.Some correction to this one-sided emphasis is now warranted by the digital platform business model, whereby users, data, and their often complex monetization are not generally measured on their side of the platform in a pricebased unit of measurement.

In the context of digital platforms, non-price operating information-and in particular user numbers-uniquely underpins financial performance.This is because digital platforms are multisided, with users on the one side and firms on the other side, co-creating value (Rochet and Tirole, 2006;Martens, 2016).This co-creation of production underlies the platform's 'network effects', which makes it beneficial for any single individual (or firm) to be on the platform (Khan, 2017;Mazzucato, 2018).Mass adoption of the product becomes central to achieving the platform's network effects, often through providing the product for free or at a discount.As a result, the user may not be 'monetized' fully immediately, waiting first for mass adoption to occur.User data can also drive or improve the utility of the network indirectly.This includes better search results and product rankings, more accurate user reviews, better ads targeting, and an enhanced user experience.

# No disclosure requirements for users and operating metrics

Given that digital platforms derive their value from users far more so than in any in-person consumption model, investors increasingly buy and sell tech shares, such as Netflix or Pinterest, based on user growth figures, rather than earnings or revenue.foot_5Disclosure of operating metrics tends to happen sporadically by Big Tech, often in earnings calls, or is aggregated across time and products.Meta, for example, doesn't release user numbers for Instagram, WhatsApp, or Messenger separate from Facebook.

The major merger and acquisition story of 2022 turned on a dispute between Elon Musk and Twitter over a user operating metric ('monetizable daily active users', mDAU), highlighting the need for clear, consistent, and externally verifiable reporting on such metrics.Musk argued that if mDAU numbers were incorrectly inflated by Twitter then this was a 'company material adverse effect', since mDAU reflects the number of people who Twitter can show ads to.foot_6Despite the potential materiality of this, digital platform companies are given carte blanche by the SEC on the user numbers they choose to release and how they choose to define them.Twitter acknowledges that its definition of mDAU 'is not based on any standardized industry methodology'-but none exists for it to use.The lack of publicly agreed standards severely hampers Twitter's ability to defend itself against claims of false mDAU calculations from Musk. 16acebook, in particular, acknowledged early on to investors the centrality of user monetization to its business model.Its 2012 10-K report (the first after the firm's 18 May 2012 initial public offering (IPO)) reports monthly active users (MAUs) and daily active users (DAUs), noting clearly that monetization metrics are material to investors' ability to evaluate the company's future business prospects, stating:

The size of our user base and our users' engagement are critical to our success [...}.Our financial performance has been and will continue to be significantly determined by our success in adding, retaining, and engaging active users.

Subsequently, average revenue per user (ARPU) is also used by Facebook in its 10-K reports.

# Disclosures for only one side of a multi-sided platform

The above failing reflects the fact that only one side of multi-sided digital platforms-the moneyed side-is subject to disclosure requirements (Eisenmann et al., 2006).No regulatory oversight for user operating metrics exists in the US or elsewhere.This has direct financial implications for investors, suppliers, and others.Claims, for example, that Facebook distorted user numbers,foot_8 or that Twitter calculated user numbers incorrectly,foot_9 means that they may have overcharged advertisers significantly, misrepresenting their advertising impact, and also miscalculated their total company value.

# Absence of monetization disclosures

Monetization refers to the practice of converting user data, attention, or activity into sales revenue and profit.Due to a lack of non-financial disclosures required by digital platform firms, disclosures on free products are absent from current reporting requirements by global companies.Only aggregate ecosystem-wide monetization tends to be disclosed.For example, Alphabet reports its aggregate 'advertising' in its 10-K-but not product-by-product.

And it reports no details on the free products which underpin and allow for such monetization.For example, Google Search is free for consumers to use, but it is also the underlying traffic engine for Google Ads, where user attention is monetized.Despite this connection, few operating metrics, such as monthly active users, user engagement, or algorithmic details, are released on Search in Alphabet's 10-K report.

Monetization of users can be complex and indirect, arising from the data trail which users leave behind them as they go from activity to activity.This allows for their information and attention to be turned into a valuable asset and monetized across platform products (Birch et al., 2021;O'Reilly, 2021).Indirect monetization disclosure requirements do not exist either, despite it being a major risk facing investors if regulations were to prevent it from continuing. 19Only in 2021, on an earnings call, did Facebook disclose how it monetizes WhatsApp to drive ad sales on its other platforms, for example. 20Most users and regulators are still unaware that WhatsApp is indirectly monetized by Meta.

(iii) Digital disclosures regime in the European Union and the USA

The AICOA

The American Innovation and Choice Online Act (AICOA) is one of two key pieces of proposed legislation to govern digital platforms in the US-the other being the Open Markets Act (not dealt with here). 21The AICOA is a very short and focused text, aimed at preventing illegal discrimination by large 'covered platforms' through limiting self-preferencing, exclusion of competitors, use of 'insider' information, and 'tying' product placements to the purchasing of a platform's good or service.No disclosure requirements are built into the Act, but presumably these would follow from the 'agency enforcement guidelines' which would implement and interpret the Act, and which would include promoting transparency.

The European Union has passed two major laws governing digital platforms and 'gatekeepers': the Digital Markets Act (DMA) and the Digital Services Act (DSA) (European Commission, 2022a).

# The DMA

The focus of the EU's Digital Markets Act is on producer harms and it provides a list of permissible and impermissible business practices for digital 'gate keepers'-defined as entrenched and durable platform services that are large (defined by revenue or market capitalization), and with many end users (> 45 million) and business users (> 10,000).The DMA bans most self-preferencing 22 and use of privileged 'insider' information, opens up app-stores to competition, and requires messaging interoperability between platforms.Disclosures are a central feature of the DMA.It seeks to regulate bad behaviour ex ante, instead of having to rely on the courts to police anti-competitive behaviour ex post.Yet in practice, only continuous disclosures by digital platforms to the public and regulators can help ensure bad behaviour is stopped before it has become a pervasive feature of the platform.

Key disclosure requirements in the DMA include continuous and real-time access to data resulting from firms using the platform, which should be provided on request to business users.Such access is also provided to consumers to facilitate data portability.These disclosures are undertaken directly to the user on their request.Special provisions exist for advertisers and publishers, where gatekeepers must (European Commission, 2022a, p. 39):

provide advertisers and publishers to whom they supply online advertising services, when requested, with free of charge information that allows both sides to understand the price This, however, assumes that the internal company data are correct (e.g.how unique users are defined, etc.).Data on search and ranking results can also be provided on request to third party online search engines-though it is unclear what constitutes a search engine.

A major shortcoming of the DMA's approach to disclosures is that information on product and platform user numbers and their monetization will remain under the private control of companies and the European Commission.Without public disclosures on platform operating metrics (especially numbers of monthly active users) the European Commission (EC) runs the risk of being seen as arbitrary and partisan in who it labels as a gatekeeper.Instead, such information should be part of Big Tech's annual reporting to the EC, which the EC should in turn publish publicly.Moreover, the DMA's regulations as a whole may not be sustainable-and capable of renewal-without the public and the EC receiving regular disclosures from digital platform providers.At present the EC can request information from gatekeepers and investigate.But this is too weak to ensure the Act is hitting the moving target of digital platforms' evolving market power.

# The DSA

The EU's Digital Services Act is a complex and wide-ranging Act. 23It is more consumer-facing than the DMA and adopts a rights-based lens for its disclosures and regulations, focusing on several noneconomic societal harms which result from misinformation.Following Frances Haugen, the focus is on limiting user targeting, misinformation spreading, and the negative consequences of engagementdriven algorithms.Disclosures are prominent in the DSA but fall largely within this rights-based framework: they are a question of individual user choice (provided on request), which unduly limits their scope and impact.However, one compulsory and important disclosure is that the factors driving algorithms ('recommender' and 'ranking' systems) need to be shown to the user as a default.As with the DMA, though, without continuous managed access to how these systems are operating, it remains unclear how regulations can be properly policed and adjusted over time.Data disclosure in the DSA is undertaken primarily through digital platforms providing moderated and proportionate data access to researchers, including to explore big data questions.This, however, exists only within the scope of its 'risks' framework-and outlined further in Article 31 of the DSA on 'Data access and scrutiny'.The idea behind Article 31 is that vetted researchers or vetted non-profit organizations can ask for big datasets that help explore the 'systemic risks' laid out in the DSA.Risks are service specific and include technology design, business-model choices, illegal content, exercise of fundamental rights, and intentional manipulation of the service.Research can be published on these topics if it does not disclose any confidential data.

# (iv) Chinese digital regulations and oversight

Since late 2020, China has adopted several regulations to enhance oversight of digital platforms and achieve specific social outcomes. 24Addictive games, social media, streaming, and algorithms were initially targeted, with a 'youth mode' being required. 25In 2021 the Data Security Law and the Personal Information Protection Law were passed, regarded as China's equivalent to the EU General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).In addition, the Personal Information Privacy Law was passed (in effect 1 November 2021), 26 to regulate algorithmically-driven price discrimination, making it illegal. 27 The EU's draft Digital Services Act is extremely similar to this law in key respects-except perhaps in its public-facing disclosure obligations.China has also ordered the major tech companies to ensure interoperability of platforms: making them open up their platforms to each other, 28 and punishing them for false advertising-among other things. 29But can these regulations be properly policed?For example, the government has found that policing price discrimination and algorithmic behaviour is extremely difficult, even with access to the source code. 30

Public-facing disclosures are not important to China's monitoring of digital platforms.Moreover, they are in many ways inimical to its heavily censored, increasingly authoritarian approach to governance-under which the Chinese government must have a monopoly on information.

Governance of digital platforms is increasingly exerted through China's Communist Party and government structures, rather than through decentralized market mechanisms.Control is also exerted through ownership, with this serving a direct monitoring purpose.For example, the Cyberspace Administration of China (CAC) is the central internet regulator, censor, oversight, and control agency for the People's Republic of China. 31The CAC is also the majority owner of the China Internet Investment Fund, which has ownership stakes in technology firms such as ByteDance, Weibo Corporation, SenseTime, Kuaishou, and dozens of others. 32In several of these companies, the CAC has a 'golden share' arrangement, whereby its 1 per cent ownership stake enables the state investor to appoint a director to the board, ensuring insider access to important corporate decisions and the power to veto them.This approach has limitations though, and so far seems to have been unable to regulate Chinese digital platforms in an orderly manner. 33

# III. Ecosystems: diversification and monetization

The level of detail in which each segment is reported is itself as much a problem as the number of segments reported.Microsoft is among the biggest offenders here (Figure 3).It only releases 'revenue' and 'operating income after depreciation and amortization' (OIADP) by segment.This means that profitability (a proxy for market power) and capital investments and R&D (a proxy for future growth) by segment is concealed from investors, competitors, and regulators.

Monetization disclosures have not scaled either with Big Tech's increasingly diversified, ecosystemcentric, business models.Although there are no mandated monetization disclosures in 10-K reports for digital platform companies, technology companies frequently make such disclosures to shareholders-sporadically on earnings calls and in a highly aggregated manner in the 10-K (Birch et al., 2021;Strauss et al., 2021).These are rarely integrated systematically into their 10-K reporting though, where the legal liability is higher.Monetization disclosures would not require Big Tech to collect new data, especially if they were based on internally used product and platform key performance indicators (KPIs).User operating metrics are closely tracked in one form or another by all Big Tech companies (see online Appendices for details), since they are central to management's internal assessment of each division's business prospects and success (Katz and Shapiro, 1994;Falcon Doss, 2021;Murphy, 2021).Yet these are not disclosed in their 10-K reports, except in a highly aggregated manner.This keeps competitors, investors, the public, and antitrust regulators in the dark as to the growth prospects and changing market share (CFA, 2018).For example, the FTC in its revised filings on Facebook had to resort to using third-party estimates of user operating metrics for WhatsApp and Instagram.foot_15While Twitter, in its court case with Elon Musk, had no standard industry methodology of calculating monetizable monthly active users to fall back on in its defence.

# IV. Harms from the present disclosures regime

A lack of disclosures creates harms to consumers, competitors and competition, regulators, and the public at large.These are explored below with reference to several illustrative case studies: the Apple vs Epic Games (App Store) trial; Amazon hiding the financials of Amazon Web Services from competitors; and Alphabet's absence of disclosures for YouTube and its other products-despite requests from the SEC.

Harms from lax disclosures are indirect and often more subtle, resulting from information being hidden from the public for an extended period of time.They are no less serious though, as is evident from the collapse of Wirecard in Germany, the poor disclosure of off-balance-sheet items by banks during the 2008 global financial crisis (GFC), and the looming global environmental catastrophe which a lack of company disclosures has worsened.Such disclosure-based harms are rarely 'primary' in nature and instead prevent the underlying harm from being discovered in a timely manner, thereby limiting the ability of regulators, or competitive pressures, from being brought to bear on the concealed market behaviour in question.

In the examples we discuss, a lax disclosures regime facilitated Apple concealing above-normal profit margins and anti-competitive practices in its App Store.This saw higher prices for consumers and may have also harmed innovation in the ecosystem of apps in the App Store.With Amazon, concealing financials of its new product allowed it to effectively exclude competitors from a major new product market for years to come, concentrating reliance on a single firm for setting the direction and pace of innovation in the sector.With Alphabet, regulatory oversight of its major products was escaped through non-disclosure.This continues to have considerable implications for all stakeholders given the billions of users who use these products, the unclear terms on which profits are received by its content creators on YouTube or how its user data is monetized, how advertisers are forced to use its Ad Manager to display ads on YouTube (and potential restrictions on the way in which rivals serve ads on YouTube),foot_16 and how misinformation campaigns are managed.In all of these examples, antitrust activity was impeded by limiting regulators' ability to scrutinize product markets for possible abuses of market power.Investors are also prevented from allocating capital efficiently in this opaque business environment, as companies' true business prospects cannot properly be assessed.

# (i) Apple vs Epic Games: hiding market power, exerting gatekeeper control

Apple's court case with gaming company Epic Games illustrates the real economic value that hiding the App Store's segment information has for Apple.It helps Apple extract extraordinarily high profit margins-and high levels of total profits-from firms in its ecosystems through evading proper oversight from regulators.Epic Games sued Apple after its Fortnite game was removed from Apple's App Store in August 2020.This came after Epic Games built a direct payment system into Fortnite that would allow it to bypass the 30 per cent 'fee' that Apple takes on any in-app purchase in its App Store.With one billion iPhones in use, and because games are the largest source of App Store fees, a lot was at stake.Between January 2017 and October 2020, Epic is estimated to have paid $237 million in fees to Apple for Fortnite (Clayton, 2021).

The profit margin of the App Store was a key piece of evidence over which competing expert testimony was given in the trial.Although not by itself sufficient to show evidence of anticompetitive conduct (Gonzalez Rogers, 2021), in conjunction with other factors it can be a potent symbol of market power or of monopoly power.

As a result, 'segment reporting' became a key component of Apple's defence in court and served as a notable barrier to Epic Games and the court properly assessing Apple's market power through the App Store.This is because Apple's App Store financials are not broken out separately in its 10-K.As a result, Apple CEO Tim Cook could claim in the lawsuit that the App Store's true profit margin was unknowable because 'we don't have a separate profit and loss statement for the App Store' (Goldsmith, 2021).This defence was repeated by Apple's chief compliance officer (Lyons, 2021).Part of the argument was that product bundling made product-by-product financial reporting meaningless.'It's not a separate standalone business for us,' Kyle Andeer, Apple's chief compliance officer, said of the App Store at a congressional hearing in April 2021, and instead should be viewed in tandem with the products that it helps sell, often at low or no margin.This defence was repeated in the Epic Games court case where Apple proposed that the App Store is 'an integrated feature of our devices' (Gurman, 2021), that relies on all of Apple's intellectual property assets (Lyons, 2021), making it impossible to detail the App Store's precise profit margin (or allocate specific assets to it).

This argument was ultimately dismissed by the Judge, who accepted the opposing council's expert witness's profit margin estimates for the App Store.These profit margins were based on the financials Apple was forced to provide Epic's expert witness with and were accepted by the court as being 'extraordinarily high', exceeding 70 per cent (Gonzalez Rogers, 2021).This is higher than estimates of Google's Play Store profit margin at 62 per cent (Financial Times, 2021).Moreover, the judge ruled that, in light of the high profit margins, Apple's restrictions on iOS game distribution had 'increased prices for developers' and that a 'third-party store could likely provide game distribution at a lower commission and thereby either drive down prices or increase developer profits' (Gonzalez Rogers, 2021).

Despite the ruling, the App Store's financials-and operating metrics (such as user market share)are still not publicly available.They are hidden in Apple's aggregated 10-K reporting because of excessive managerial discretion afforded to it by current segment reporting rules.Meanwhile the absence of recognition of a special category of market power afforded to the App Store due to its 'gatekeeper' power (Khan, 2017) means that it is not subject to additional and separate disclosure obligations.

# Segment reporting has excessive managerial discretion

Segment reporting should, in theory, compel the disclosure of large products' financials-those segments worth 10 per cent or more of combined segment profit/loss, revenue, or assets.Private estimates show that the App Store already appears to count for at least 10 per cent of Apple's profits (and maybe also revenue).In fact, estimates show Apple may get as much as 20 per cent of its total profits from its App Store, similar to estimates of total profits that Alphabet gets from its Play Store (Peers, 2021).Yet in practice Apple is largely free to define segments worth disclosing as it sees fit.The rules state that a reportable segment must first internally operate as an independent segment with its own financials that are viewed regularly by the public entity's chief operating decision-maker to assess performance and allocate resources (Flood, 2020).Apple can simply deny that this takes place for its App Store-though it seems unlikely that a profit source of such magnitude would not be subject to independent financial oversight by the chief operating officer.

# Gatekeeper harms

The App Store's impact is far in excess of the direct revenue it generates for Apple, precisely because, as a gatekeeper platform, it 'serve[s] effectively as infrastructure for digital markets they are distribution channels, the arteries of commerce' (Khan, 2017, p. 326).Other firms rely on the App Store to do business in the digital economy.The App Store's role as an 'essential facility' that Epic was reliant on was one of the questions raised during the Apple vs Epic Games trial-see discussion in Gonzalez Rogers (2021, p. 158) of 'essential facility' doctrine. 38Given its narrow use in the US, the legal claim based on an essential facilities theory failed in court.But the economic facts, on the considerable commerce which the App Store facilitates (in a similar manner to other major arteries of commerce, such as a train), are difficult to ignore.

As of June 2020, Apple's App Store hosted almost 2 million apps and was visited by half a billion people each week across 175 countries (Apple, 2020).Apple argues that its App Store ecosystem facilitated $519 billion in billing and sales in 2019 (an amount similar to the GDP of Sweden)-or 10 times Apple's total services net sales that year.foot_18Apple phones, on which the App Store relies, are now used by more than half of all smartphone users in the US. 40It is hard to believe that such an important gatekeeping product does not warrant separate disclosure obligations.

(ii) Amazon Web Services: entrenching market dominance through hiding segment financials

Amazon hid from the public and its rivals the financials of Amazon Web Services (AWS), its cloud computing business, for longer than permitted by 10-K regulations.This helped entrench its advantage over competitors for several years.Since regulators did not have access to Amazon's disaggregated financials they had no way to check that Amazon's segment disclosures were in-line with regulations.However, it appears little effort was made by the US SEC to check for such violations and punish any possible violations ex post.

Within a single decade, AWS went from an internal project to providing the essential digital cloud compute, storage, and database infrastructure for most major global companies today.It is not an understatement to say that: '[AWS] was one of the greatest enablers of the post-recession technology boom-arguably more important than even the iPhone, though outsiders understood very little about it' (Stone, 2021, p. 96).

Amazon did everything it could not to draw competitors' attention to the new cloud service it had invented.This involved several strategies.First, Bezos decided to keep prices very low initially, even if it meant making a loss on the service.Amazon's loss leading strategy is well known (Khan, 2016).Yet from a disclosures perspective, loss leader pricing also helps to conceal a segment's 'true' (market determined) financial importance.By artificially suppressing profits and revenue, loss leader pricing delays the product meeting the 10 per cent profit or revenue thresholds required to be a reportable segment.

Second, Amazon adopted a specific segment reporting strategy for AWS to help it further build a 'moat' around AWS through its public concealment, protecting it from competitors for as long as possible (Stone, 2021)-perhaps even beyond the legal limits permitted by segment reporting.This may have been equally, if not more, important than its loss leading strategy.According to Stone (2021):

Both he [Bezos] and Jassy lobbied to conceal the division's financial details, even amid the widespread skepticism [sic] that throttled the company and its stock price in 2014.But in 2015, Amazon's finance department argued that the division's revenue was approaching 10 per cent of Amazon's overall sales and would eventually trigger [segment] reporting requirements under federal law.'I was not excited about breaking our financials out, because they contained useful competitive information,' Jassy admitted.

This strategy of hiding segment strength through financial aggregation proved remarkably effective for Amazon: 'Nice company.

[But] they make no money, Charlie!' quipped Steve Ballmer, Microsoft's then CEO, commenting on Amazon in October 2014.This reflected just how widespread and egregious the popular misunderstanding of Amazon's increasingly diversified business model had become.Amazon was intentionally perpetuating this myth.Notes Stone (2021):

Of course, Ballmer had little grasp of how Amazon's eventual engine of profitability, Amazon Web Services, was performing-and that was how Jeff Bezos wanted it.Over its first decade, AWS's revenues and profits were a closely guarded secret.The division generated $4.6 billion in sales in 2014 and was growing at a 50 per cent annual clip.But Amazon disguised those numbers, along with nascent advertising revenues, in a sundry 'other' category on its income statement, so that potential competitors like Microsoft and Google would not recognize how attractive a business cloud computing actually was.Observers and analysts could only guess at the financial dimensions of a unique enterprise computing business, anomalously tucked inside an online retailer.

In one sense, disclosures exist to reduce the surprises that investors and the public are exposed to.Yet AWS's public release as a standalone segment in Amazon's 10-K report hugely surprised competitors and investors.When Amazon announced to the world for the first time in April 2015 that it had a highly profitable, growing, and now fully formed 10-year-old business segment tucked away called Amazon Web Services, investors were shocked.Amazon's share price rose 15 per cent.

Competitors and regulators equally were caught flat footed.Technology analyst Ben Thompson sarcastically called Amazon's April 2015 earnings report 'one of the technology industry's biggest and most important IPOs'. 41Instead of segment reporting helping to reveal hidden information from Amazon's financials, it had done the exact opposite: it facilitated the decade-long gestation and silent global takeover of Amazon's AWS.

While Google Maps did not carry direct advertising until several years ago, it became an integral component of Google's wider monetization strategy, especially by allowing it to improve local search.As early as 2006, Maps began to provide relevant neighbourhood business listings, including actual maps and directions that would appear within the search results page.This was good for consumers, but also allowed Google's ad customers to target ads very precisely by geography, capitalizing on Google's knowledge of where you typically go in the world and places you seem most interested in, based on searches (D'Anastasio and Mehrotra, 2019).Maps became further integrated to improving Google's wider ads monetization with the launch of store sales measurement, released in beta in 2017.This product saw Google link users' offline purchases to their online ad-related activity, allowing it to infer if a digital ad viewed online by a user had led to an offline purchase (Liao, 2018a(Liao, , 2018b)).This feature was enabled by Google purchasing troves of credit card purchase data (initially from Mastercard) and integrating it with its own data on user activity.

# SEC tries to get further disclosures from YouTube

By 2010, 43 per cent of all videos viewed online by US households were on YouTube, with more than 14 billion videos viewed in May 2010 alone by 183 million users in the US, according to Comscore ( 2010).Yet it would take segment disclosure rules another decade to squeeze out of Alphabet any financial or operating metrics on this product segment-and even then Alphabet did not release YouTube as a standalone segment with its profits reported.During this time Google had free rein on how to regulate content on the YouTube platform, what intellectual property rights to assign to content providers' output, how to monetize the information of users who visits the platform, and much else.Todayfoot_20 YouTube shares ad sales with more than 2 million content creators; is expanding into TV with 700 million hours of YouTube watched on smart TVs in January 2022 alone; is becoming pivotal to the music industry and musicians-paying them $4 billion in revenue sharing from ads mostly;foot_21 and is the only US media service still operating in Ukraine during the war with Russia.

From a segment reporting perspective, all evidence indicates that YouTube has for years been treated internally as an independent operating segment-even though Alphabet has refused to disclose it as one externally in its 10-K report. 46Furthermore, YouTube offers a distinct advertising product from Google-video, as opposed to text and/ or image ads-yet for years Google has refused to release separate financials on YouTube, claiming that 'advertising is advertising' and that Alphabet's CEO does not review YouTube's financials for operating decisions.Alphabet was only compelled to eventually release some, albeit limited, financial information on YouTube in its 10-K report because it slipped up in its exchanges with the SEC (Owens, 2018(Owens, , 2020;;Strauss et al., 2021).

In July 2017 the SEC asked Alphabet to: 'Please clarify whether YouTube and Google Play represent or comprise [independent] operating segments that are [then] aggregated into the Google reportable segment.'On the Google Play Store, Alphabet responded that it was not a sufficiently material product to warrant disclosure.This is at odds with recent court filings, which indicate that the Play Store accounted for 20 per cent of Alphabet's reported operating income just 2 years later, in 2019 (Dave, 2020). 47  Regarding the YouTube segment, Alphabet argued to the SEC that its business model was all 'just advertising' revenue: 'Google is largely a single online advertising business, [...] we believe further disaggregation of [advertising] revenues by type of customer or sales channel would not enhance our disclosures.' 48Why then were some of YouTube's financials eventually released by Alphabet?This is because Alphabet thought it was being clever when it said that only Google CEO Sundar Pichai used YouTube financials for resource allocation, and not the company's chief decision-maker, Alphabet CEO Larry Page.This meant that YouTube would not meet the definition of an 'operating segment' requiring disclosure (Owens, 2020).But Pichai became CEO of Alphabet as a whole in 2019.As a result, by Q4 2019 Alphabet was compelled to at least release revenue separately for YouTube ads, Search, and Cloud.

In Q4 2020 Alphabet changed into a two-segment company, with 'Google Services', 'Google Cloud', and 'Other Bets', but little in the way of additional disclosures followed.YouTube subscription revenue was not separately disclosed, nor the payments it makes to content creators who share in YouTube's ad revenue-required to calculate YouTube's profit contribution to Alphabet.This situation persists to the present day.

Alphabet's 2020 10-K report notes that YouTube had almost $20 billion in annual ad revenue alone.This means that if YouTube were a standalone ads-only entity, it might be roughly the world's fourth-largest seller of digital ads, after its parent company, Alphabet, Facebook, and Amazon (Pressman, 2021).In the final analysis, despite considerable SEC pressure, the total revenue and profitability of YouTube is still not made public by Alphabet, nor how users are monetized, nor the data needed to assess the impact the product has on society.Only in July 2022 did YouTube decide of its own accord, and on its own terms, to provide limited data releases from YouTube through an API to an external researcher.foot_22

# V. Regulating Big Tech through enhanced oversight: the role of disclosures

Instead of overhauling accounting conventions, or mandating disclosure requirements for all publicly listed firms, we initially recommend targeted disclosure requirements applicable only to 'gatekeeper' platforms, adopting the same gatekeeper definition as the EU's Digital Markets Act.This approach can be applied in almost all jurisdictions globally, either through introducing new legislation or updating existing disclosure requirements for a specific category of publicly listed firms.This has the benefit of ease of implementation and effectively first trials the proposed disclosure requirements below using a subset of firms.

Our disclosure recommendations for gatekeepers are applicable to a broad range of geographical jurisdictions, since they simply expand upon widely used accounting and disclosure conventions for publicly listed firms.The template used is that of the 10-K in the US, since this is where Big Tech are listed.This 10-K template is, however, by no means alien to foreign jurisdictions, who all have similar disclosure requirements to the 10-K in place for publicly listed firms.As a result, the recommendations below can be adopted or integrated into existing national legislation quite easily.foot_23

A key challenge in regulating Big Tech, and which disclosure requirements must grapple with, is that-unlike the public utility railroads of old-Big Tech are global entities.Their remotely consumed digital services are intangible and easily produced and consumed in different parts of the world.Moreover, apart from Amazon, all of Big Tech earn the majority of their revenues from abroad.foot_24(WhatsApp is arguably the closest thing to a globally used digital public utility, with strong network effects and a generic nature to it.)In the absence of globally agreed and enforced disclosure and monitoring requirements for digital platforms, national and regional regulations must fill the void.National regulations can, however, have global effects.They foster and inspire imitation and adoption in other jurisdictions.And even if the disclosure requirements are not technically extraterritorial in nature-in the way that the EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) isdisclosures can require Big Tech to disaggregate their information on a geographical basis-if this is a relevant division used internally by management.As a result of this, disclosures in one jurisdiction can have considerable global spillovers, facilitating regulations in other parts of the world through putting globally relevant information into the public sphere.

Our disclosure recommendations, including our focus on non-price disclosures, are in many respects analogous to the approach taken by the Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) reporting movement, whereby improved disclosures help define and advance more desirable economic outcomes.With climate change, for example, the 'E' of ESG is meant to help company disclosures align with sustainable production pathways.Similarly, if we want digital markets to foster competition between multiple firms, and consumers not to have their data monetized in problematic and opaque ways, that means making sure the required disclosures by platform companies help steer digital markets in that same direction, too.

Our core recommendations cover the following disclosure requirements: (i) internally used operating metric disclosures (such as monthly active business users and end users), which underpin a platform's market share and monetization potential; (ii) disclosures covering globally dominant 'free' products (e.g.WhatsApp, YouTube, Google Maps) which escape traditional 'profit and loss' reporting, but with millions of monthly active business users and end users, and dominant market shares; (iii) how the platform 'monetizes' user attention and data: how platforms make money from their users should be disclosed in the form of a 'monetization narrative'-comparable to the other narratives that public firms are required to disclose, such as on business risks more generally.This would bring data disclosures to the fore, since it would require explaining how data are monetized within the platform's business model; and (iv) financial and non-financial disclosures disaggregated at the product level, through updating segment reporting rules to remove its discretionary basis and ensure it covers all large products regardless of its relative financial contribution to the firm in question.Disclosures should be mandatory for all major 'gatekeeper' platforms and over time integrated into reporting standards for all publicly listed firms-through the 10-K in the US and IFRSbased standards in the EU.

Another way of looking at these disclosure requirements is that all sides of the digital platform should be disclosed (e.g. the free side which users engage with and the monetized 'moneyed' side); and all major platforms should be subject to them:foot_25

• To cover the financial, price side(s), of the digital platform, we recommend giving segment reporting some 'teeth', by ensuring it is mandatory (rather than up to management's judgement), scales with firm size, and cannot ignore large product categories.Any line of business with $5 billion or more in direct or indirect revenue or contribution to profit should be broken out for investors in granular financial detail-in line with several recommendations made by investors and the accounting profession (CFA, 2018).Until now, companies have relied on 'management discretion' rules that say they can report results the way their top decision-makers see them.This gives Apple, for example, the flexibility to report its results by geography rather than by product line; and allows it to claim ignorance over the App Store's profit margin.• At the non-price side(s) of the platform, we recommend disclosure of operating metrics (such as monthly active users) used by the firm to measure platform performance.Regulators should recognize that operational metrics are as important to digital production and its assessment, as labour costs and labour productivity metrics are to physical production.Operating metrics drive a platform's financial performance, helping the public and regulators assess its future business prospects and present market shares.Operating metrics should eventually detail who is using the product-not just in which geography, but which firms, suppliers, and users.This is hardly a novel requirement, with ASC 280 already requiring public entities to disclose who their major customers are (Flood, 2020).Such characteristics can help assess the economic and social impacts and reliances which the company has.• At the intersection of the price and non-price sides of the platform, we recommend a monetization narrative in Part 1 of the 10-K, to explain how value is created on the platform as a whole, including across products but within the same ecosystem.How users are directly and indirectly monetized in each product should be detailed; how users in one product are used to increase monetization in another ecosystem product should also be detailed; and, finally, how ecosystem products and users are used to increase monetization for products outside of the firm's digital ecosystem should also be detailed.These ecosystem disclosures would bring data disclosures to the fore and explain how data are used within the firm's business model.• We recommend specific gatekeeper disclosure obligations to ensure major platforms have reporting safeguards in place.Platforms and products which meet clearly defined end user and firm user thresholds should be subject to gatekeeper disclosure obligations regardless of their direct financial size, in line with Strauss et al. (2021) andEuropean Commission (2022a).This draws on the gatekeeper power regulated by the Digital Markets Act (ibid.).Any company whose platform controls access to a globally significant multi-sided market or platform-as Alphabet does with Google Search and YouTube, as Apple and Alphabet do with their app stores, and as Amazon does with its marketplace-should be required to break out that business as a separate operating segment.That means disclosing detailed information on revenue, profit and loss, capital investment and R&D expense, costs incurred, as well as the key operating metrics these firms use to determine their products' development and future potential.

We have two other recommendations that provide guidance on what exactly should be disclosed:

(i) External reporting of internally used operating metrics.The operating metrics used internally by managers to assess product and platform performance should be disclosed externally, by reportable segment and gatekeeper platform.If operating metrics are assessed by managers across geographical regions-as they often are-then this should be included in the disclosure.(ii)

Disclosure of detailed and consistent financials by segment.At present, wide discretion exists as to which financials companies disclose by product segment and how the financials are defined.Reforms should draw on existing recommendations by leading accounting practitioners and investors (CFA, 2018).

Fortunately, there is precedent in motivating for, and making progress with, the above industryspecific disclosure recommendations.For one, there are the special reporting rules for the airline industry, which reflects their (quite literal) gatekeeping function in governing access to airport gates along their enormous investments in 'mobile' rather than fixed assets.foot_26Second, there are efforts under way at the SEC to come up with standardized disclosures for ESG reporting to advance public governance over companies.foot_27Third, the accounting profession is seeking changes to segment reporting rules, with the FASB acknowledging widespread dissatisfaction with it from practitioners and investors (FASB, 2019).

One possibility is that our disclosure recommendations be incorporated directly within an ESG reporting framework, either by extending governance disclosures to digital markets in a clear manner, or by adding a 'D' for digital-with a focus on the governance implications for society and competition authorities from enhanced digital disclosures by Big Tech ('DESG').'G' (governance) in many popular ESG reporting standards already uses 'materiality' as one basis to determine what the firm should disclose, yet this often ignores how material digital disclosures have become to investors, consumers, and society at large-since no separate and specific acknowledgment of these digital issues exists. 55ESG scores often already assess a company's ability to identify sources of longterm value creation, develop long-term metrics to assess them, and transparently report these items publicly.For digital markets and platforms this requires disclosure of the operating metrics that we have discussed above, including on monetization, data, operating metrics used internally, and financial disclosures disaggregated by product. 56Incorporating digital governance disclosures into ESG could have enormous benefits, especially since in the US even 'material' disclosures do not have to be disclosed by public firms unless a specific requirement to do so exists. 57

# VI. Conclusion

This paper aims to show why enhanced disclosures are an essential, yet overlooked, policy in the regulatory toolkit needed to govern the Big Tech digital platforms and help rein in the anticompetitive uses of their market power.Proper disclosures help highlight to the public, investors, competitors, and regulators where and how value is being created, and where it is being extracted through the exercise of monopoly rents.Without disclosure requirements, governance relies on whistleblowers, voluntary disclosure, and protracted litigation, which covers harms usually long after their initial occurrence.

The case studies detailed in this paper highlight a failure by Big Tech to disclose one or more sides of their digital platforms externally: the (free or subsidized) user side, the moneyed side, or their digital ecosystems as a whole.Our disclosure recommendations aim to ensure that financial and operating metrics of each of these sides of their platforms-and as a complete ecosystem-are no longer hidden from public view.The recommendations cover both financial and non-financial operating disclosures on a product-by-product basis.They include a 'monetization' disclosure in the form of a detailed narrative on how platforms monetize their users (inclusive of their data and attention).Our disclosure recommendations can easily be applied in jurisdictions across the world since they build on existing accounting conventions and can initially be applied only to companies identified as 'gatekeepers'-drawing on the definition from the EU's Digital Markets Act.

Rather than design disclosures to prevent specific types of economic and social harms by Big Tech, our focus is on a broad set of public disclosures that work largely within existing accounting guidelines.Our goal is not just to empower regulators, but also policy-makers, researchers, public investors, and competitors. 58 Our recommendations are by no means comprehensive.In particular, further analysis is needed on algorithmic disclosures.This follows from the growing use of algorithmic rents (Fortson, 2020) by digital platforms to transfer profits and welfare away from consumer and producers and towards themselves, often through various forms of self-dealing (European Commission, 2022b).Data disclosures have also not been dealt with here in a substantive manner.In general, we have not addressed in detail issues which may require major changes to accounting conventions, including those surrounding the recognition of intangible assets and expenditures on them, such as data and human capital.Instead, we have tried to incorporate these vital disclosures within existing approaches and conventions.

# (iii) Reporting only one side of multi-sided platforms: Alphabet's absence of operating and monetization disclosures

Free products make hundreds of billions of dollars annually for Alphabet, yet are subject to no operating or monetization disclosures-except in detailing direct aggregate advertising revenues from them.Alphabet owns at least nine products-including Google Maps, Chrome, Android, and YouTube-each with more than one billion active monthly users and dominant global market shares, but with minimal 10-K disclosure requirements since these products are provided 'free' to the consumer.

# Monetization

Big Tech companies have built their platforms into digital ecosystems.User activity and data in one product increase the monetizable value of another product within the ecosystem.A good example of this is Google Maps, which is provided for free to consumers yet has considerable monetary value to Alphabet, both indirectly and directly.No public disclosures for the product are required for Google Maps, though, until it crosses a major (10 per cent) financial threshold from being directly monetized by Alphabet.
