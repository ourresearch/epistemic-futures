# The True Inventor of the Unconference

## Full text

Sara Winge is the person behind [Foo Camp](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foo_Camp>), O'Reilly Media's pioneering "unconference," which we launched in 2003, and which has spawned hundreds of related events known and loved by passionate geeks worldwide, from [Bar Camp ](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BarCamp>)to [City Camp](<http://citycamp.com>) to [ORD Camp](<https://ordcamp.com>). We invite a couple of hundred really interesting people for an event without a program, which emphasizes interaction between the participants rather than on prepared presentations. The event has been phenomenally successful in convening groups to explore emerging ideas. In addition to the original Foo Camp focused on computer science, we run one in partnership with Nature and Google on the natural sciences, and have recently launched one with Sage Publishing and Facebook to focus on the social sciences. We do a similar convening for understanding the changes needed for education in the 21st century and another for bringing together economists and activists working to find our way to "[the Next Economy](<http://www.oreilly.com/nexteconomy/newsletter.html>)."

Sara based the design of Foo Camp in part on the ["Open Space" work of Harrison Owen from 1985](<http://www.openspaceworld.com/brief_history.htm>), who is widely credited with developing the concept. However, Sara just discovered that [Alexander von Humboldt](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_von_Humboldt>), one of the world's greatest scientists, pioneered the idea nearly 200 years ago, in 1828! Humboldt was one of the most widely traveled and accomplished scientific explorers, an inspiration to Charles Darwin and many others, and is considered to be the last person able to have mastered all of the science known in his day. He was [a pioneer in the creation of infographics](<http://www.mappingthenation.com/blog/alexander-von-humboldt-master-of-infographics/>) such as the one shown at the top of this piece. And according to Andrea Wulf, the author of [The Invention of Nature: Alexander von Humboldt's New World](<http://www.andreawulf.com/about-the-invention-of-nature.html>), he is the person who first articulated a vision of nature as an interconnected whole. It turns out that he also invented the unconference.

Here's the tidbit Sara found in Andrea Wulf's book:

[](<http://www.amazon.com/Invention-Nature-Alexander-Humboldts-World/dp/0345806298/>)

"Humboldt was revolutionizing the sciences. In September 1828 he invited hundreds of scientists from across Germany and Europe to attend a conference in Berlin. Unlike previous such meetings at which scientists had endlessly presented papers about their own work, Humboldt put together a very different programme. Rather than being talked at, he wanted the scientists to talk with each other. There were convivial meals and social outings such as concerts and excursions to the royal menagerie on the Pfaueninsel in Potsdam. Meetings were held among botanical, zoological and fossil collections as well as at the university and the botanical garden. Humboldt encouraged scientists to gather in small groups and across disciplines. He connected the visiting scientists on a more personal level, ensuring that they forged friendships that would foster close networks. He envisaged an interdisciplinary brotherhood of scientists who would exchange and share knowledge. ‘Without a diversity of opinion, the discovery of truth is impossible,’ he reminded them in his opening speech.

"Around 500 scientists attended the conference. It was an ‘eruption of nomadic naturalists’, Humboldt wrote to his friend Arago in Paris. Visitors arrived from Cambridge, Zurich, Florence and as far away as Russia. From Sweden, for instance, came Jöns Jacob Berzelius, one of the founders of modern chemistry, and from England several scientists including Humboldt’s old acquaintance Charles Babbage. The brilliant mathematician Carl Friedrich Gauss, who came from Göttingen and stayed for three weeks in Humboldt’s apartment, thought the congress was like pure ‘oxygen’."

History is a lovely thing. As is the hunt for knowledge!
