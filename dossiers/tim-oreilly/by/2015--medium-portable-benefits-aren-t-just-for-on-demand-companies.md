# Portable Benefits Aren’t Just for On-Demand Companies

## Full text

**Portable Benefits Aren’t Just for On-Demand Companies**

Something that needs to be said: portable benefits aren’t just for on-demand companies! While it only became clear to policy makers how out of step our current labor classifications are with how people work today when the on-demand economy offered up a whole new class of workers who didn’t fit neatly into the traditional distinctions between employees and independent contractors, portable benefits have actually been needed for a long time.

In my research into labor issues in the On-Demand economy in preparation for my [Next:Economy Summit](https://conferences.oreilly.com/next-economy) later this week, I discovered an appalling loophole in traditional W2 employment benefits, which I wrote about in [Workers in a World of Continuous Partial Employment](https://medium.com/the-wtf-economy/workers-in-a-world-of-continuous-partial-employment-4d7b53f18f96): traditional low-wage employers use algorithmic scheduling to make sure that their workers never get above 29 hours per week, so that they aren’t eligible for health benefits and other perks available to full-time employees. Erasing the distinction between full and part time workers, and having companies contribute to all benefits in proportion to hours worked would make this loophole no longer so attractive to companies, perhaps letting these workers go full time with a single employer. And even if it didn’t have that effect, those workers would at least be able to get fractional benefits from multiple employers, adding up to the equivalent of full time. (Of course, this also means solving the scheduling problem, so that workers have enough visibility into and control over their schedule that working for multiple employers becomes possible.)

But in addition, I learned from Brad Smith, the CEO of Intuit, one of our speakers at the Summit, that their data shows that 40% of their self-employed customers also have income from a W2 job. So this problem of multiple employers with different tax and benefit regimes far predates the on-demand economy. Solving it would mean that all workers are treated more fairly.
