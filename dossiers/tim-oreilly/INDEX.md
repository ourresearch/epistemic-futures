## Quick bio

Tim O'Reilly (b. 6 June 1954, County Cork, Ireland) is the founder and CEO of O'Reilly Media, the technical publisher and learning-platform company he started in 1983 as O'Reilly & Associates. He convened the 1998 "Freeware Summit" at which the term *open source* was adopted, coined and popularized *Web 2.0* through the 2004 Web 2.0 Conference and his 2005 essay "What Is Web 2.0", and later framed "government as a platform," the Maker movement, and "the WTF economy." He is a partner in the early-stage venture firm O'Reilly AlphaTech Ventures (OATV) and has served on the boards of Code for America, PeerJ, Civis Analytics and PopVox. His book *WTF? What's the Future and Why It's Up to Us* was published by Harper Business in 2017.

Since 2024 his primary current work is the **AI Disclosures Project** at the Social Science Research Council, which he founded and co-directs with the economist Ilan Strauss; the project publishes working papers and policy briefs on AI market disclosure, training-data provenance and copyright, attribution in LLM search, and open protocols (MCP), and runs the Substack **Asimov's Addendum**. He describes his current focus as "the impact of algorithms on society, governance of AI platforms, and the intersection of technology and economic policy." He also writes on O'Reilly Radar and, since 2026, on a personal Substack, *Conversations with AI*.

**Mechanics that made this work — worth reusing:**

1. **Use `https://web.archive.org`, not `http://`.** This is what actually broke the previous pass.
   Port 80 to web.archive.org was intermittently refused from this host — curl exit 7,
   `Failed to connect … after 48 ms`, which reads exactly like rate-limiting and provokes a useless
   exponential backoff. Over HTTPS the *same* CDX queries and replay URLs answered in ~0.5 s at a
   ~100% success rate. Wayback was never the bottleneck; the scheme was.
2. **Round-robin retries, not per-URL retry loops.** One attempt per URL per round with `-m 45`,
   failures requeued for the next round. A per-URL loop with a long timeout stalls the whole queue
   for ~20 minutes on a single bad URL.
3. *Snapshot enumeration.* `https://web.archive.org/cdx/search/cdx?url=radar.oreilly.com/tim/&output=json&fl=timestamp,original,statuscode&filter=statuscode:200`
   → one timestamp per year-month (164) → fetch `…/web/<ts>id_/<original>` → drop
   `div.sidebar, #right, #footer, nav, .widget, .related` → collect `a[href]` matching
   `radar\.oreilly\.com(/archives)?/(\d{4})/(\d{2})/<slug>\.html`.
4. *Post-text retrieval — the URL-form trap.* `web/2013/<url>` 404s for most legacy posts. The
   Movable Type era used **underscore** slugs under `/archives/`; the WordPress era used **hyphen**
   slugs without `/archives/`; Wayback holds each form only for the years it was live. The working
   recipe is a per-month CDX **prefix** query in the form that era used
   (2005–2008 → `/archives/<Y>/<M>/`, 2009–2015 → `/<Y>/<M>/`), key the results on the slug with
   `[^a-z0-9]` stripped so the two forms join, then fetch each post at its **own** timestamp.
   135 month-maps were built this way.
5. **The author archive is contaminated — always verify the byline on the post page itself.** About
   19% of the URLs harvested from `radar.oreilly.com/tim/` are other people's posts (they leak in via
   recent/related blocks in the later templates). Every radar template encodes the author in an
   avatar filename, `photo_<slug>_s.jpg`, which is the most reliable signal across all four
   templates; the visible "by <Name>" line is the cross-check. **No item was written to `by/` without
   an affirmative Tim byline on the archived page** — the `archived_byline` column records what was
   found for every row, including the rejected ones.
6. The legacy site went through **four** page templates 2005–2015 (Movable Type `div.content` +
   `h3#aNNNN` + `span.author`; a 2007–2008 `div.post_block > div.right > div.content`; a 2009–2012
   `h1#title`/`h2#title` + `div.entry-body`; and a 2013–2015 WordPress `div.entry-content`). An
   extractor that handles only one of them silently produces zero-length bodies for the rest.

### Cross-post de-duplication (deliberate — do not "restore" these)

O'Reilly cross-posts heavily: the same essay routinely appears on Medium, O'Reilly Radar, LinkedIn,
and sometimes a working-paper series and a journal on top of that. Where two files carried the same
normalised title, **one copy was kept (the longer extraction) and the other deleted**, so the corpus
holds one text per work rather than three. Roughly 50 files were removed this way.

Exceptions kept deliberately, as genuinely distinct publications rather than cross-posts:
the Claremont Report (SIGMOD Record 2008 and CACM 2009); "Algorithmic Attention Rents" (UCL IIPP
working paper 2023 and the Data & Policy article 2024); and "MCP in Practice", where the SSRC working
paper and the O'Reilly Radar post about it are separate pieces — the Radar one is retitled
"MCP in Practice (O'Reilly Radar post)" so the two no longer collide.

Also merged: OpenAlex carries two records (`W112243358` and `W7166945360`, the latter titled
"Reading 2.0") for a single 2006 Deep Blue deposit at `hdl.handle.net/2027.42/57301`. One copy is
kept and the note records the merge. After this pass **no two items share a `source_url`.**

`radar-post-index.tsv` and `asimov-addendum-post-index.tsv` both carry a `file` column pointing at
wherever each post's text actually ended up, so a de-duplicated post is still traceable to the file
that holds it.

**Note for anyone re-running collection:** if parallel workers are used, they must be told that
de-duplication is the lead's job and is intentional. In this pass a subagent was instructed to "skip
nothing on dedup grounds", correctly followed that instruction, and repeatedly re-created files the
lead had removed — seven files bounced three times before the lead's rule was allowed to win. Give
per-surface workers a "write only, never restore" rule, and dedupe once at the end.
