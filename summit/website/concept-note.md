# Concept Note

*Source: https://epistemicfuturessummit.pubpub.org/pub/w1aicj1h (release 1) — the page linked from the home page as "Read the Workshop Rationale". Captured 2026-08-15, verbatim.*

The Epistemic Futures Summit is an invitation-only workshop bringing together scholars, technologists, publishers, and open-knowledge leaders to rethink how human knowledge can be sustained, curated, and trusted in the age of AI. At a time when traditional epistemic institutions are strained and AI systems are reshaping how knowledge and beliefs are produced and processed, we will map the emerging knowledge ecology and develop practical strategies to support its resilience and integrity. The Summit is also intended to be a design opportunity: a rare chance to bring the right people together to invent the mechanisms, standards, and institutions that the next era of knowledge will need.

## What was broken, what was breaking, and what threatens to break?

Even before the advent of generative AI, the institutions we rely on to produce, vet, and preserve knowledge were already under serious strain. Some of these pressures are more than half a century old. Scholars have been warning about "too much to read" since the 1960s, as the sheer volume of published literature outgrew anyone's capacity to keep up. The "Serials Crisis," the relentless rise in journal subscription costs, has dominated library budgets for more than thirty years, and the open-access movement it spawned is still fighting over who gets to read what and who pays. Meanwhile, the incentives that shape scholarly life ("publish or perish," impact factors, citation counts) have steadily rewarded volume and visibility, sometimes at the expense of rigor.

The web at the start of the century added even more pressure. Search engines and social media inserted themselves between knowledge producers and the public, displacing traditional gatekeepers; the collapse of print advertising gutted the business models that once sustained newspapers and publishers; and the rise of self-publishing meant anyone could now reach an audience without an institution's blessing. Within scholarship, the spread of preprints offered a deliberate alternative to the traditional model: rather than filter, then publish, why not publish, then filter? Share findings quickly and openly, and let evaluation happen in the open afterwards.

At the same time, trouble was emerging from within the formally vetted literature itself. The 2010s brought a "replication crisis" in the sciences (the growing realization that many published, peer-reviewed findings could not be reproduced), alongside a steady stream of retractions, data fabrication, and questionable research practices. Tellingly, these failures occurred largely inside the traditional system of peer review and established journals, not at its preprint margins. They were a reminder that formal gatekeeping has never been a guarantee of reliability. And they did measurable damage to public trust, just as waves of online misinformation and the growing politicization of expertise were doing the same. Together, these pressures have eroded confidence to the point where some of these challenges look not merely difficult but existential.

In short, the knowledge system was already stressed and fragile when generative AI arrived. The Epistemic Futures Summit starts with the understanding that AI didn't create these problems. But it is now a powerful new force acting on an already-cracking system. It could make the problems worse, but it could also help us fix them. Indeed, several of these long-standing dysfunctions have proven stubbornly resistant to incremental reform; the disruption AI brings may be precisely the opening we need to redesign systems that were overdue for reinvention. This Summit treats that opening as an invitation to build.

## Why is it so hard to think clearly about AI?

It helps to remember where we started. Many of us were once genuine tech optimists. At the turn of the century, the technology industry seemed to have broken the mold of stuffy corporatism: it professed grand missions (to organize the world's knowledge, to let people connect across any distance), adopted bold, jargon-free principles like "don't be evil," and presented itself as an unusually humane place to work, one that valued its people and meant to leave the world better than it found it. We had certainly seen hype cycles come and go, and the occasional spectacular fraud, but until quite recently, the industry kept a certain halo. It still looked like it harbored real social aspirations, and an extraordinary reserve of public goodwill went with that.

But the halo has tarnished. We have watched fraud-driven boom-and-bust cycles (Enron, Theranos, FTX) slip past the auditors meant to catch them. We have learned to recognize and name the manipulation tactics we now see everywhere: “enshittification,” “rage-bait,” “manufactured doubt,” “regulatory capture.” We have experienced surveillance capitalism turning our attention and personal data into product, so gradually that most of us didn't notice until it was already the default business model. We have seen mass layoffs, abandoned principles, and the open, unapologetic embrace of the very tactics we had just learned to name. And none of this has been helped by a leadership class that can seem to cultivate the charisma of Bond villains. We have begun, too, to grasp the physical cost of all this: the mined lithium, the cobalt, the rare-earth metals, the water and the power-hungry data centers behind it all. And just when, in the wealthy democracies, we might traditionally look to governments for help, some are instead stripping power from watchdogs and regulators, openly declaring an interest in controlling the very institutions that produce and safeguard knowledge.

So we don't come to AI with open arms. We come to it cynical, ready to hear each sweeping promise as one more "revolution" destined to slide into the "trough of disillusionment" and stall there. We read its marketing with the jaundiced eye of people who feel they've been here before. And we're more aware that AI's costs aren't abstract: its appetite for energy is vast and increasingly visible, raising pointed questions about what all that electricity is actually buying us. Even the industry has begun to notice. Microsoft's CEO has spoken of needing to earn AI's "social permission" to consume so much energy. It's a striking phrase, and one that could apply to many of AI's other side effects, actual and predicted. It's also a tacit admission that such permission can no longer be assumed.

## Why is it so hard to talk clearly about AI?

There's a further difficulty in the language we use to talk about AI. Start with the name: "artificial intelligence" simply asserts what is most in dispute, that what these systems do amounts to intelligence at all. It gets worse when "AI" collapses into "LLMs," as it usually does today (a conflation encouraged partly by marketing). One particular statistical technique has come to stand for the whole field, borrowing the human aura of the word "intelligence." From there the anthropomorphism extends to our verbs. We say a model "knows," "understands," or "reasons," as though it had a mind. We even say it "hallucinates," recasting a systematic error as a slightly charming and eccentric tic. But what we're really describing is a vast statistical model reflecting back the intentions of the people whose writing it was trained on. And the chat interface deepens the spell, exploiting our very old human habit of seeing mind and meaning where there is none, a tendency on display as far back as people's reactions to ELIZA in 1964.

This isn't nit-picking, because how we frame the technology changes what we let it do. Consider the "AI detectors" now used to police student and scholarly writing: framed as tools that spot AI, they actually flag statistical patterns, patterns that happen to correlate with how certain people write, with the result that non-native and non-standard English speakers are disproportionately, and falsely, accused. The same slipperiness surrounds "AI predictive policing," which the public can hear either as an intelligent system worth trusting or as what some have bluntly called "racist linear algebra": a database of historical patterns faithfully reproducing their biases. The words invite one reading or the other. So part of the work of this workshop is simply to talk about AI more precisely than our inherited vocabulary encourages. We cannot sustain human knowledge with language that keeps confusing a statistical model for a knowing mind. But there is a constructive task here too: developing a clearer, shared vocabulary is itself one of the concrete, achievable things this Summit can create, and a foundation on which better tools and norms can be built.

And the difficulty is not only in the words we inherit; some of it is actively manufactured. The companies building these systems command marketing budgets without precedent for such a young technology. Leaked financial documents in 2026, verified by the Financial Times, showed OpenAI spending roughly 44% of its revenue (~ $5.7 billion) on sales and marketing. That money shapes much of the language in which the rest of us end up discussing AI. But one thing sets this hype cycle apart from blockchain or the metaverse: the technology can be turned into an engine for promoting itself. A company selling access to a system that produces fluent text can use that same system to flood the discourse with promotional copy, talking points, and ready-made rebuttals. Earlier hype cycles had to buy their advocates. This one can generate them on demand, and the rest of us are left to work out where assessment ends and marketing begins.

## What will the Summit work through?

Before we can decide what to do, we need a shared picture of what is actually happening, which means resisting two easy temptations: the complacency that insists nothing has really changed, and the techno-doom that declares everything already lost. Some of what feels like an AI crisis is genuinely new. Some is a pre-existing structural failure now accelerating. And some is plain moral panic. Sorting one from another is the groundwork for everything else, including the harder question of what these tools may be doing to us, and not just for us. Getting this diagnosis right is what makes real design possible. Once we can see clearly which problems are which, we can begin to match each with the mechanism most likely to solve it.

Then there is the question of authority. For centuries, the sheer effort of producing scholarly-looking work served as a rough proxy for its worth: writing was hard, so fluent, well-referenced output was at least a half-decent signal that someone had done the work. That assumption is now broken. Generating plausible, scholarly-shaped text has become trivially cheap, while evaluating it for genuine meaning and importance remains as slow and costly as ever. The age-old asymmetry has flipped: writing is now cheaper than reading. So how does the evaluation of scholarly work adapt to a world where producing it, or something that convincingly resembles it, can be scaled almost infinitely?

Maybe history has something to teach us here. Many past "epistemic crises" can be read as moments when an expensive signal of trust suddenly became cheap. Socrates worried that writing would let people fake the understanding that once required memorization; the printing press, and later desktop publishing and the web, each made it cheap to produce what had previously demanded great expense, skill, or privilege. Each time, the response followed a similar arc: a wave of democratization, a wave of moral panic, and then the slow invention of new, harder-to-fake signals (imprints, peer review, citations, the whole scholarly apparatus of attribution). Every previous crisis of this kind was eventually met by human ingenuity that produced durable new institutions. We have done this before, and we can do it again, deliberately and together, rather than leaving it to chance.

AI has now cheapened another such signal: fluency itself. Mastery of grammar, register, and academic prose once took years to acquire, and it functioned as a gate that kept many capable people out, as any non-native speaker can attest. Its debasement is painful to those who spent decades perfecting it (this author included). But fluency was always a signal of privilege as much as merit, a marker of who had access to the right schooling, not of whose ideas were sound. Its passing may be a chance to build better signals in its place, ones that track the strength of the ideas, the quality of the data, and the rigor of the analysis rather than the polish of the prose. The question is what those signals should be, and how we might help build them.

We also need to talk about infrastructure, and not just the technical kind. Knowledge doesn't sustain itself. It rests on datasets, archives, corpora, and the long-term scholarly record, a commons increasingly subject to enclosure. But it rests just as heavily on people: the editors, reviewers, curators, librarians, and researchers whose labor creates and maintains that record, and whose work is too often unpaid, underpaid, or simply invisible. The extractive pressures that AI training brings, the concentration of ownership, and the sovereignty questions shaping the wider tech landscape now bear directly on both the materials scholarship is built from and the human effort that keeps them trustworthy. The same surveillance logic that once turned our attention and personal data into product now eyes the scholarly record as raw material, treating decades of accumulated human judgment as a free input rather than something costly to produce and maintain. So the question isn't only how we safeguard these resources and keep them open, but who pays to sustain the labor behind them, and how that labor gets recognized and rewarded. This is a chance to design new models of stewardship and fair compensation, models that finally make visible, and properly value, the human work that underpins trustworthy knowledge.

AI increasingly produces, synthesizes, and mediates what we come to know. For most of us, this was never really a choice. It was foisted on us through forced upgrades, default settings, and mandates from managers answering to shareholders or funders who have themselves been swept up in the hype. This raises difficult questions about veracity, trust, opacity, and the concentration of power in whoever owns the infrastructure. But beneath all of them sits a prior question: how do we separate what these systems can genuinely do from the perennial promise that they are always just five years away from doing more?

It also raises a subtler, more technical worry. Large language models are non-deterministic, and that unpredictability compounds when their outputs are chained together. So far, they've proved most useful where fast, deterministic checks can catch their errors, which is why they've taken hold in software development. Much of science is the opposite: feedback loops measured in years or decades, results that are rarely a simple pass or fail. A flawed result can propagate for years before anyone notices. So as more of the work around it is automated, human judgment matters more, not less: it becomes the scarce, decisive ingredient, and the cost of removing it climbs accordingly. If human judgment is becoming the most valuable ingredient in the system, then designing tools and workflows that amplify and reward it, rather than replace it, is exactly the kind of constructive work this Summit can advance.

At the same time, if human judgment is the ingredient worth protecting, two risks threaten it from opposite directions, and they may feed each other.

The first is cognitive atrophy. As we lean on these systems to summarize, draft, and decide, we let our own capacities waste away: close reading, sustained reasoning, the ability to write a coherent argument from scratch. The second is model collapse. As AI floods the internet with its output, future models train on what earlier AI produced rather than on what humans wrote, and there's no easy way to separate the two. Each generation becomes a photocopy of a photocopy- quirks and errors amplify with every pass, outputs drift further from the human source, and the results grow less faithful and less reliable.

Put them together and you get a vicious circle: humans thinking less carefully, relying on machines that are themselves degrading, each side accelerating the other's decline. This is why keeping humans meaningfully in the loop is vital. The alternative is likely a slow mutual deterioration. The vicious cycle here can be broken by design. If we can build workflows and incentives that keep verified human thought at the center, the same feedback loop can be turned virtuous, with better human judgment producing better data and better tools in turn.

There is another hopeful corollary here. One of the few genuinely new livelihoods to emerge from the AI boom is that of the "annotator": the human paid to label, correct, and curate the data these systems learn from. A market for careful human judgment has sprung up at the very heart of the AI economy, a tacit admission of how much these systems still depend on us. It also raises a question for the people this Summit convenes: if well-curated, trustworthy, human-vetted knowledge is the scarce ingredient, then are researchers, scholarly publishers, and the wider open-knowledge community in fact the ultimate annotators? And if they are, what follows? Part of our work is to figure out how to claim it: to design the mechanisms that let scholars and publishers be recognized, and rewarded, as the trusted source of the human judgment and curation that our knowledge ecosystems depend on.

## Where this leads

We're not convening to produce another cautious consensus report that gets skimmed and shelved. Over our time together, we want to produce three concrete things: a short, plain-spoken manifesto for sustaining human knowledge; a practical roadmap of six to ten interventions we can actually pursue; and a list of the open questions we can't yet answer. The emphasis throughout is on what we can build. This is a working session aimed at concrete, actionable solutions, not another round of handwringing.

Throughout, we want to hold on to the distinction we started with: what was already broken, what was breaking, and what AI now threatens to break faster. The right response differs in each case, and some of the hardest problems predate AI by decades. Take the perverse incentives inside our own institutions that reward the very behaviors now corroding trust. We have no illusion that a workshop can undo a problem half a century in the making. But AI changes the calculus: dysfunctions we once managed to tolerate become far more dangerous when the production of plausible, unvetted work is suddenly free and infinite. Problems we once could afford to defer can no longer wait. AI may be what finally forces a reckoning, and with it, the momentum and the mandate to build solutions that have eluded us for decades.

AI didn't start the strain on human knowledge. But it has raised the stakes of getting our response right. It has also, for the first time in a generation, created both the urgency and the opportunity to redesign how human knowledge is sustained, curated, and trusted, and to build something better than what came before. This is an invitation to help get it right.
